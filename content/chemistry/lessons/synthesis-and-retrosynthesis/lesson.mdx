<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Planning backwards">

A medicinal chemist sketches a molecule that might block an enzyme. Before anyone can test it, someone has to make it, from compounds that can be bought, using reactions that actually work, in as few steps as possible, without destroying the parts of the molecule already built. Organic **synthesis** is that craft, and since the 1960s it has had an explicit logic.

The logic is to plan **backwards**. Starting from the target, a chemist asks: which bond could I have made last, and from what? Then the same question of each precursor, and so on, until every branch ends in something purchasable. E. J. Corey at Harvard set out this **retrosynthetic analysis** as a formal method in the 1960s, used it to make dozens of natural products, including prostaglandins and ginkgolide B, and received the 1990 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

This lesson teaches the method with the reactions of <LessonLink slug="reaction-mechanisms">Reaction mechanisms</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="carbonyls-and-aromatics">Carbonyl and aromatic chemistry</LessonLink>, then looks at what makes a route good: selectivity, protecting groups, step count, yield and waste. It is about strategy, as chemists discuss it on a whiteboard. How each step is carried out safely at the bench or in a plant is a separate, professional skill, learnt in a supervised laboratory.

<Analogy>

Planning a route through a maze is easier from the exit, because the exit is one place and the entrances are many. Retrosynthesis works the same way: the target is fixed, while the possible starting materials number in the millions. The analogy has one limit: in a maze every corridor works in both directions, but a disconnection is only useful if the corresponding *forward* reaction really works.

</Analogy>

</Section>

<Section id="disconnections" kind="definitions" title="Disconnections, synthons and equivalents">

<Definition term="retrosynthetic vocabulary">

- A **disconnection** is the imaginary breaking of a bond in the target, the reverse of a bond-forming reaction. It is written with a double-shafted **retrosynthetic arrow**, target ⇒ precursors, and the broken bond is marked with a wavy line.
- A **synthon** is an idealised fragment produced by a disconnection, usually an ion such as CH₃CO⁺ or a carbanion. Synthons are bookkeeping devices and need not exist.
- A **synthetic equivalent** is a real reagent that behaves like a synthon: ethanoic anhydride or ethanoyl chloride for the acylium synthon CH₃CO⁺, a Grignard reagent for a carbanion.
- A **functional-group interconversion** (FGI) changes one functional group into another without changing the skeleton (an alcohol into a ketone, a nitro group into an amine), and is written with the same arrow.

</Definition>

Good disconnections are not arbitrary. The safest place to cut is next to a heteroatom, especially the C–N bond of an amide and the C–O bond of an ester or ether, because the forward reactions (acyl substitution, alkylation) are reliable. Carbon–carbon bonds are best cut next to a functional group that can supply the necessary polarity: a bond between the α- and β-carbons of a β-hydroxy ketone points to an aldol reaction, a bond next to a tertiary alcohol points to a Grignard addition to a ketone, and a bond between two aromatic rings points to a cross-coupling.

<Example title="A tertiary alcohol: two disconnections, one choice">

Take 2-phenylbutan-2-ol, C₆H₅C(OH)(CH₃)CH₂CH₃. The carbon carrying the OH has three carbon groups, and any of the three bonds to it can be disconnected to a ketone plus a carbanion synthon, whose equivalent is a Grignard reagent. Cutting the ethyl bond gives 1-phenylethan-1-one plus an ethyl carbanion; cutting the methyl bond gives 1-phenylpropan-1-one plus a methyl carbanion; cutting the phenyl bond gives butan-2-one plus a phenyl carbanion. All three are sound. A chemist picks by asking which starting materials are cheap, which Grignard reagent is easiest to handle, and whether the rest of the target has groups that the Grignard reagent would attack.

</Example>

<Example title="A β-hydroxy ketone: recognising a pattern">

4-Hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one, (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂COCH₃, has an OH on the carbon β to a ketone: the signature of an aldol product. Disconnecting the bond between the α-carbon and the β-carbon gives an enolate synthon of propanone and a second propanone as the electrophile. So the target is a "dimer" of propanone, and the forward reaction is an aldol addition of propanone to itself. Recognising such patterns, called **retrons** in Corey's language, is most of the skill.

</Example>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why are the good disconnections next to heteroatoms and functional groups? Because a polar bond can only be made between a nucleophile and an electrophile, and functional groups are what create that polarity: a carbonyl carbon is electrophilic, the carbon next to it becomes nucleophilic as an enolate, and a nitrogen or oxygen lone pair is a nucleophile. A disconnection that leaves both fragments with the "wrong" polarity needs a reagent that reverses a group's natural polarity, which chemists call *umpolung*, and such reagents are rarer and harder to use.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="selectivity" kind="intuition" title="Selectivity and protecting groups">

A real target has several functional groups, and every reagent must touch only the one intended. Chemists distinguish three kinds of **selectivity**:

- **Chemoselectivity**: which functional group reacts. Sodium borohydride reduces a ketone but leaves an ester alone; lithium aluminium hydride reduces both.
- **Regioselectivity**: which position of a group reacts. The directing effects of <LessonLink slug="carbonyls-and-aromatics">Carbonyl and aromatic chemistry</LessonLink>, the choice between kinetic and thermodynamic enolates, and Zaitsev or Hofmann elimination are all regioselectivity.
- **Stereoselectivity**: which stereoisomer forms. Most medicines are single enantiomers (<LessonLink slug="stereochemistry">Stereochemistry</LessonLink>), so modern synthesis relies on asymmetric catalysis, recognised by the 2001 Nobel Prize to William Knowles, Ryoji Noyori and Barry Sharpless and the 2021 prize below, and on enzymes.

When no reagent is selective enough, the chemist **protects** the group that must not react: converts it temporarily into an unreactive form, does the reaction elsewhere, then removes the protection.

<Example title="Protecting a ketone">

Suppose a molecule contains both a ketone and an ester, and the plan is to add a Grignard reagent to the ester only. The Grignard reagent would attack the ketone first, since ketones are more electrophilic than esters. The answer is to convert the ketone into a cyclic **acetal** with ethane-1,2-diol, under acid catalysis: an acetal is an ether on both sides and ignores strongly basic nucleophiles. After the Grignard step, aqueous acid hydrolyses the acetal back to the ketone. Peptide synthesis relies on the same idea on a grand scale: each amino acid's amine is protected while its acid end is coupled, then unprotected for the next coupling, the basis of Bruce Merrifield's solid-phase method (Nobel Prize 1984), which machines now run automatically.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

A protecting group is never free. It costs two steps, one on and one off, each with its own yield, waste and chance of disturbing something else, and it adds mass that is thrown away at the end. So "protect it" is a correct answer and also a confession that no selective reaction was found. Chemists such as Phil Baran have shown how far a route can go [without any protecting groups at all](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05569), by ordering the steps so that each reagent meets only groups it ignores.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="counting" kind="computation" title="Counting steps, yield and waste">

Every step loses material. If step $i$ has yield $y_i$, a **linear** synthesis, in which each step feeds the next, delivers an overall yield $\prod_i y_i$. A **convergent** synthesis makes two or more fragments separately and joins them late, so the number of steps the material passes through, the **longest linear sequence**, is much shorter than the total number of steps.

```python run
import numpy as np

print("overall yield (%) of a linear synthesis")
print("steps    99%/step   95%/step   90%/step   80%/step")
for n in (5, 10, 20, 30, 40):
    print(f"{n:5d}  " + "".join(f"{100 * y**n:10.1f} " for y in (0.99, 0.95, 0.90, 0.80)))

# Twelve steps at 90% each, arranged two ways.
y = 0.90
linear = y**12                     # A -> ... -> target in one chain of 12 steps
convergent = y**(5 + 2)            # two 5-step branches, then coupling + one last step: 7 in the longest chain
print(f"\n12 steps, linear:      longest chain 12 steps, overall yield {100 * linear:.1f}%,"
      f" starting material per mol of product {1 / linear:.2f} mol")
print(f"12 steps, convergent:  longest chain  7 steps, overall yield {100 * convergent:.1f}%,"
      f" each starting material per mol of product {1 / convergent:.2f} mol")

# How good must each step be for a 25-step synthesis to deliver 10%?
need = 0.10 ** (1 / 25)
print(f"\na 25-step linear route needs an average of {100 * need:.1f}% per step to give 10% overall")
```

The table is the most important arithmetic in synthesis. Twenty steps at a very respectable 90% leave 12% of the material; at 80% per step, a little over 1%. Arranging the same twelve steps convergently raises the overall yield from 28% to 48%, and each starting material is needed in 2.1 rather than 3.5 times the final amount. A 25-step synthesis that is to deliver 10% needs an *average* of 91% per step.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Run the numbers backwards for a medicine. A route of 12 linear steps at 85% each gives 14% overall, so every mole of drug needs about seven moles of the first intermediate, plus the reagents and solvents for twelve reactions and twelve purifications. That is why process chemists will spend years removing a single step, and why "fewer steps" and "higher yield per step" appear in every comparison of routes.

</Narrator>

Yield measures how much of the *intended* product survives; it says nothing about everything else that went into the flask. Barry Trost's **atom economy** (1991) asks what fraction of the mass of all the reactants ends up in the product, assuming every step goes perfectly. The classic comparison is ibuprofen. The original route of the Boots Company, from the 1960s, took six steps; the BHC route, introduced in 1992, takes three catalytic ones. Both are read here from balanced equations.

```python run
import re

MASSES = {"H": 1.008, "C": 12.011, "N": 14.007, "O": 15.999, "Na": 22.990, "Cl": 35.45}

def molar_mass(formula):
    """Molar mass in g/mol of a formula such as 'C13H18O2' (no brackets)."""
    return sum(MASSES[el] * (int(n) if n else 1) for el, n in re.findall(r"([A-Z][a-z]?)(\d*)", formula))

def atom_economy(reactants, product):
    return molar_mass(product) / sum(molar_mass(f) for f in reactants)

ibuprofen = "C13H18O2"
# Every reagent whose atoms appear in the balanced equations of each route (the usual bookkeeping).
routes = {
    "Boots route, 6 steps": ["C10H14", "C4H6O3", "C4H7ClO2", "C2H5ONa", "H3O", "NH3O", "H4O2"],
    "BHC route, 3 steps":   ["C10H14", "C4H6O3", "H2", "CO"],
}
print(f"ibuprofen {ibuprofen}: {molar_mass(ibuprofen):.2f} g/mol\n")
for name, reactants in routes.items():
    total = sum(molar_mass(f) for f in reactants)
    ae = atom_economy(reactants, ibuprofen)
    print(f"{name}: reactants {total:6.1f} g/mol, atom economy {100 * ae:5.1f}%, "
          f"by-products {total - molar_mass(ibuprofen):5.1f} g per mol of ibuprofen")
# In the BHC route the only by-product is ethanoic acid, C2H4O2, which can be recovered and used.
used = (molar_mass(ibuprofen) + molar_mass("C2H4O2")) / sum(molar_mass(f) for f in routes["BHC route, 3 steps"])
print(f"BHC route counting the recovered ethanoic acid as product: {100 * used:.1f}%")
```

Even with perfect yields, the six-step route turns only 40% of the mass of its reagents into ibuprofen, and makes about 309 g of by-products per mole (206 g) of drug. The three-step route reaches 77%, and its one by-product, ethanoic acid, is recovered, so nearly every atom bought ends up in something useful. Fewer steps, catalysts instead of stoichiometric reagents, and reactions that add whole molecules (hydrogen, carbon monoxide) rather than swap groups are what make the difference. The large-scale steps use hazardous gases such as carbon monoxide, and run only in closed, monitored industrial plant; the strategy is what transfers to other problems.

</Section>

<Section id="search" kind="computation" title="Retrosynthesis as search">

Retrosynthesis is a search problem. Each molecule is a node with several possible disconnections (an "or": any one will do), and each disconnection leads to precursors that must *all* be made (an "and"). The whole space is an **AND/OR tree**, and a synthetic plan is a subtree whose leaves are all purchasable. The two ibuprofen routes can be *found* by searching such a tree. Below, a hand-written "database" holds eight known reactions, each read backwards as a rule. A recursive function expands the target, tries every rule for every intermediate, and combines the routes to all the precursors a rule needs.

```python run
# A tiny reaction "database" for ibuprofen, 2-[4-(2-methylpropyl)phenyl]propanoic acid.
# Each entry reads a known forward reaction backwards: product <= (reaction, precursors).
# Short labels: ketone = 1-[4-(2-methylpropyl)phenyl]ethan-1-one, alcohol = the corresponding
# ethan-1-ol, and aldehyde / oxime / nitrile / epoxy ester are the intermediates of the Boots route.
RULES = {
    "ibuprofen":   [("Pd-catalysed carbonylation", ["alcohol", "carbon monoxide"]),
                    ("nitrile hydrolysis", ["nitrile", "water"])],
    "alcohol":     [("hydrogenation", ["ketone", "hydrogen"])],
    "nitrile":     [("oxime dehydration", ["oxime"])],
    "oxime":       [("oxime formation", ["aldehyde", "hydroxylamine"])],
    "aldehyde":    [("hydrolysis and decarboxylation", ["epoxy ester", "water"])],
    "epoxy ester": [("Darzens condensation", ["ketone", "ethyl chloroacetate"])],
    "ketone":      [("Friedel-Crafts acylation", ["(2-methylpropyl)benzene", "ethanoic anhydride"])],
}
BUYABLE = {"(2-methylpropyl)benzene", "ethanoic anhydride", "ethyl chloroacetate",
           "hydroxylamine", "hydrogen", "carbon monoxide", "water"}

def routes(molecule):
    """Every way to make `molecule` from buyable compounds: a list of routes, each a list of steps
    in forward order. A molecule with several rules is an OR node; a rule with several precursors
    is an AND node, because every precursor must be made."""
    if molecule in BUYABLE:
        return [[]]
    found = []
    for reaction, precursors in RULES.get(molecule, []):
        partial = [[]]
        for p in precursors:
            partial = [a + b for a in partial for b in routes(p)]
        step = f"{' + '.join(precursors)} -> {molecule}   [{reaction}]"
        found += [steps + [step] for steps in partial]
    return found

all_routes = sorted(routes("ibuprofen"), key=len)
for k, route in enumerate(all_routes, 1):
    print(f"route {k}: {len(route)} steps")
    for i, step in enumerate(route, 1):
        print(f"   {i}. {step}")
n_rules = sum(len(r) for r in RULES.values())
print(f"\n{len(all_routes)} routes from {n_rules} rules; shortest: {len(all_routes[0])} steps")
```

The search rediscovers both routes and ranks the BHC route, three steps, first. Both begin with the same Friedel–Crafts acylation; they differ in how they add the last carbon. The older route builds it through a chain of carbonyl chemistry (an epoxy ester, an aldehyde, an oxime and a nitrile), while the newer one inserts carbon monoxide directly with a palladium catalyst.

Real planning programs work the same way at a vastly larger scale. Corey and Todd Wipke's LHASA program (1969) encoded hand-written rules; today's planners extract hundreds of thousands of rules automatically from published and patented reactions, use neural networks to rank which rules to try at each node, and search with methods such as Monte Carlo tree search ([Segler, Preuss and Waller, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25978)). Open-source examples include [AiZynthFinder](https://github.com/MolecularAI/aizynthfinder) and [ASKCOS](https://askcos.mit.edu/). How well they work, and where they fail, is a subject of <LessonLink slug="ai-for-chemistry">AI for chemistry</LessonLink>; joining a planner to robotic hardware is a subject of <LessonLink slug="self-driving-labs">Self-driving laboratories</LessonLink>.

<Section id="landmarks" kind="history" title="Landmark syntheses, as strategy">

Synthesis became an intellectual discipline through a series of targets that seemed impossible until someone found the strategy.

- **Tropinone, 1917.** Robert Robinson in Liverpool made this alkaloid skeleton in a single pot from three simple components, by imagining how a plant might assemble it: an early triumph of *biomimetic* thinking, in which the strategy follows a guess about biosynthesis.
- **Robert Burns Woodward, 1940s–1970s.** Woodward, at Harvard, showed that molecules of great complexity could be planned with the logic of mechanism and conformation: the antihypertensive reserpine (1956), with five adjacent stereocentres on one ring set by exploiting the shape of rigid intermediates; chlorophyll (1960); and, with Albert Eschenmoser at ETH Zurich, vitamin B12 (1972), a project of about a hundred chemists over more than a decade. Puzzles met during the B12 work led to the Woodward–Hoffmann rules of <LessonLink slug="molecular-orbitals">Molecular orbitals</LessonLink>. He received the 1965 Nobel Prize "for his outstanding achievements in the art of organic synthesis".
- **Corey, 1960s–1990s.** Corey made retrosynthesis explicit and teachable, introduced many reagents built for selectivity, and used them in syntheses such as the prostaglandins, hormones that became medicines.
- **Paclitaxel (Taxol), 1994.** Two groups, led by Robert Holton and K. C. Nicolaou, completed this anticancer natural product almost simultaneously, by different strategies. The medicine itself is now made from plant cell cultures and by semi-synthesis from a related natural product, a reminder that the best *supply* route need not be a total synthesis.
- **Eribulin, 2010.** This anticancer medicine, developed by the Japanese company Eisai from Yoshito Kishi's synthesis of the marine natural product halichondrin B, is manufactured by total synthesis in more than sixty steps, made practical by a highly convergent plan.
- **Artemisinin, 2013.** A semi-synthetic route to the antimalarial joins synthetic biology and chemistry: engineered yeast makes a precursor, artemisinic acid, and a short chemical sequence finishes the job ([Paddon and colleagues, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12051)).

</Section>

<Section id="nobel-methods" kind="human" title="Methods that changed the plan">

A new reaction changes synthesis when it creates a new kind of disconnection. Three recent Nobel Prizes recognised exactly that.

**Palladium-catalysed cross-coupling (2010: Richard Heck, Ei-ichi Negishi, Akira Suzuki).** Joining two aromatic rings, or an aromatic ring to an alkene, used to be awkward. Cross-coupling makes the C–C bond between two pieces that carry a halogen and a metal or boron, with a palladium catalyst that runs round a cycle many times.

Akira Suzuki and Norio Miyaura's version, with boronic acids that are stable, mostly non-toxic and tolerant of water, became so popular that it is now the second most used reaction in medicinal chemistry, after amide formation ([Brown and Boström, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01409)). It created the **biaryl disconnection**: any bond between two rings can be cut into an aryl halide and an arylboronic acid. The biphenyl core of the blood-pressure medicine losartan, for example, is made this way.

**Alkene metathesis (2005: Yves Chauvin, Robert Grubbs, Richard Schrock).** Metathesis swaps the partners of two C=C bonds: RCH=CHR + R′CH=CHR′ ⇌ 2 RCH=CHR′. Chauvin proposed the mechanism, through a four-membered ring containing a metal, in 1971; Schrock's molybdenum and tungsten catalysts and Grubbs's ruthenium catalysts made it practical. Its most important strategic gift is **ring-closing metathesis**, which joins two alkene ends of one chain into a ring and releases ethene, the easiest way to make medium and large rings. It is used to manufacture macrocyclic antiviral medicines and, in its ring-opening form, tough polymers.

**Asymmetric organocatalysis (2021: Benjamin List, David MacMillan).** In 2000 List showed that the amino acid proline catalyses an asymmetric aldol reaction, and MacMillan that a small chiral amine catalyses an asymmetric Diels–Alder reaction. Both work through the carbonyl chemistry of the previous lesson: the catalyst's amine condenses with a carbonyl compound to form an enamine (a nitrogen version of an enol) or an iminium ion (a more electrophilic version of the carbonyl), inside a chiral environment that favours one enantiomer. Organocatalysts are cheap, stable in air and free of metals, which matters for medicines.

<Section id="professional" kind="reading" title="The professional view">

Two kinds of chemist plan syntheses, with different goals. In **drug discovery**, the aim is to make hundreds of analogues quickly, so routes favour reliable reactions (amide formation, cross-coupling) and late-stage diversification. In **process chemistry**, the aim is to make tonnes of one compound safely, cheaply, reproducibly and with little waste, so a route is judged by its step count, overall yield, the **process mass intensity** (kilograms of all materials used per kilogram of product, often well over a hundred for a medicine), the hazards of its reagents and intermediates, and how robust each step is to small changes. The two often lead to completely different routes for the same molecule.

The process chemist's toolbox now includes enzymes. For the diabetes medicine sitagliptin, Merck and Codexis replaced a rhodium-catalysed asymmetric hydrogenation with an engineered transaminase enzyme, evolved in the laboratory to accept the drug's ketone precursor, which increased the yield and removed the metal ([Savile and colleagues, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1188934)); directed evolution of enzymes was recognised by the 2018 Nobel Prize to Frances Arnold. Continuous **flow chemistry** lets hazardous steps run in small volumes, and retrosynthesis software, as of 2026, routinely proposes routes for chemists to judge. The judgement remains human: whether a step will be selective on this particular molecule, scale safely, or meet regulatory standards for impurities. These themes continue in <LessonLink slug="catalysis-and-green-chemistry">Catalysis and green chemistry</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="medicinal-chemistry">Medicinal chemistry</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Retrosynthesis is just the synthesis written backwards."** A disconnection is only as good as the forward reaction it stands for. Synthons such as CH₃CO⁺ are fictions that must be replaced by real reagents, and a disconnection that has no reliable forward reaction, or that would be ruined by another group in the molecule, is useless however neat it looks.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If every step gives 99%, length does not matter."** Fifty steps at 99% give 61%; at 95%, under 8%. And each step still costs time, solvent, energy and purification, whatever its yield.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The shortest route is always best."** A short route through a hazardous intermediate, an expensive catalyst or an unselective step can lose to a longer, safer and more robust one. Routes are compared on yield, cost, safety, waste and robustness together.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Total synthesis is finished: anything can be made."** Almost any structure can be made by a large enough effort, but making it *efficiently* is still hard, and targets continue to drive the invention of new reactions. Many medicines still come from fermentation, plants or semi-synthesis because no synthetic route is competitive.

</Misconception>
