<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What is everything made of?">

Put an ice cube in a glass of juice and watch it shrink. Switch on a kettle and a cloud appears above the spout. Someone opens the oven in the kitchen, and a few moments later you can smell the bread from the next room. Leave a wet towel in the sun, and by lunchtime it is dry: the water has gone somewhere.

These everyday things all raise the same questions. What is the ice, the juice, the water, the air, the bread *made of*? Why can the same water be hard ice, runny liquid and invisible vapour? How does the smell of bread travel through a house?

Chemistry is the science of what things are made of and how they change. This first lesson gives you its most important idea, one that explains all of the puzzles above: **everything around you is made of tiny particles that never stop moving.**

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Matter and its states">

<Definition term="matter">

**Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space. Water, air, rock, your body and the stars are matter. Light, sound and heat are not: they are forms of **energy**, which matter can carry, give out and take in.

</Definition>

Air may not look like much, but it is matter: a balloon full of air has more mass than the same balloon empty, and you feel air pushing on you on a windy day.

Matter on Earth is mostly found in three **states**:

<Definition term="solid, liquid and gas">

- A **solid** keeps its own shape and its own volume (the amount of space it fills). Think of a stone or a coin.
- A **liquid** keeps its volume but flows to take the shape of its container. Think of water in a glass.
- A **gas** has no shape and no fixed volume of its own: it spreads out to fill whatever container it is in. Think of the air in a room.

</Definition>

The same substance can be in different states. Water is a solid (ice) below 0 °C, a liquid between 0 °C and 100 °C, and a gas (water vapour) above 100 °C, at the pressure of the air around us at sea level. The changes between states have names:

| Change | From | To | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **melting** | solid | liquid | ice cream on a hot day |
| **freezing** | liquid | solid | water in an ice-cube tray |
| **boiling** and **evaporation** | liquid | gas | a kettle; a puddle drying |
| **condensation** | gas | liquid | drops on a cold window; a bathroom mirror |
| **sublimation** | solid | gas | snow vanishing on a cold, dry, sunny day |

Every pure substance has its own **melting point** and **boiling point**. A few, measured at normal air pressure and rounded:

| Substance | Melts at | Boils at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| water | 0 °C | 100 °C |
| ethanol (the alcohol in hand gel) | −114 °C | 78 °C |
| nitrogen (most of the air) | −210 °C | −196 °C |
| table salt (sodium chloride) | 801 °C | 1465 °C |
| iron | 1538 °C | 2861 °C |

That is why air is a gas at room temperature (its boiling point is far below), and why iron is a solid (its melting point is far above).

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Particles in motion">

Here is the big idea, called the **particle model** of matter:

1. All matter is made of extremely small particles, far too small to see even with an ordinary microscope. (In the next lessons you will meet them properly: atoms, and groups of atoms called molecules.)
2. The particles are **always moving**. The hotter something is, the faster its particles move.
3. Particles **attract** each other when they are close, a little like weak magnets.

The three states are just three ways these particles can arrange themselves.

Now the puzzles from the start of the lesson have answers.

**Why does a solid keep its shape?** Its particles are held tightly in place by their attractions. They still move, but only by jiggling (vibrating) on the spot, so the solid cannot flow.

**What happens when ice melts?** Heating makes the particles vibrate harder and harder. At the melting point they shake free of their fixed places, though they still cling together. Now they can slide past one another: the solid has become a liquid.

**What happens when water boils?** Keep heating, and the particles move faster still. At the boiling point, many of them have enough energy to break away from their neighbours completely and fly off as a gas. The bubbles you see rising in boiling water are pockets of water vapour forming inside the liquid.

**Then why does a puddle dry up at only 20 °C?** Not all the particles in a liquid move at the same speed: some are fast, some slow. A few of the fastest ones near the surface escape even at room temperature. That is **evaporation**, and it happens at any temperature, just more quickly when it is warm, windy or dry. Boiling is evaporation that happens all through the liquid at once.

**How does a smell travel?** When bread bakes, some of its particles escape into the air. They are knocked about by the air particles, millions of times a second, and slowly wander outwards. This spreading of particles from where there are many to where there are few is called **diffusion**. You can see it in a glass of water: drop in a little food colouring, don't stir, and watch the colour creep outwards. Try it in a glass of warm water and one of cold water side by side, with an adult's help: the colour spreads faster in the warm one, because its particles move faster.

<Analogy>

Think of a crowded school playground. When the bell has rung and everyone is lined up, the children are like particles in a solid: each has a place and can only fidget. At break time, in a crowded corner, they are like a liquid: close together, but moving around each other. When the whole school runs out onto an empty field, they are like a gas: spread far apart and dashing about in all directions.

</Analogy>

<Section id="mixtures" kind="definitions" title="Pure substances and mixtures">

Most of the matter around you is not one substance but several, jumbled together.

<Definition term="pure substance and mixture">

A **pure substance** contains only one kind of matter, with the same particles all through it: pure water, pure gold, pure table salt. A **mixture** contains two or more substances that are mingled but not joined together, so each keeps its own properties: sea water, air, soil, orange juice, granite.

</Definition>

The chemist's word "pure" is stricter than the one on food packets. Orange juice labelled "100% pure" may have nothing added, but to a chemist it is a mixture of water, sugars, acids, vitamins, colours and many other substances.

There is a neat test. A pure substance melts and boils at a single, sharp temperature. A mixture usually melts or boils gradually, over a range of temperatures, and at different temperatures from its ingredients. That is why salt is spread on icy roads: salty water freezes at a lower temperature than pure water, so the ice melts.

Pure substances come in two kinds, **elements** (such as gold, oxygen and carbon) and **compounds** (such as water and salt), and you will meet both in <LessonLink slug="atoms-and-elements">Atoms and elements</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="molecules-and-compounds">Molecules and compounds</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="separating" kind="examples" title="Pulling mixtures apart">

Because each substance in a mixture keeps its own properties, you can use a difference in properties to separate them. Chemists do this every day, and so do cooks, water engineers and gold miners.

<Example title="Filtering: sand and water">

Sand does not dissolve in water. Pour sandy water through a coffee filter in a funnel, and the water passes through the tiny holes in the paper while the sand grains, which are too big, stay behind. **Filtering** separates a solid that has not dissolved from a liquid. Your kitchen sieve, a tea strainer and the filter in a fish tank all work the same way.

</Example>

<Example title="Evaporating: salt from salt water">

Salt *dissolves* in water: its particles spread out among the water particles so finely that a filter cannot catch them. To get the salt back, let the water go instead. With an adult, stir a teaspoon of salt into half a cup of warm water, pour a little onto a saucer and leave it on a sunny windowsill for a few days. As the water evaporates, small cube-shaped salt crystals appear. This is **evaporating**, and in hot, sunny places, from the Rann of Kutch in India to the coast of Senegal and the salt pans of Portugal, sea salt has been made this way for thousands of years.

</Example>

<Example title="Chromatography: the colours hiding in ink">

The black ink in a felt-tip pen is often a mixture of several coloured dyes. **Chromatography** ("colour writing") can separate them. You can do it at home with an adult, using only paper, water and a washable felt-tip pen:

1. Cut a strip of coffee-filter paper or kitchen paper about 2 cm wide.
2. Draw a pencil line across it 2 cm from the bottom, and put a small, strong dot of ink on the line.
3. Put about 1 cm of water in a glass. Hang the strip from a pencil laid across the top so that its end dips into the water but the ink dot stays **above** the water.
4. Wait 10 to 20 minutes and watch. Then take the strip out and let it dry.

The water soaks up the paper and carries the dyes with it. Dyes that cling more to the paper move slowly; dyes that dissolve better in water travel further. So the dot spreads into a column of separate colours. Black and brown pens often hide surprising blues, yellows and pinks. Use washable pens only: permanent markers need other solvents, which are not for home experiments.

</Example>

Which method to choose depends on how the substances differ:

| Mixture | The difference to use | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sand and water | grain size: sand does not dissolve | filtering |
| salt and water | water evaporates, salt does not | evaporating |
| dyes in ink | how strongly each dye clings to paper | chromatography |
| iron filings and sand | iron is attracted to a magnet | a magnet |
| water and the salt in it, keeping the water | different boiling points | distillation (in a laboratory) |

**Distillation** boils a liquid and cools the vapour so that it condenses somewhere else, leaving dissolved solids behind. Large plants in countries such as Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates distil sea water to make drinking water; others, as in Israel and Australia, mostly push it through extremely fine membranes instead, a method called reverse osmosis. Because it uses hot glassware and boiling liquids, distillation belongs in a school laboratory, with a teacher.

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="A smell on a random walk">

Why does a smell spread quickly near its source but take so long to fill a big room? Each smell particle does not travel in a straight line. It flies a tiny distance, bumps into an air particle, shoots off in a new direction, bumps again, and so on. Scientists call this a **random walk**.

This program follows 2000 imaginary smell particles on a grid. At every step, each particle moves one square east, west, north or south, chosen at random. Every so often the program measures how far, on average, the particles are from where they started. (The "random" choices come from a fixed starting number, a *seed*, so you get the same answer every time you run it.) Press **Run**:

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=2026)   # fixed seed: the same "random" walks every run

walkers = 2000              # 2000 smell particles, all starting at the same spot
moves = np.array([[1, 0], [-1, 0], [0, 1], [0, -1]])   # east, west, north, south
position = np.zeros((walkers, 2))

print("steps taken   average distance from start")
step = 0
for checkpoint in [25, 100, 400, 1600]:
    while step < checkpoint:
        direction = rng.integers(0, 4, size=walkers)   # each particle picks a way to go
        position += moves[direction]
        step += 1
    distance = np.sqrt((position ** 2).sum(axis=1))    # straight-line distance of each
    print(f"{step:11d}   {distance.mean():8.1f} steps")

# Real air: a typical smell molecule spreads by diffusion alone with
# D = 0.1 square centimetres per second (a measured value, roughly).
D = 0.1
one_minute = np.sqrt(6 * D * 60)            # the square-root rule for 3D
five_metres = 500 ** 2 / (6 * D)            # seconds to wander 500 cm
print(f"\nStill air, 1 minute: about {one_minute:.0f} cm")
print(f"Still air, to wander 5 m: about {five_metres / 86400:.1f} days")
```

Look at the table. After 25 steps the particles are on average 4.4 steps from the start; after 100 steps, 8.7; after 400 steps, 17.6; after 1600 steps, 34.6. Each time the number of steps is multiplied by **4**, the distance only **doubles**. To get twice as far, a random walker needs four times as long; to get ten times as far, a hundred times as long. That is the **square-root rule** of random walks, and it is why diffusion is quick over tiny distances and slow over large ones.

So when you smell bread from the next room within a minute, the air itself must be carrying the smell along, in draughts and warm rising currents.

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How chemists use these ideas">

The particle model began as an idea argued about by philosophers, and became solid science only about a century ago. In 1827 the Scottish botanist Robert Brown saw tiny grains from pollen jiggling endlessly in water under his microscope. In 1905 Albert Einstein explained this **Brownian motion**: the grains are being knocked about by invisible, moving water particles, and he worked out how far they should wander. A few years later the French physicist Jean Perrin measured the jiggling carefully, found that Einstein's predictions were right, and used them to count the particles. After that, almost no scientist doubted that atoms and molecules are real.

Separating mixtures is also a huge part of modern chemistry. Chromatography was invented around 1900 by the Russian-Italian botanist Mikhail Tsvet, who used it to separate the coloured pigments in leaves. Today chemists use machines that push mixtures through long, thin tubes packed with special materials, then detect each substance as it comes out. They test medicines for purity, check food and drinking water for pollution, find banned substances in athletes' blood and help police laboratories identify unknown materials. You will meet these methods in <LessonLink slug="analytical-chemistry">Analytical chemistry</LessonLink>, and the forces that hold liquids and solids together in <LessonLink slug="gases-liquids-and-solids">Gases, liquids and solids</LessonLink>.

Materials scientists use diffusion too: carbon atoms wandering into hot iron are how some steels are hardened (see [diffusion](/en/materials/diffusion/) in Lumenary Materials).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The particles in a solid don't move."** They do: they vibrate on the spot, all the time. They just can't move past each other. Only at the lowest temperature possible, absolute zero (−273.15 °C), would the jiggling be at its smallest, and even then it does not quite stop.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Things expand when heated because their particles get bigger."** The particles themselves stay the same size. They move faster and push each other further apart, so the spaces between them grow.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The bubbles in boiling water are air."** They are water vapour: water that has turned into a gas inside the liquid. (The small bubbles you see on the bottom of a pan *before* it boils are mostly air that was dissolved in the water.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The white cloud above a kettle is steam."** Real steam, water vapour, is invisible. The white cloud is made of tiny droplets of liquid water that form when the vapour meets cooler air and condenses. Look closely and you will see a clear gap just above the spout before the cloud begins. Keep your hands away from it: steam and the air near it can scald.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"When sugar dissolves in tea, it melts."** Melting needs heat to turn a solid into a liquid on its own: sugar melts at about 186 °C. Dissolving is different: the sugar's particles spread out among the water's particles. Sugar dissolves in cold water too, just more slowly.

</Misconception>

<Reflection title="a world that can be understood">

Early scientists such as Robert Boyle, a founder of modern chemistry, believed that the world was made by a God of order, and so expected it to follow patterns that patient study could uncover. It is a remarkable thing that the behaviour of ice, kettles and smells can be explained by one simple picture of moving particles. Whatever you believe, that wonder is a good companion for learning chemistry.

</Reflection>
