<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What Lewis structures cannot explain">

The Lewis structures and VSEPR shapes of <LessonLink slug="chemical-bonding">Chemical bonding</LessonLink> are astonishingly useful for a theory built from dots on paper. But they fail on some very ordinary molecules.

- **Oxygen is magnetic.** The Lewis structure of O₂, $\mathrm{O{=}O}$ with two lone pairs on each atom, has every electron paired. Yet oxygen is *paramagnetic*: it is drawn into a magnetic field. A classic lecture demonstration, performed by trained staff because liquid oxygen is both extremely cold and a powerful oxidiser, pours it between the poles of a strong magnet, where it hangs as a pale blue bridge. Paramagnetism means unpaired electrons — two of them, it turns out.
- **Helium does not form He₂**, though every atom has a pair of electrons to share.
- **Benzene is far more stable** than any structure with three ordinary double bonds, and its six C–C bonds are all the same length, 139 pm.
- **Longer conjugated molecules are coloured**, and their colour moves towards the red as they grow, as you saw with the dyes of <LessonLink slug="quantum-mechanics-for-chemists">Quantum mechanics for chemists</LessonLink>.

All four have one explanation. Electrons in a molecule do not belong to atoms or to bonds between pairs of atoms; they occupy **molecular orbitals** that spread over the whole molecule. This lesson builds them from the atomic orbitals you know, by solving small matrix eigenvalue problems.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Waves that add and cancel">

Bring two hydrogen atoms together. Each electron's 1s wavefunction starts to overlap the other nucleus's. Waves that overlap combine, and there are two ways for two equal waves to combine: **in phase**, so that they add and pile up amplitude between the nuclei, or **out of phase**, so that they cancel and leave a node halfway between.

An electron in the in-phase combination spends extra time between the two nuclei, where it is attracted by both. Its energy is *lower* than in a separate atom: a **bonding orbital**. An electron in the out-of-phase combination is pushed out of the region between the nuclei, and its wavefunction must bend sharply through the node, which costs kinetic energy. Its energy is *higher*: an **antibonding orbital**.

<Analogy>

Hang two identical pendulums side by side and join them with a weak spring. Alone, each swings at one frequency. Coupled, the pair has two patterns of motion: both swinging together (the spring is never stretched, so the frequency stays low) and swinging in opposition (the spring is stretched every swing, so the frequency is higher). Coupling splits one frequency into two. Coupling atomic orbitals splits one energy into two in the same way, and the stronger the coupling — the more the orbitals overlap — the larger the split.

</Analogy>

One approximation makes the whole picture possible. Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so the electrons rearrange almost instantly as the nuclei move. We can therefore fix the nuclei at some separation $R$, solve for the electrons, and repeat for other values of $R$. This is the **Born–Oppenheimer approximation** (Max Born and J. Robert Oppenheimer, 1927), and the energy as a function of $R$ is the molecule's **potential energy curve**, the landscape on which its nuclei vibrate (<LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink>).

</Section>

<Section id="lcao" kind="definitions" title="LCAO and the variational principle">

<Definition term="LCAO molecular orbital">

A **molecular orbital** (MO) is a one-electron wavefunction spread over a molecule. In the **linear combination of atomic orbitals** (LCAO) approximation it is written as

$$
\psi = \sum_{\mu} c_\mu \, \phi_\mu ,
$$

a weighted sum of atomic orbitals $\phi_\mu$ centred on the atoms, with coefficients $c_\mu$ to be found.

</Definition>

How should the coefficients be chosen? By the most useful theorem in quantum chemistry.

<Theorem name="The variational principle">

For any well-behaved trial wavefunction $\psi$, the energy expectation value

$$
E[\psi] = \frac{\int \psi^* \hat H \psi \, \mathrm{d}V}{\int \psi^* \psi \, \mathrm{d}V}
$$

is greater than or equal to the true ground-state energy $E_0$, with equality only if $\psi$ is the true ground state.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Expand $\psi$ in the (orthonormal) eigenfunctions of $\hat H$: $\psi = \sum_n a_n \psi_n$ with $\hat H\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$ and $E_0 \le E_1 \le \cdots$. Then $\int\psi^*\hat H\psi\,\mathrm{d}V = \sum_n |a_n|^2 E_n \ge E_0 \sum_n |a_n|^2 = E_0 \int\psi^*\psi\,\mathrm{d}V$, with equality only if every $a_n$ with $E_n > E_0$ is zero.

</Proof>

So lower is always better: we choose the coefficients that make $E$ as small as possible. Setting $\partial E/\partial c_\mu = 0$ gives the **secular equations**, a generalised eigenvalue problem

$$
\mathbf H \mathbf c = E\, \mathbf S \mathbf c, \qquad H_{\mu\nu} = \int \phi_\mu \hat H \phi_\nu \,\mathrm{d}V, \qquad S_{\mu\nu} = \int \phi_\mu \phi_\nu \, \mathrm{d}V .
$$

For two identical atoms A and B with one orbital each, name the integrals: the **Coulomb integral** $\alpha = H_{AA}$ (roughly the energy of an electron in the atomic orbital, in the molecule), the **resonance integral** $\beta = H_{AB}$ (negative: the energy of coupling), and the **overlap** $S = S_{AB}$. Symmetry forces $c_A = \pm c_B$, and the two orbitals and energies are

$$
\psi_\pm = \frac{\phi_A \pm \phi_B}{\sqrt{2(1 \pm S)}}, \qquad E_\pm = \frac{\alpha \pm \beta}{1 \pm S} .
$$

Because $\beta < 0$, $E_+$ is the bonding level. And because of the $1 \pm S$ denominators, the antibonding orbital is raised *more* than the bonding one is lowered.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The variational principle is why the LCAO method can be trusted to improve: every new basis function adds a dimension to the space in which we minimise, and the minimum can only go down. It is the same idea as the <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">Rayleigh quotient</TopicLink> of a symmetric matrix, whose minimum over all vectors is the smallest eigenvalue.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="h2" kind="computation" title="H₂⁺ and H₂">

The hydrogen molecule-ion H₂⁺, two protons and one electron, is the simplest molecule. With one 1s orbital on each proton every integral has a closed form. In atomic units, with $R$ in bohr,

$$
S = e^{-R}\left(1 + R + \tfrac{R^2}{3}\right), \quad j = \frac1R - e^{-2R}\left(1 + \tfrac1R\right), \quad k = e^{-R}(1 + R),
$$

and the two LCAO energies, including the repulsion $1/R$ between the protons, are

$$
E_\pm(R) = -\tfrac12 + \frac1R - \frac{j \pm k}{1 \pm S}.
$$

Here $j$ is the attraction of one proton for an electron sitting in the other atom's orbital and $k$ is the corresponding attraction for the overlap density. The block below traces both curves, finds the bond length and bond energy, and then applies the variational principle once more by letting the 1s orbitals shrink, $e^{-\zeta r}$, with $\zeta$ chosen to minimise the energy.

```python run
import numpy as np

hartree_eV, bohr_pm = 27.211386, 52.917721

def integrals(R):
    """Overlap S, Coulomb j and exchange-like k for two hydrogen 1s orbitals a distance R (bohr) apart."""
    S = np.exp(-R) * (1 + R + R**2 / 3)
    j = 1 / R - np.exp(-2 * R) * (1 + 1 / R)
    k = np.exp(-R) * (1 + R)
    return S, j, k

def h2plus(R):
    """LCAO energies (hartree) of H2+, bonding and antibonding, including the 1/R repulsion of the protons."""
    S, j, k = integrals(R)
    return -0.5 + 1 / R - (j + k) / (1 + S), -0.5 + 1 / R - (j - k) / (1 - S)

print("Energies relative to H + H+ far apart; the bar shows the depth of the bonding curve.")
print("  R (pm)   bonding (eV)   antibonding (eV)")
for R_pm in [60, 80, 100, 132, 160, 200, 300, 400]:
    Eb, Ea = h2plus(R_pm / bohr_pm)
    bar = "#" * int(round(-(Eb + 0.5) * hartree_eV * 10)) if Eb < -0.5 else ""
    print(f"{R_pm:7d}   {(Eb + 0.5)*hartree_eV:10.2f}   {(Ea + 0.5)*hartree_eV:14.2f}   {bar}")

R = np.linspace(1.0, 6.0, 5001)
Eb, _ = h2plus(R)
i = np.argmin(Eb)
print(f"\nLCAO minimum: R_e = {R[i]*bohr_pm:.0f} pm, D_e = {(-0.5 - Eb[i])*hartree_eV:.2f} eV")

# Variational improvement: let the 1s orbitals shrink, exp(-zeta r). Kinetic energy scales as zeta^2,
# potential energy as zeta, so E(zeta, R) follows from the zeta = 1 integrals at distance zeta*R.
def h2plus_zeta(zeta, R):
    S, j, k = integrals(zeta * R)
    T = (0.5 - S / 2 + k) / (1 + S)                 # kinetic energy at zeta = 1
    V = -0.5 - (j + k) / (1 + S) - T                # electron-nucleus attraction at zeta = 1
    return zeta**2 * T + zeta * V + 1 / R
Z, RR = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(1.0, 1.5, 251), np.linspace(1.5, 3.0, 301), indexing="ij")
E = h2plus_zeta(Z, RR)
iz, ir = np.unravel_index(np.argmin(E), E.shape)
print(f"best zeta = {Z[iz, ir]:.2f}: R_e = {RR[iz, ir]*bohr_pm:.0f} pm, D_e = {(-0.5 - E[iz, ir])*hartree_eV:.2f} eV")
print("exact (from the full Schrodinger equation): R_e = 106 pm, D_e = 2.79 eV")
```

The bonding curve has a well: the simplest LCAO puts the minimum at 132 pm with a bond energy of 1.76 eV. The antibonding curve has no well at all; an electron in it pushes the nuclei apart. Letting the orbitals contract to $\zeta = 1.24$ (they are pulled in by two protons instead of one) moves the bond length to 106 pm, exactly right, and the bond energy to 2.35 eV, closer to the true 2.79 eV and, as the variational principle promises, still below it in strength (above it in energy).

<Narrator who="margaret">

Two numbers worth remembering from this table. Even a two-function calculation gets the existence, rough length and rough strength of a chemical bond from nothing but the Schrödinger equation. And one well-chosen extra parameter, $\zeta$, fixed the bond length and recovered more than half of the error in the bond energy — the pattern of all of computational chemistry, where better answers come from more flexible trial functions.

</Narrator>

### Bond order

Now fill the orbitals with electrons, at most two per orbital with opposite spins. Define

$$
\text{bond order} = \tfrac12\,(\text{electrons in bonding MOs} - \text{electrons in antibonding MOs}).
$$

H₂⁺ has one bonding electron: bond order ½. H₂ has two, paired in the bonding σ orbital: bond order 1, a bond 74 pm long that takes 4.48 eV (432 kJ/mol) to break. He₂ would have two bonding and two antibonding electrons: bond order 0, and since the antibonding orbital is raised more than the bonding one is lowered, two helium atoms repel. He₂⁺, with three electrons, has bond order ½ and does exist, in electrical discharges through helium.

"Bond order 0, so He₂ does not exist" is true for covalent bonding, not absolutely. The weak dispersion attraction between any two atoms does hold a helium dimer together, by less than a millionth of an electronvolt and with the atoms several nanometres apart on average; it was first detected in ultracold helium beams in the 1990s. <Section id="diatomics" kind="examples" title="Diatomic molecules of the second row">

From lithium to neon the valence orbitals are 2s and 2p. Each pair of matching atomic orbitals gives a bonding and an antibonding MO:

- the two 2s orbitals give $\sigma_{2s}$ and $\sigma^*_{2s}$;
- the two 2p orbitals pointing along the bond give $\sigma_{2p}$ and $\sigma^*_{2p}$;
- the two pairs of 2p orbitals perpendicular to the bond overlap side-on and give two degenerate $\pi_{2p}$ and two degenerate $\pi^*_{2p}$ orbitals.

Side-on overlap is weaker than head-on, so the π splitting is smaller than the σ splitting. One complication changes the order: in the lighter elements (up to nitrogen) the 2s and 2p energies are close enough that $\sigma_{2s}$ and $\sigma_{2p}$ mix, which pushes $\sigma_{2p}$ above $\pi_{2p}$. From oxygen on, 2s lies much lower, the mixing fades, and $\sigma_{2p}$ is below $\pi_{2p}$. Photoelectron spectra, which measure the energy needed to remove an electron from each orbital, confirm both orders.

The filling is mechanical enough to program. The block below fills the valence MOs of every second-row homonuclear diatomic, and of the oxygen ions, applying Hund's rule within each degenerate pair.

```python run
# Fill the valence molecular orbitals of second-row diatomics and read off bond order and unpaired electrons.
# Each level: (name, number of orbitals, +1 bonding / -1 antibonding), lowest first.
MIXED = [("σ2s", 1, +1), ("σ*2s", 1, -1), ("π2p", 2, +1), ("σ2p", 1, +1), ("π*2p", 2, -1), ("σ*2p", 1, -1)]
UNMIXED = [("σ2s", 1, +1), ("σ*2s", 1, -1), ("σ2p", 1, +1), ("π2p", 2, +1), ("π*2p", 2, -1), ("σ*2p", 1, -1)]

def fill(levels, electrons):
    bonding = antibonding = unpaired = 0
    config = []
    for name, n_orb, kind in levels:
        e = min(electrons, 2 * n_orb)
        electrons -= e
        if e == 0:
            continue
        config.append(f"({name})^{e}")
        if kind > 0:
            bonding += e
        else:
            antibonding += e
        # Hund's rule: in a set of n_orb degenerate orbitals, spread out before pairing
        unpaired += e if e <= n_orb else 2 * n_orb - e
    return (bonding - antibonding) / 2, unpaired, " ".join(config)

molecules = [("Li2", 2), ("Be2", 4), ("B2", 6), ("C2", 8), ("N2", 10),
             ("O2", 12), ("F2", 14), ("Ne2", 16), ("O2+", 11), ("O2-", 13), ("O2 2-", 14)]
print(f"{'species':7s} {'bond order':>10s} {'unpaired':>9s}   valence configuration")
for name, n_valence in molecules:
    # s-p mixing puts sigma2p above pi2p for the lighter elements (up to N2)
    levels = MIXED if name in ("Li2", "Be2", "B2", "C2", "N2") else UNMIXED
    order, unpaired, config = fill(levels, n_valence)
    print(f"{name:7s} {order:10.1f} {unpaired:9d}   {config}")
```

Oxygen comes out with bond order 2, as in the Lewis structure, but with **two unpaired electrons**, one in each π\* orbital: the paramagnetism is explained. B₂ is paramagnetic too, which is the experimental evidence for the s–p mixed order: with the unmixed order its last two electrons would pair in $\sigma_{2p}$. Be₂ and Ne₂ have bond order 0, and neither forms a stable molecule; both are known only as extremely weakly bound pairs in cold gas-phase experiments.

Bond order tracks what we can measure (values from the [NIST Chemistry WebBook](https://webbook.nist.gov/chemistry/) and standard tables, rounded):

| Species | Bond order | Bond length (pm) | Energy to break the bond (kJ/mol) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| N₂ | 3 | 110 | 945 |
| O₂⁺ | 2.5 | 112 | — |
| O₂ | 2 | 121 | 498 |
| O₂⁻ (superoxide) | 1.5 | about 130 | — |
| F₂ | 1 | 141 | 159 |

Removing an electron from O₂ takes it out of an *antibonding* orbital, so O₂⁺ has a *stronger, shorter* bond than O₂ — the opposite of what "removing a bonding electron" intuition suggests, and a prediction no Lewis structure makes.

<Section id="huckel" kind="computation" title="Hückel theory: π electrons from an adjacency matrix">

For larger molecules we need a simpler model. In 1931 Erich Hückel, in Stuttgart, proposed one for the π electrons of planar conjugated hydrocarbons. Its assumptions are drastic and its success is remarkable.

<Definition term="Hückel model">

Treat only the π electrons, with one 2p orbital per carbon atom, perpendicular to the molecular plane. In the secular equations $\mathbf H\mathbf c = E\mathbf S\mathbf c$ set

- $H_{\mu\mu} = \alpha$ for every carbon;
- $H_{\mu\nu} = \beta$ if atoms $\mu$ and $\nu$ are bonded, and $0$ otherwise;
- $\mathbf S = \mathbf I$ (neglect overlap).

Then $\mathbf H = \alpha\mathbf I + \beta\mathbf A$, where $\mathbf A$ is the **adjacency matrix** of the carbon skeleton, so the orbital energies are $E_k = \alpha + x_k\beta$ with $x_k$ the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$. Since $\beta < 0$, the largest $x_k$ is the most bonding orbital.

</Definition>

The physics has been reduced to the shape of a graph: the π energies depend only on which atoms are joined. (This is the start of spectral graph theory; see <TopicLink id="math.discrete.graph-theory">Graph theory</TopicLink>.) Below we build the matrices for ethene, buta-1,3-diene and benzene and diagonalise them with `numpy.linalg.eigh`.

```python run
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=3, suppress=True)

def chain(n):
    """Adjacency matrix of a chain of n carbon atoms (a linear polyene)."""
    A = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n - 1):
        A[i, i + 1] = A[i + 1, i] = 1
    return A

def ring(n):
    A = chain(n)
    A[0, n - 1] = A[n - 1, 0] = 1
    return A

def huckel(A, n_electrons):
    """Hueckel MOs: H = alpha*I + beta*A, so E = alpha + x*beta with x the eigenvalues of A.
    beta < 0, so the LARGEST x is the LOWEST energy. Returns x (lowest energy first), coefficients, pi energy."""
    x, C = np.linalg.eigh(A)
    x, C = x[::-1], C[:, ::-1]                     # sort from most bonding to most antibonding
    occ = np.zeros(len(x))
    occ[: n_electrons // 2] = 2                     # closed shells only in this simple filling
    return x, C, float(occ @ x), occ

for name, A, ne in [("ethene", chain(2), 2), ("buta-1,3-diene", chain(4), 4), ("benzene", ring(6), 6)]:
    x, C, e_pi, occ = huckel(A, ne)
    homo, lumo = x[ne // 2 - 1], x[ne // 2]
    print(f"{name}: orbital energies alpha + x*beta with x = {x}")
    print(f"   pi energy = {ne}alpha + {e_pi:.3f}beta;  HOMO-LUMO gap = {homo - lumo:.3f}|beta|")
    print(f"   delocalisation energy vs {ne // 2} isolated ethene(s): {e_pi - ne:.3f}beta")

# Butadiene's MOs: count the sign changes (nodes) along the chain, and the pi bond orders
x, C, e_pi, occ = huckel(chain(4), 4)
print("\nbutadiene MO coefficients (columns = MOs, lowest energy first):\n", C * np.sign(C[0]))
for k in range(4):
    print(f"   MO {k + 1}: x = {x[k]:+.3f}, nodes = {int(np.sum(np.diff(np.sign(C[:, k])) != 0))}")
P = (C * occ) @ C.T                                 # Coulson pi bond orders p_rs = sum_i n_i c_ri c_si
print("pi bond orders: C1-C2 =", round(P[0, 1], 3), " C2-C3 =", round(P[1, 2], 3))
_, Cb, _, occb = huckel(ring(6), 6)
print("benzene: every C-C pi bond order =", round(((Cb * occb) @ Cb.T)[0, 1], 3))
```

Read the output as a chemist:

- **Ethene** has one bonding π orbital at $\alpha + \beta$ and one antibonding at $\alpha - \beta$: the π bond.
- **Butadiene**'s four MOs have 0, 1, 2 and 3 nodes, exactly like the particle in a box — Hückel orbitals of a chain *are* sampled box wavefunctions. Its π energy, $4\alpha + 4.472\beta$, is lower than two separate ethenes by $0.472\beta$, the **delocalisation energy**. The π bond orders, 0.894 for the outer bonds and 0.447 for the middle one, say that the central bond has partial double-bond character, consistent with its being shorter (about 147 pm) than a typical C–C single bond (154 pm).
- **Benzene** has π energy $6\alpha + 8\beta$, lower than three ethenes by $2\beta$. With the thermochemical value $\beta \approx -75$ kJ/mol, that is about 150 kJ/mol of extra stability, the same size as the "resonance energy" estimated from heats of hydrogenation (<LessonLink slug="thermochemistry">Thermochemistry</LessonLink>). All six bonds have the same π bond order, 2/3.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Hückel's $\beta$ is not one number. Fitted to thermochemistry it comes out near $-75$ kJ/mol (about $-0.8$ eV); fitted to ultraviolet spectra, roughly three times larger, around $-2.5$ eV. The model's neglect of electron repulsion is absorbed into $\beta$ differently by each kind of experiment. Use Hückel for trends, node counts, orbital shapes and symmetries, and never compare its $\beta$ across different kinds of measurement.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="aromaticity" kind="human" title="Rings, aromaticity and the 4n + 2 rule">

Benzene's extra stability belongs to a family. For a ring of $N$ atoms Hückel's matrix can be diagonalised by hand.

<Theorem name="Hückel energies of a ring">

The adjacency matrix of a ring of $N$ atoms has eigenvalues $x_k = 2\cos(2\pi k/N)$, $k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$, with eigenvectors $c_j = e^{2\pi i jk/N}$. So a ring has one lowest level ($k = 0$, $x = 2$), then degenerate pairs ($k$ and $N - k$), and, if $N$ is even, one highest level ($x = -2$).

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Row $j$ of $\mathbf A\mathbf c$ is $c_{j-1} + c_{j+1}$ (indices modulo $N$). With $c_j = \omega^{jk}$, $\omega = e^{2\pi i/N}$, this is $\omega^{jk}(\omega^{-k} + \omega^{k}) = 2\cos(2\pi k/N)\, c_j$. The $N$ vectors with $k = 0, \ldots, N-1$ are linearly independent (they are the columns of the discrete Fourier transform), so these are all the eigenvalues. Since $\cos(2\pi k/N) = \cos(2\pi(N-k)/N)$, levels $k$ and $N-k$ coincide.

</Proof>

The ring's MOs are standing waves running round the ring, the discrete version of a particle on a circle, built from the <TopicLink id="math.number.complex-numbers">complex numbers</TopicLink> $\omega^{jk}$. A neat picture follows: draw a circle of radius $2|\beta|$ centred on $\alpha$, inscribe the ring polygon with one vertex at the bottom, and the vertices sit at the orbital energies (the **Frost circle**, after Arthur Frost and Boris Musulin, 1953).

The pattern — one level, then pairs — means a ring's bonding levels are completely filled by $2, 6, 10, 14, \ldots$ electrons, that is $4n + 2$. With $4n$ electrons the last pair of degenerate orbitals is half full. The block checks the theorem and compares rings with the open chains made of the same atoms and electrons.

```python run
import numpy as np

def chain(n):
    A = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n - 1):
        A[i, i + 1] = A[i + 1, i] = 1
    return A

def ring(n):
    A = chain(n)
    A[0, n - 1] = A[n - 1, 0] = 1
    return A

def fill(A, n_electrons, tol=1e-6):
    """Aufbau with Hund's rule over degenerate shells of H = alpha + x*beta.
    Returns (pi energy in units of beta, unpaired electrons, HOMO-LUMO gap in |beta|)."""
    x = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(A))[::-1]          # most bonding (largest x) first
    shells = []
    for v in x:
        if shells and abs(shells[-1][0] - v) < tol:
            shells[-1][1] += 1
        else:
            shells.append([v, 1])
    energy, unpaired, left, gap = 0.0, 0, n_electrons, None
    for i, (v, g) in enumerate(shells):
        e = min(left, 2 * g)
        left -= e
        energy += e * v
        unpaired += e if e <= g else 2 * g - e
        if gap is None and 0 < e < 2 * g:
            gap = 0.0                                  # a partly filled shell: HOMO = LUMO
        elif gap is None and e == 2 * g and left == 0:
            gap = v - shells[i + 1][0]
    return energy, unpaired, gap

# The eigenvalues of a ring of N atoms are 2cos(2 pi k / N): check it
for N in range(3, 9):
    assert np.allclose(np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(ring(N))), np.sort(2 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * np.arange(N) / N)))
print("ring eigenvalues equal 2cos(2 pi k/N) for N = 3..8\n")

cases = [("cyclopropenyl cation", 3, 2), ("cyclobutadiene", 4, 4), ("cyclopentadienyl cation", 5, 4),
         ("cyclopentadienyl anion", 5, 6), ("benzene", 6, 6), ("tropylium cation", 7, 6),
         ("cyclooctatetraene, planar", 8, 8), ("cyclooctatetraene dianion", 8, 10)]
print(f"{'species':27s} {'pi e':>4s} {'4n+2':>5s} {'unpaired':>8s} {'gap/|beta|':>10s} {'ring - chain':>13s}")
for name, N, ne in cases:
    e_ring, unpaired, gap = fill(ring(N), ne)
    e_chain, _, _ = fill(chain(N), ne)               # the same atoms and electrons, ring opened
    print(f"{name:27s} {ne:4d} {'yes' if (ne - 2) % 4 == 0 else 'no':>5s} {unpaired:8d} {gap:10.3f} {e_ring - e_chain:+9.3f} beta")
```

Since $\beta$ is negative, a positive number in the last column means closing the ring *lowers* the energy. Every $4n + 2$ species is a closed shell with a sizeable HOMO–LUMO gap and is stabilised by ring closure: these are **aromatic**. The cyclopentadienyl anion and the tropylium cation are real, stable ions for exactly this reason, though neither is benzene. Every $4n$ species has two unpaired electrons in a degenerate pair and no gap. Cyclobutadiene and the cyclopentadienyl cation are even destabilised by closing the ring: they are **antiaromatic**. Real cyclobutadiene escapes by distorting to a rectangle with alternating bonds and survives only at very low temperatures or trapped in frozen inert gas. Cyclooctatetraene escapes differently: it folds into a tub shape, its π orbitals stop overlapping round the ring, and it behaves as an ordinary polyene — while its planar dianion, with ten π electrons, is aromatic.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the "4n + 2 rule" is not a rule of thumb at all but a counting fact about the eigenvalues of a cycle: one nondegenerate bottom level plus degenerate pairs. The same counting, with the pairs coming from the symmetry group of the ring, is where <TopicLink id="math.algebra.groups">group theory</TopicLink> enters chemistry.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="frontier" kind="human" title="HOMO and LUMO">

Two orbitals matter more than the rest: the **highest occupied molecular orbital** (HOMO) and the **lowest unoccupied molecular orbital** (LUMO), together called the **frontier orbitals**.

- **Colour and light.** The lowest-energy electronic absorption usually lifts an electron from HOMO to LUMO. Butadiene's gap, $1.236|\beta|$, is smaller than ethene's, $2|\beta|$, and it keeps shrinking along the polyene series, which is why long conjugated molecules such as β-carotene (eleven conjugated C=C bonds) absorb visible light and look orange. Measuring such absorptions is the subject of <LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink>.
- **Ionisation.** By Koopmans' theorem (Tjalling Koopmans, 1934), the energy needed to remove an electron from an orbital is approximately minus that orbital's energy. Photoelectron spectroscopy measures these energies directly and is how MO diagrams such as the one for O₂ are checked.
- **Reactivity.** When two molecules react, the largest stabilising interaction is usually between the HOMO of one and the LUMO of the other, because those orbitals are closest in energy. Kenichi Fukui in Kyoto developed this idea from 1952, and R. B. Woodward and Roald Hoffmann at Harvard used orbital symmetry to explain which ring-forming reactions are allowed; Fukui and Hoffmann shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. You will meet frontier orbitals again in <LessonLink slug="reaction-mechanisms">Reaction mechanisms</LessonLink>, where nucleophiles donate from their HOMO into an electrophile's LUMO.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view">

Hückel theory packs a great deal of insight into a 6 × 6 matrix, and chemists still use it to reason about π systems on the back of an envelope. But practising chemists compute molecular orbitals with far more complete methods. Roald Hoffmann's **extended Hückel** method (1963) kept every valence orbital and the overlaps, and became the workhorse of qualitative inorganic and solid-state chemistry. Today **Hartree–Fock** and **density functional theory**, the subjects of <LessonLink slug="computational-chemistry">Computational chemistry</LessonLink>, solve the same secular equations $\mathbf F\mathbf c = \varepsilon\mathbf S\mathbf c$ self-consistently with all electrons and realistic basis sets, and produce the coloured orbital plots in research papers. The same mathematics, applied to a crystal with a lattice of atoms, gives band structures: the Hückel ring with $N \to \infty$ is, in this model, a one-dimensional metal (see [electronic structure and bands](/en/materials/electronic-structure-and-bands/) in Lumenary Materials).

The qualitative lessons survive every upgrade: count nodes, match symmetry, fill from the bottom, look at the HOMO and the LUMO.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Antibonding orbitals are empty, so they don't matter."** In He₂, F₂ and O₂ they are occupied, and their electrons cancel bonding. And empty ones matter too: a reagent that pushes electrons into a molecule's σ\* or π\* LUMO weakens exactly the bond that orbital belongs to, which is how many reactions begin.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Benzene's double bonds flip back and forth between two positions."** The two Kekulé structures are two drawings of one molecule, not two molecules interconverting. Benzene has a single structure with six equal bonds and six π electrons in delocalised orbitals.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Bond orders must be whole numbers."** O₂⁺ has bond order 2.5 and benzene's π bond orders are 2/3. Bond order is a count of electrons in bonding minus antibonding orbitals, not a count of lines in a drawing.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Molecular orbitals are just the bonds of a Lewis structure."** Canonical MOs, the eigenvectors, spread over the whole molecule: methane has one low MO of one symmetry and three equal higher ones, not four identical C–H bond orbitals, and photoelectron spectra show those two energies. Localised bond orbitals can be built by mixing occupied MOs, which leaves the total energy and electron density unchanged; both descriptions are correct, and each is convenient for different questions.

</Misconception>
