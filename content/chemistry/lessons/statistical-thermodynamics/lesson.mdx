<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="From energy levels to tables">

Look up the standard entropy of carbon monoxide gas in the [NIST Chemistry WebBook](https://webbook.nist.gov/chemistry/) and you will find 197.660 ± 0.004 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, quoted to six figures. Nobody measured that number by warming solid carbon monoxide from near absolute zero, and nobody could: the crystal freezes in with some molecules pointing the wrong way, as <LessonLink slug="entropy-and-free-energy">Entropy and free energy</LessonLink> explained. It was *computed*, from the mass of the molecule, the spacing of its rotational lines and the wavenumber of its vibration, all measured by the methods of <LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink>.

The bridge from those molecular facts to the thermodynamic quantities of <LessonLink slug="thermochemistry">Thermochemistry</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="chemical-equilibrium">Chemical equilibrium</LessonLink> is **statistical thermodynamics**. Quantum mechanics tells us which energies a molecule may have; statistics tells us how a mole of molecules shares out its energy among those levels; and from that sharing follow the entropy, the heat capacity, the Gibbs energy and the equilibrium constant. This lesson builds the bridge and walks across it with real molecules, checking every number against the tables.

</Section>

<Section id="boltzmann" kind="intuition" title="The Boltzmann distribution">

Take one molecule in a gas at temperature $T$. It collides constantly with the others, which act as a huge reservoir of energy, and it hops from one quantum state to another. Which states does it spend its time in? Low-energy states are favoured, but not exclusively: the reservoir can lend energy, and a state's chance falls off exponentially with the energy it costs.

<Definition term="Boltzmann distribution">

In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, the probability that a molecule is in a particular state $i$ of energy $\varepsilon_i$ is proportional to $e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_\mathrm{B}T}$. For an energy **level** with $g_i$ states of the same energy (its degeneracy),

$$
p_i = \frac{g_i\, e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_\mathrm{B}T}}{q}, \qquad q = \sum_j g_j\, e^{-\varepsilon_j/k_\mathrm{B}T}.
$$

</Definition>

The yardstick is $k_\mathrm{B}T$. Levels much less than $k_\mathrm{B}T$ above the lowest are almost as populated as it; levels many times $k_\mathrm{B}T$ above are almost empty. Chemists keep $k_\mathrm{B}T$ in several units at once, and the program below starts by printing them.

Two real cases show the range. Rotational levels of a small molecule are spaced by a few cm⁻¹, far below $k_\mathrm{B}T$, so dozens of them are occupied at room temperature; their degeneracy $2J + 1$ grows while the exponential falls, so the most populated level is not the lowest. Vibrational quanta are usually hundreds or thousands of cm⁻¹, so most molecules sit in $v = 0$. The program computes both.

```python run
import numpy as np

h, c, kB, NA = 6.62607015e-34, 2.99792458e10, 1.380649e-23, 6.02214076e23   # c in cm/s
T = 298.15
print(f"kT at {T} K = {kB * T * NA / 1e3:.3f} kJ/mol = {kB * T / (h * c):.1f} cm^-1 = {kB * T / 1.602176634e-19 * 1e3:.1f} meV\n")

# Rotational levels of CO: E_J = hcB J(J+1), degeneracy 2J + 1
B = 1.9225                                   # cm^-1
J = np.arange(0, 60)
weights = (2 * J + 1) * np.exp(-h * c * B * J * (J + 1) / (kB * T))
p = weights / weights.sum()                  # the Boltzmann distribution
print("CO at 25 C: fraction of molecules in each rotational level")
for j in range(0, 25, 2):
    print(f"  J = {j:2d}  {p[j]:.4f}  " + "#" * int(round(400 * p[j])))
print(f"most populated level: J = {np.argmax(p)};  levels holding 99% of molecules: J = 0 to {np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p), 0.99)}")

# Vibrations: for a harmonic oscillator the fraction NOT in v = 0 is 1 - (1 - e^-x) = e^-x, x = hc nu / kT
print("\nfraction of molecules vibrationally excited (v >= 1)")
print("molecule   wavenumber/cm^-1     298 K       1000 K")
for name, nu in [("H2", 4161), ("N2", 2330), ("CO", 2143), ("Cl2", 554), ("I2", 213)]:
    f = [np.exp(-h * c * nu / (kB * TT)) for TT in (298.15, 1000)]
    print(f"{name:8s} {nu:12d} {f[0]:14.2e} {f[1]:11.3f}")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

Read the vibrational table as a thermometer for "how classical" a motion is. At room temperature only three CO molecules in a hundred thousand are vibrating above their zero-point level, so vibration contributes almost nothing to CO's heat capacity; but more than a third of I₂ molecules are excited, because iodine is heavy and its bond is soft. The rotational table, peaking at J = 7, is the reason infrared bands of gases have their characteristic double hump of lines (<LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink>).

</Narrator>

<Section id="partition-function" kind="definitions" title="The partition function">

Why the exponential? It follows from the one assumption behind <LessonLink slug="entropy-and-free-energy">Entropy and free energy</LessonLink>: an isolated system is equally likely to be in any of its microstates.

<Theorem name="The Boltzmann distribution">

A system in thermal contact with a much larger reservoir at temperature $T$ is found in a particular state $i$ of energy $\varepsilon_i$ with probability $p_i = e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_\mathrm{B}T}/q$, where $q = \sum_j e^{-\varepsilon_j/k_\mathrm{B}T}$ is summed over states.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let the system and reservoir together be isolated, with total energy $E$. When the system is in state $i$, the reservoir has energy $E - \varepsilon_i$ and can be in any of $W_\mathrm{R}(E - \varepsilon_i)$ microstates. All microstates of the whole are equally likely, so $p_i \propto W_\mathrm{R}(E - \varepsilon_i) = \exp[S_\mathrm{R}(E - \varepsilon_i)/k_\mathrm{B}]$, using $S = k_\mathrm{B}\ln W$. Because $\varepsilon_i \ll E$, expand to first order: $S_\mathrm{R}(E - \varepsilon_i) \approx S_\mathrm{R}(E) - \varepsilon_i\, \partial S_\mathrm{R}/\partial E = S_\mathrm{R}(E) - \varepsilon_i/T$, by the statistical definition of temperature, $1/T = \partial S/\partial E$. Hence $p_i \propto e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_\mathrm{B}T}$, and requiring $\sum_i p_i = 1$ fixes the constant as $1/q$. The neglected second-order term is proportional to $\varepsilon_i^2/C_\mathrm{R}$, where $C_\mathrm{R}$ is the reservoir's heat capacity, and vanishes for a large reservoir.

</Proof>

<Definition term="molecular partition function">

The **molecular partition function** is $q = \sum_\text{levels} g_i e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_\mathrm{B}T}$, with energies measured from the molecule's lowest state. It is a pure number, roughly the **number of states thermally accessible** to the molecule: close to 1 at very low temperature (only the ground state), and growing without limit as $T$ rises.

</Definition>

The partition function holds all the thermodynamics. For $N$ molecules of an ideal gas, which are independent and indistinguishable, the total partition function is $Q = q^N/N!$, and differentiating gives (per mole, with $U(0)$ the energy when every molecule is in its lowest state):

$$
U - U(0) = RT^2\left(\frac{\partial \ln q}{\partial T}\right)_V, \qquad
C_V = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V, \qquad
S = \frac{U - U(0)}{T} + R\left(\ln\frac{q}{N_\mathrm A} + 1\right),
$$

$$
H - H(0) = U - U(0) + RT, \qquad G - G(0) = -RT\ln\frac{q}{N_\mathrm A}.
$$

The "+1" and the $N_\mathrm A$ come from the $N!$, that is, from the indistinguishability of identical molecules. A useful equivalent, which the programs below use, is to write the average energy and its fluctuations directly: $U - U(0) = N_\mathrm A\langle\varepsilon\rangle$ and $C_V = N_\mathrm A(\langle\varepsilon^2\rangle - \langle\varepsilon\rangle^2)/k_\mathrm{B}T^2$. A large heat capacity means large energy fluctuations. Mathematically, $q$ as a function of $\beta = 1/k_\mathrm{B}T$ is a *generating function*: $-\partial\ln q/\partial\beta$ is the mean energy and the second derivative its variance, as with a moment-generating function in <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-theory">probability theory</TopicLink>; and $\beta$ is the Lagrange multiplier that appears when $\ln W$ is maximised at fixed total energy (<TopicLink id="math.applied.optimisation">optimisation</TopicLink>).

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why should a molecule favour its low-energy states at all, when nothing pushes it down? The preference comes from its surroundings. Every joule the molecule takes is a joule the rest of the gas no longer has, and the rest of the gas then has fewer ways to arrange itself; the factor $e^{-\varepsilon/k_\mathrm{B}T}$ is exactly that loss of arrangements, as the proof shows. A molecule "prefers" low energy only because the whole system prefers more microstates.

</Narrator>

If a molecule's energy is a sum of independent parts, $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\text{trans} + \varepsilon_\text{rot} + \varepsilon_\text{vib} + \varepsilon_\text{el}$, the exponential of a sum is a product and the partition function factorises: $q = q_\text{trans}\,q_\text{rot}\,q_\text{vib}\,q_\text{el}$. Then $\ln q$, and every thermodynamic function, is a sum of contributions, one per kind of motion.

</Section>

<Section id="contributions" kind="examples" title="Translation, rotation, vibration and electrons">

Each factor comes from a quantum model of <LessonLink slug="quantum-mechanics-for-chemists">Quantum mechanics for chemists</LessonLink> or <LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink>, summed over its levels.

**Translation.** A molecule of mass $m$ in a container of volume $V$ is a particle in a three-dimensional box. Its levels are so close together that the sum becomes an integral:

$$
q_\text{trans} = \frac{V}{\Lambda^3}, \qquad \Lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_\mathrm{B} T}}.
$$

The **thermal wavelength** $\Lambda$ is about the de Broglie wavelength of a molecule at temperature $T$: 19 pm for CO at room temperature. $q_\text{trans}$ counts how many boxes of size $\Lambda^3$ fit in $V$: an astronomically large number, which is why translation dominates the entropy of a gas.

**Rotation.** A linear molecule has levels $hcBJ(J+1)$ with degeneracy $2J + 1$, where $B$ is the rotational constant in cm⁻¹. When $T$ is much larger than the **rotational temperature** $\Theta_\text{rot} = hcB/k_\mathrm{B}$, the sum is close to an integral:

$$
q_\text{rot} \approx \frac{T}{\sigma\,\Theta_\text{rot}} = \frac{k_\mathrm{B}T}{\sigma hcB}.
$$

The **symmetry number** $\sigma$ is 1 for an unsymmetrical linear molecule (CO, HCl) and 2 for a symmetrical one (N₂, CO₂, I₂). A non-linear molecule has three rotational constants and $q_\text{rot} = (\sqrt{\pi}/\sigma)\,(k_\mathrm{B}T/hc)^{3/2}/\sqrt{ABC}$, with $\sigma = 2$ for water and 12 for methane.

**Vibration.** Each normal mode is, to a good approximation, a harmonic oscillator with levels $hc\tilde\nu\,v$ above its zero-point level. The sum is a geometric series:

$$
q_\text{vib} = \frac{1}{1 - e^{-\Theta_\text{vib}/T}}, \qquad \Theta_\text{vib} = \frac{hc\tilde\nu}{k_\mathrm{B}}.
$$

A non-linear molecule of $n$ atoms has $3n - 6$ modes and multiplies their factors; a linear one has $3n - 5$.

**Electrons.** Excited electronic states usually lie so high that $q_\text{el}$ is just the degeneracy of the ground state: 1 for most closed-shell molecules, 3 for O₂ (a triplet), and 4 for an iodine atom, whose ground state ²P₃/₂ has four states.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The symmetry number is not a fudge factor. Turning I₂ end over end gives an arrangement indistinguishable from the original, and quantum mechanics, through the symmetry of the wavefunction under exchange of the two identical nuclei, allows only half of the rotational levels for each nuclear-spin state. At ordinary temperatures this halves $q_\text{rot}$, which is what $\sigma = 2$ does. At low temperatures it is not a factor of two at all, and hydrogen gas, with its widely spaced levels, behaves as a mixture of two different substances (ortho- and para-hydrogen), a puzzle solved by [David Dennison in 1927](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1927.0105).

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="carbon-monoxide" kind="computation" title="Carbon monoxide from its spectrum">

Now the real calculation. Carbon monoxide's spectroscopic constants, from the NIST WebBook, are a rotational constant $B_0 = 1.9225$ cm⁻¹ and a fundamental vibrational wavenumber of 2143.27 cm⁻¹; its molar mass is 28.010 g/mol. Translation uses the formula, at the standard pressure of 1 bar; rotation and vibration are summed level by level with a single function that returns $q$, $U$, $C_V$ and $S$ from any list of levels.

```python run
import numpy as np

h, c, kB, NA = 6.62607015e-34, 2.99792458e10, 1.380649e-23, 6.02214076e23   # SI; c in cm/s
R, p0 = kB * NA, 1e5                                                          # J/(mol K), Pa

def from_levels(energies_K, degeneracies, T):
    """q, and U, Cv, S per mole divided by R, from a list of levels (energies in kelvin, E/k)."""
    e, g = np.asarray(energies_K, float), np.asarray(degeneracies, float)
    w = g * np.exp(-e / T)                 # Boltzmann weights
    q = w.sum()
    mean = (w * e).sum() / q               # <E>/k
    mean2 = (w * e**2).sum() / q
    return q, mean / T, (mean2 - mean**2) / T**2, np.log(q) + mean / T   # q, U/RT, Cv/R, S/R

# Carbon monoxide, from spectroscopy (NIST Chemistry WebBook)
M = 28.010e-3           # kg/mol (natural isotopic mixture)
B = 1.9225              # rotational constant of the v = 0 level, cm^-1
nu = 2143.27            # wavenumber of the fundamental vibration, cm^-1
T = 298.15
theta_rot, theta_vib = h * c * B / kB, h * c * nu / kB

# Translation: particle in a box of the molar volume at 1 bar (Sackur-Tetrode)
Lam = h / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * (M / NA) * kB * T)
q_tr = (R * T / p0) / Lam**3
S_tr, Cp_tr, H_tr = R * (np.log(q_tr / NA) + 2.5), 2.5 * R, 2.5 * R * T

# Rotation: sum over J levels, energy J(J+1) theta_rot, degeneracy 2J+1 (symmetry number 1)
J = np.arange(0, 300)
q_rot, U_rot, Cv_rot, S_rot = from_levels(J * (J + 1) * theta_rot, 2 * J + 1, T)
# Vibration: harmonic levels v * theta_vib, measured from the zero-point level
v = np.arange(0, 40)
q_vib, U_vib, Cv_vib, S_vib = from_levels(v * theta_vib, np.ones_like(v), T)

print(f"theta_rot = {theta_rot:.3f} K   theta_vib = {theta_vib:.0f} K   thermal wavelength = {Lam * 1e12:.2f} pm")
print(f"q_trans = {q_tr:.3e} (in 1 molar volume)   q_rot = {q_rot:.2f} (high-T formula T/theta: {T / theta_rot:.2f})"
      f"   q_vib = {q_vib:.6f}")
print(f"\n            translation   rotation   vibration     total     tables")
S = [S_tr, R * S_rot, R * S_vib]
Cp = [Cp_tr, R * Cv_rot, R * Cv_vib]
H = [H_tr / 1e3, R * T * U_rot / 1e3, R * T * U_vib / 1e3]
print(f"S°  J/(mol K) {S[0]:10.2f} {S[1]:10.2f} {S[2]:11.4f} {sum(S):9.2f}    197.66")
print(f"Cp° J/(mol K) {Cp[0]:10.2f} {Cp[1]:10.2f} {Cp[2]:11.4f} {sum(Cp):9.2f}     29.14")
print(f"H-H(0) kJ/mol {H[0]:10.3f} {H[1]:10.3f} {H[2]:11.5f} {sum(H):9.3f}     8.671")
print(f"(with the high-temperature formula q_rot = T/theta_rot instead of the sum: H - H(0) = "
      f"{(3.5 * R * T + R * T * U_vib) / 1e3:.3f} kJ/mol)")
```

Three molecular numbers and the Planck, Boltzmann and Avogadro constants reproduce the table to within 0.01 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for the entropy and 0.001 kJ/mol for the enthalpy. The breakdown is instructive. Translation provides three-quarters of the entropy, 150.42 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; rotation provides 47.23, because about a hundred rotational states are accessible; vibration provides 0.003, because CO hardly vibrates at room temperature. The explicit sum over rotational levels gives $q_\text{rot} = 108.12$, slightly more than the high-temperature formula's 107.79. That small difference, $\tfrac13$ to a very good approximation, is what brings $H - H(0)$ to 8.670 kJ/mol instead of 8.677.

This is how standard entropies of gases are known so precisely. When the calorimetric third-law entropy of CO came out about 4.6 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ *below* this statistical value, the difference was not an error in either: it is the residual entropy of the disordered crystal, close to $R\ln 2$.

</Section>

<Section id="equilibrium" kind="computation" title="Equilibrium constants from molecular properties">

Because $G - G(0) = -RT\ln(q/N_\mathrm A)$ for each gas, and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}G^\circ = -RT\ln K$, an equilibrium constant can be written entirely in terms of partition functions:

$$
K = \prod_J \left(\frac{q^\circ_{J}}{N_\mathrm A}\right)^{\nu_J} e^{-\Delta_\mathrm{r}E_0/RT},
$$

where $q^\circ_J$ is the partition function of species $J$ in the molar volume at the standard pressure, $\nu_J$ its stoichiometric number (positive for products), and $\Delta_\mathrm{r}E_0$ the difference between the lowest energies of products and reactants, per mole. The two factors pull in opposite directions: $\Delta_\mathrm{r}E_0$ is an energy, and the product of partition functions counts how many states each side can spread over.

Take the dissociation of iodine vapour, I₂(g) ⇌ 2 I(g). The energy is the dissociation energy measured from the molecule's lowest vibrational level, $D_0 = 12\,440$ cm⁻¹ (148.8 kJ/mol); the molecule's constants are $B_0 = 0.03731$ cm⁻¹, $\tilde\nu = 214.5$ cm⁻¹ and $\sigma = 2$; the atoms have only translation and their electronic degeneracy of 4. The program compares the result with the equilibrium constant calculated from tabulated enthalpies of formation and entropies.

```python run
import numpy as np

h, c, kB, NA = 6.62607015e-34, 2.99792458e10, 1.380649e-23, 6.02214076e23   # c in cm/s
R, p0 = kB * NA, 1e5
cm_to_K = h * c / kB                     # 1 cm^-1 corresponds to 1.4388 K

def ln_q_std(M, T, B=None, sigma=1, nus=(), g_el=1):
    """ln(q°/N_A) for an ideal gas at 1 bar: translation, rigid linear rotor (B, sigma),
    harmonic vibrations (wavenumbers nus, from the zero-point level) and electronic degeneracy."""
    Lam = h / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * (M * 1e-3 / NA) * kB * T)
    lnq = np.log(kB * T / p0 / Lam**3) + np.log(g_el)
    if B is not None:
        lnq += np.log(T / (sigma * B * cm_to_K))
    for nu in nus:
        lnq -= np.log(1 - np.exp(-nu * cm_to_K / T))
    return lnq

D0 = 12440.0     # cm^-1: energy to split I2 from its lowest level into two ground-state atoms (148.8 kJ/mol)

def K_iodine(T):
    """K° for I2(g) <=> 2 I(g) from molecular data alone."""
    ln_I = ln_q_std(126.904, T, g_el=4)                         # I atom: 2P3/2 ground state, 4 states
    ln_I2 = ln_q_std(253.809, T, B=0.03731, sigma=2, nus=[214.5])
    return np.exp(2 * ln_I - ln_I2 - D0 * cm_to_K / T)

# The same K from thermochemical tables (CODATA values on the NIST WebBook) at 298.15 K
dH = 2 * 106.76 - 62.42             # kJ/mol: 2 x DfH°(I, g) - DfH°(I2, g)
dS = 2 * 180.787 - 260.687          # J/(mol K): 2 x S°(I, g) - S°(I2, g)
K_tables = np.exp(-(dH * 1e3 - 298.15 * dS) / (R * 298.15))
print(f"298.15 K: K from molecular data = {K_iodine(298.15):.3e}   from tables = {K_tables:.3e}")
print(f"1000 K:   K from molecular data = {K_iodine(1000):.3e}   from NIST-JANAF tables = 3.08e-03\n")

# Where does K pass 1 (Delta G° = 0)? Bisection on ln K.
lo, hi = 800.0, 2000.0
for _ in range(60):
    mid = (lo + hi) / 2
    lo, hi = (mid, hi) if K_iodine(mid) < 1 else (lo, mid)
print(f"K = 1 at T = {lo:.0f} K")

# ln K splits into a "number of states" term and an energy term
for T in (298.15, 1000.0, 1446.0):
    states = 2 * ln_q_std(126.904, T, g_el=4) - ln_q_std(253.809, T, B=0.03731, sigma=2, nus=[214.5])
    energy = D0 * cm_to_K / T
    print(f"{T:7.2f} K: ln K = {states:6.2f} (states) - {energy:6.2f} (bond energy) = {states - energy:7.2f}")
```

Molecular data alone give $K = 6.35 \times 10^{-22}$ at 298.15 K; the thermochemical tables give $6.28 \times 10^{-22}$, a difference of 1% in a number twenty-one orders of magnitude below one. At 1000 K the two agree within 4%, and most of the gap comes from treating the soft I₂ vibration as harmonic. The last lines show the competition. Two free atoms have far more states available than one molecule, worth about 12 in $\ln K$ at every temperature; the bond costs $D_0/k_\mathrm{B}T$, which is 60 at room temperature but falls as $1/T$. The two balance at about 1450 K, above which iodine vapour is mostly atoms at standard pressure. Every dissociation shares this pattern: high temperature favours the side with more particles, because entropy is weighted by $T$ in $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The energy zero must be the same for every species, and it is easy to get wrong. Each partition function above counts energies from its own molecule's lowest state, including the zero-point vibration, so the energy term must be $D_0$, measured from that state, and not the depth of the potential well $D_\mathrm{e}$, which is larger by the zero-point energy, about 107 cm⁻¹ for I₂. Mixing the two conventions changes $K$ at room temperature by a factor of about 1.7.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="heat-capacity" kind="computation" title="Heat capacities">

Classical physics has a simple rule, **equipartition**: every quadratic term in a molecule's energy contributes $\tfrac12 R$ to the molar heat capacity $C_V$. Translation has three ($\tfrac32 R$), a linear rotor two ($R$), and each vibration two, kinetic and potential ($R$). So a diatomic gas should have $C_V = \tfrac72 R$, and $C_p = C_V + R = \tfrac92 R$, at every temperature. At room temperature it does not: CO has $C_p = 3.5R$. Explaining this was one of the first triumphs of quantum theory.

The rule holds only for motions whose level spacing is much smaller than $k_\mathrm{B}T$. A motion with widely spaced levels is **frozen**: almost every molecule is in its ground level, and adding a little heat cannot excite it. For one harmonic vibration the heat capacity is Einstein's function, from his [1907 paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/andp.19063270110) on solids,

$$
\frac{C_{V,\text{vib}}}{R} = \frac{x^2 e^{x}}{(e^{x} - 1)^2}, \qquad x = \frac{\Theta_\text{vib}}{T},
$$

which is 0 at low temperature and rises to 1, the classical value, when $T \gg \Theta_\text{vib}$.

For carbon monoxide, with $\Theta_\text{vib}$ = 3084 K, the program follows $C_p$ from 100 K to 5000 K and compares it with the NIST-JANAF tables.

```python run
import numpy as np

h, c, kB, R = 6.62607015e-34, 2.99792458e10, 1.380649e-23, 8.314462618
theta_vib = h * c * 2143.27 / kB          # CO, in K

def einstein(T, theta):
    """Heat capacity of one harmonic vibration, in units of R."""
    x = theta / T
    return x**2 * np.exp(x) / np.expm1(x)**2

def cp_over_R(T):
    return 2.5 + 1.0 + einstein(T, theta_vib)   # translation (with pV), rotation, vibration

nist = {298.15: 29.14, 500: 29.82, 1000: 33.18, 2000: 36.22}   # NIST-JANAF (Shomate fits), J/(mol K)
print("   T/K     Cp/R   Cp J/(mol K)  NIST-JANAF   vibrational part")
for T in [100, 298.15, 500, 750, 1000, 1500, 2000, 3000, 5000]:
    cp = cp_over_R(T)
    ref = f"{nist[T]:8.2f}" if T in nist else " " * 8
    print(f"{T:8.2f}  {cp:6.3f}  {cp * R:9.2f}    {ref}     " + "#" * int(round(20 * (cp - 3.5))))

lo, hi = 100.0, 5000.0                    # where is the vibration "half switched on"?
for _ in range(60):
    mid = (lo + hi) / 2
    lo, hi = (mid, hi) if einstein(mid, theta_vib) < 0.5 else (lo, mid)
print(f"\nC(vib) reaches R/2 at {lo:.0f} K = theta_vib / {theta_vib / lo:.2f}")
```

The rigid-rotor, harmonic-oscillator model stays within 1% of the tables up to 2000 K. The vibration is half switched on near 1030 K, about a third of $\Theta_\text{vib}$, and the heat capacity approaches the classical $\tfrac92 R$ only above several thousand kelvin. The remaining differences at high temperature come from anharmonicity and from the stretching of the rotating molecule, which the model leaves out. The same Einstein function, applied to the vibrations of atoms in a crystal, explains why the heat capacities of solids fall towards zero at low temperature.

<Section id="professional" kind="reading" title="Statistical thermodynamics at work">

Statistical thermodynamics runs quietly underneath much of chemistry and engineering:

- **Thermochemical tables.** The [NIST-JANAF tables](https://janaf.nist.gov/) and similar compilations used to design combustion engines, rockets and chemical reactors give $C_p$, $S$ and $G$ for gases up to 6000 K. Above a few hundred kelvin almost all of those numbers are computed from spectroscopic constants, with corrections for anharmonicity, rotation–vibration coupling and excited electronic states, exactly as in this lesson but with more terms.
- **Quantum chemistry output.** Every frequency calculation in a program such as those of <LessonLink slug="computational-chemistry">Computational chemistry</LessonLink> ends with a table of translational, rotational, vibrational and electronic contributions to $H$, $S$ and $G$, computed with the rigid-rotor, harmonic-oscillator model. Chemists then correct the low-frequency modes, which the harmonic model treats worst (they are often hindered internal rotations), because those modes carry most of the error in computed entropies. The [CCCBDB](https://cccbdb.nist.gov/) at NIST compares such calculations with experiment for hundreds of molecules.
- **Rates.** Eyring's transition-state theory, met in <LessonLink slug="chemical-kinetics">Chemical kinetics</LessonLink>, is this lesson's equilibrium formula applied to an equilibrium between reactants and the transition state: $k = (k_\mathrm{B}T/h)K^\ddagger$, with $K^\ddagger$ built from partition functions. Kinetic isotope effects follow from the change in vibrational partition functions when hydrogen is replaced by deuterium (<LessonLink slug="reaction-mechanisms">Reaction mechanisms</LessonLink>).
- **Liquids and proteins.** When molecules interact strongly the partition function cannot be factorised, and it is sampled instead: Metropolis Monte Carlo ([1953](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1699114)) and molecular dynamics generate configurations with Boltzmann probabilities, and free-energy methods compute differences in $\ln Q$, for example between a drug bound to a protein and free in water (see [atomistic simulation](/en/materials/atomistic-simulation/) in Lumenary Materials).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"At equilibrium every molecule has the average energy."** The Boltzmann distribution is a distribution. At room temperature some CO molecules are in $J = 0$ and some in $J = 20$; a few in a hundred thousand are vibrationally excited. Chemical reactions often depend on the rare, energetic tail.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The highest level is always the least populated, so the lowest level is always the most populated."** Each *state* is less likely the higher its energy, but a *level* with many states can hold more molecules than a level below it. CO's most populated rotational level at 25 °C is $J = 7$, not $J = 0$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Equipartition gives the heat capacity of any gas."** Only for motions whose level spacing is small compared with $k_\mathrm{B}T$. Vibrations of light, stiffly bonded molecules are frozen at room temperature, and so are rotations of hydrogen at low temperature.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The partition function is just the normalising constant of the Boltzmann distribution."** It is that, and also everything else: its logarithm and derivatives give $U$, $S$, $H$, $G$, $C_V$ and $K$. Normalising constants rarely know so much.

</Misconception>
