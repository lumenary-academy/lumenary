<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Careful, not scared">

Open the cupboard under a kitchen sink and you will find a small chemistry laboratory: washing-up liquid, bleach, oven cleaner, dishwasher tablets, perhaps a drain unblocker or a kettle descaler. In the bathroom there are medicines, nail-varnish remover and hand-sanitiser gel. Most of the time they are perfectly useful. Used the wrong way, several of them can burn skin, blind an eye, poison a small child or start a fire.

Chemists handle far more dangerous substances than these, every working day, and accidents are rare. That is not because they are fearless, and not because they are scared. It is because they **know what each substance can do and plan for it**: they read the label, wear the right protection, use the right equipment, and know in advance what to do if something goes wrong. Working safely is not a separate subject from chemistry; it is part of doing chemistry well.

I'm Chloe. Here is the key idea of this lesson, in one sentence: a chemical's **hazard** is what it *could* do to you, and the **risk** is how likely that is to happen, given the way you use it. Good chemists keep the hazard the same and make the risk tiny.

</Narrator>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Hazard, risk and the dose">

<Definition term="hazard and risk">

A **hazard** is something that could cause harm: a substance that burns skin, catches fire or is poisonous. A **risk** is the chance that harm actually happens, and how bad it would be, in a particular situation.

</Definition>

Petrol is a good example. Its hazard never changes: it catches fire very easily. In a sealed tank inside a car, the risk is small. Poured into an open bowl next to a barbecue, the risk is enormous. Chemists cannot change the hazard of a substance, but they can change almost everything about how it is used: how much, in what container, with what protection, near what else, and by whom.

A second idea is older. In the sixteenth century the Swiss physician Paracelsus taught that whether a substance harms you depends on the **dose**: how much of it gets into your body. Even water and table salt are harmful in very large amounts, while a few substances are dangerous in amounts too small to see. So "Is this chemical dangerous?" is not quite the right question. Better questions are: "What can it do, how much of it is here, and how could it get into or onto someone?"

<Misconception>

**"Chemicals are dangerous; natural things are safe."** Everything is made of chemicals: water, air, apples and you. Some natural substances are among the most poisonous known, such as the toxins in certain mushrooms and berries, and many made-in-a-factory substances are harmless. Whether something is natural tells you nothing about its hazard. The label and the safety information do.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="pictograms" kind="examples" title="Reading the warning signs">

Imagine buying the same cleaning product in Brazil, Kenya and Japan and finding three completely different warning symbols. For a long time that is roughly how it was. In 2002 countries working together at the United Nations agreed on one shared system, the **Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals**, or **GHS**, first published in 2003. Since then most countries, including the members of the European Union, the United States, China, Japan, Brazil and South Africa, have built it into their own laws.

The GHS uses nine **hazard pictograms**: a black symbol on a white background inside a diamond with a red border.

| Pictogram | What it warns about | Where you might meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Exploding bomb** | substances that can explode | specialist workplaces and laboratories only, never at home |
| **Flame** | catches fire easily, as a solid, liquid or gas | petrol, methylated spirit, nail-varnish remover, hand-sanitiser gel, aerosol cans |
| **Flame over circle** | an *oxidiser*: makes other things burn far more fiercely | some swimming-pool and laboratory chemicals |
| **Gas cylinder** | gas under pressure: can burst if heated; very cold gases cause cold burns | camping-gas and welding cylinders |
| **Corrosion** | destroys skin and eyes, and can eat into metal | oven cleaner, drain unblocker, some descalers and dishwasher products |
| **Skull and crossbones** | poisonous: can kill or seriously harm even in small amounts | some pesticides and laboratory chemicals; rarely found at home |
| **Exclamation mark** | less severe harm: irritates skin or eyes, causes allergies, or is harmful if swallowed | many household cleaning products |
| **Health hazard** | serious harm over time: damage to organs, cancer, breathing allergies, or danger if it goes into the lungs when swallowed | some paint thinners and lamp oils |
| **Environment** | harmful to fish and other living things in water | some garden pesticides and wood preservatives |

Many products carry more than one pictogram. Nail-varnish remover, for instance, often has a flame and an exclamation mark.

### A label, line by line

A GHS label also carries words, and each line has a job:

- **The product name**, and who made or supplied it, with a telephone number.
- **A signal word.** There are only two: **Danger** for the more severe hazards, and **Warning** for the less severe ones.
- **Hazard statements**, which say in plain words what the hazard is, for example *Causes severe skin burns and eye damage*. Each has an official code beginning with H; that one is H314.
- **Precautionary statements**, coded P, which say what to do: keep out of reach of children, wear gloves and eye protection, what to do if it gets in your eyes, how to store it and how to throw it away.

<Example title="The oven cleaner">

Amara finds a can of oven cleaner. Its label shows the **corrosion** pictogram, the signal word **Danger**, and the statement *Causes severe skin burns and eye damage*. The precautions say to wear protective gloves and eye protection, to keep the can out of reach of children and, if the product gets into the eyes, to rinse with water for several minutes and get medical help.

From that label alone, Amara knows the hazard (burns), how bad it can be (Danger, not Warning), how to lower the risk (gloves, eye protection, out of reach of her little brother) and what to do in an accident. She also knows that cleaning the oven is a job for an adult.

</Example>

### Safety data sheets

A label has room for only the most important facts. Every supplier of a hazardous chemical to a workplace must also provide a **safety data sheet** (SDS): a document, usually several pages long, with sixteen numbered sections always in the same order. They cover what the substance is, its hazards, first aid, fire-fighting, what to do about a spill, safe handling and storage, protective equipment, its physical and chemical properties, what it reacts with, how toxic it is, its effects on the environment, how to dispose of it, transport rules and the laws that apply.

Before a class does a practical, the teacher or the school's technician reads the safety data sheets for every chemical involved and writes a **risk assessment**: what could go wrong, and how they will stop it. You can look up the hazards of almost any substance yourself in the free database [PubChem](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), in the section called "Safety and Hazards".

I'm Gideon, and here is a precise point. A label lists only the hazards a substance has been *classified* with. No pictogram does not mean "safe in every way": very hot water has no label at all, and it scalds. Medicines don't carry GHS pictograms either; they come with their own leaflet. So read whatever information comes with a product, and follow its instructions exactly.

</Narrator>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="A tiny label reader">

School and university chemical stores keep a computer record of every bottle, with its pictograms, so that anyone can check its hazards at once. Here is a very small version. It stores what each pictogram means, with one precaution that goes with it, and prints a summary of a label. The three products are typical of what you might find at home, but real labels vary: always read the one in front of you.

```python run
# What each GHS pictogram warns about, and one precaution that goes with it
PICTOGRAMS = {
    "GHS01": ("exploding bomb", "can explode", "keep away from heat, sparks and flames"),
    "GHS02": ("flame", "catches fire easily", "keep away from heat, sparks and flames"),
    "GHS03": ("flame over circle", "makes other things burn fiercely", "keep away from things that burn"),
    "GHS04": ("gas cylinder", "gas under pressure", "protect from sunlight and heat"),
    "GHS05": ("corrosion", "burns skin and eyes, eats metal", "wear gloves and eye protection"),
    "GHS06": ("skull and crossbones", "poisonous, even in small amounts", "trained adults only"),
    "GHS07": ("exclamation mark", "harmful or irritating", "avoid contact with skin and eyes"),
    "GHS08": ("health hazard", "serious long-term harm to health", "trained adults only"),
    "GHS09": ("environment", "harms fish and other wildlife", "never pour it down the drain"),
}

def read_label(product, codes, signal_word):
    print(f"{product}  [{signal_word.upper()}]")
    for code in codes:
        picture, meaning, precaution = PICTOGRAMS[code]
        print(f"  {code} {picture:18} {meaning}; {precaution}")
    print()

read_label("Oven cleaner", ["GHS05"], "Danger")
read_label("Hand-sanitiser gel", ["GHS02", "GHS07"], "Danger")
read_label("Garden insect spray", ["GHS07", "GHS09"], "Warning")
```

Press **Edit** and add a label of your own: nail-varnish remover, say, with `["GHS02", "GHS07"]` and the signal word `"Danger"`.

<Section id="habits" kind="human" title="Safe habits, and why each one matters">

These are the habits of every good chemist, whether in a school laboratory or a university research group. Each rule has a reason.

1. **Wear eye protection** whenever chemicals, hot liquids or glassware are in use, even if your own experiment looks harmless: someone else's might splash. Eyes are easily damaged and cannot be replaced. Tie back long hair, and wear a lab coat and closed shoes when your teacher asks.
2. **Never taste anything in a laboratory, and never eat or drink there.** Food, drinks and chemicals are kept completely apart, so nothing ends up in the wrong place.
3. **Never smell a chemical directly**, and never put your face over an open container. Some vapours harm the nose and lungs even in small amounts.
4. **Wash your hands** with soap and water after practical work, even if you wore gloves.
5. **Never mix household cleaners.** Some combinations give off poisonous gases. Never mix bleach with other cleaners, especially ones containing ammonia or acidic toilet cleaners. Use one product at a time, exactly as its label says.
6. **Keep chemicals in their original, labelled containers**, out of reach of children and away from food. Never pour a chemical into a drink bottle or a food tub: people, often small children, have been badly hurt by drinking from a bottle that held something else.
7. **Follow instructions exactly, and do only the experiments your teacher has approved.** Changing amounts or mixing extra things "to see what happens" is how accidents start.
8. **Tell your teacher at once** about any spill, breakage, cut or splash, however small. They know how to deal with it.
9. **Know where the safety equipment is**: the eyewash, the exits, the fire blanket.

### What a school laboratory has that a kitchen does not

A school laboratory is built for chemistry. It has **fume cupboards** that draw vapours away from people; **eyewash stations** and often a safety shower; fire blankets and extinguishers; benches that resist heat and spills; proper glassware and heating equipment; goggles for everyone; a locked, ventilated store for chemicals; kits for cleaning up spills; and, most important, trained teachers and technicians who have read the safety data sheets and planned the lesson. A kitchen has none of these.

That is why the only experiments Lumenary Chemistry suggests for home are **kitchen-safe ones, done with an adult**, using things that are already sold as food: salt, sugar, vinegar, lemon juice, baking soda and red cabbage. Everything else belongs in a laboratory, with trained supervision.

</Section>

<Section id="accidents" kind="human" title="If something goes wrong">

Even careful people have accidents. What matters is knowing what to do *before* it happens, so that you can act quickly. This is not a first-aid course, but these are the basic steps that safety organisations give:

- **Tell an adult at once**, and if someone is badly hurt, call for help: your local emergency number (for example 112 across Europe and in India, 911 in North America, 999 in the United Kingdom, 000 in Australia). For a possible poisoning, a **poison information centre** can advise; find the number for your country and keep it by the phone.
- **Chemical in the eye:** rinse it straight away with plenty of clean, gently running water, holding the eyelids open, for at least 15 minutes, and get medical help.
- **Chemical on the skin:** rinse with plenty of running water for a long time, at least 10 to 20 minutes; an adult should help take off any clothing it has soaked into.
- **Chemical swallowed:** do not try to make the person sick. Call a poison centre or emergency help straight away, and take the container or label with you so doctors know what it was.
- **Fumes breathed in:** get the person into fresh air.
- **Fire:** get out, raise the alarm and call for help. Never go back for anything.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Why plain water, and not something to "cancel out" the chemical? Because adding a second chemical can make heat, and in an eye or on skin you need to wash the first one *away*, not start a new reaction. Water dilutes and rinses, and lots of it is always available.

</Narrator>

<Narrator who="margaret">

I'm Margaret. Let's see what "plenty of water" means. Workplace eyewash stations are designed to deliver about 1.5 litres a minute for 15 minutes: $1.5 \times 15 = 22.5$ litres, more than two full buckets for one pair of eyes! That is how much rinsing it takes to wash a chemical away.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Why the rules exist">

Many safety rules were learned the hard way. Early chemists often tasted and smelled the substances they made, because they had no other way to describe them; several damaged their health doing so. Marie Curie and her colleagues worked with radioactive elements before anyone understood the danger, and her laboratory notebooks are still so radioactive that they are kept in lead-lined boxes in Paris. In 1984 a leak of poisonous gas from a pesticide factory in Bhopal, India, killed thousands of people and harmed many more; it changed how factories everywhere plan for emergencies and tell their neighbours about the chemicals they store.

Today's rules, from goggles in class to safety data sheets and the GHS pictograms, are how chemists make sure those lessons are never needed again.

<Reflection title="care for the next person">

The command to "love your neighbour as yourself" has a very practical side in a laboratory. Labelling a bottle properly, wiping up a spill, and putting things back where they belong all protect someone you may never meet: the next class, the technician, the person who empties the bin. Safe habits are a form of care.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"If it doesn't have a pictogram, it's completely safe."** A label shows only the hazards a substance has been classified with. Boiling water, broken glass and medicines carry no GHS pictograms, but each needs care.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Goggles are only for dangerous experiments."** Splashes are most likely when you are not expecting them, and they can come from someone else's experiment. Eye protection is worn whenever chemicals, hot liquids or glassware are in use.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A little bit of a stronger cleaner will work better."** Using more than the label says, or adding a second product, does not clean better; it adds risk, and some mixtures release poisonous gases. Use one product, as directed.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The Danger and Warning signal words mean the same thing."** They don't. **Danger** is used for the more severe hazards and **Warning** for the less severe ones, and a label carries only one of them: the more serious one that applies.

</Misconception>
