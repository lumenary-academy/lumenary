<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Chemistry on a computer">

In 1929 Paul Dirac [remarked](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1929.0094) that the physical laws needed for the whole of chemistry were now completely known, and that the only difficulty was that the equations were far too complicated to solve. The rest of the century was spent on that difficulty. <LessonLink slug="quantum-mechanics-for-chemists">Quantum mechanics for chemists</LessonLink> solved the Schrödinger equation exactly for one electron; <LessonLink slug="molecular-orbitals">Molecular orbitals</LessonLink> solved a drastically simplified version for π electrons. Real molecules have dozens or thousands of electrons that all repel each other.

Today a chemist can compute, on a laptop, the structure of a medium-sized molecule to within a picometre or two, its vibrational spectrum to within a few per cent, and the energy of a reaction to within a few kJ/mol; with a computing cluster, the mechanism of an enzyme. Computation has become an instrument, used alongside the spectrometer, and the 1998 Nobel Prize, to Walter Kohn and John Pople, and the 2013 prize, to Martin Karplus, Michael Levitt and Arieh Warshel, recognised the methods that made it so.

Like any instrument, it has a working range and ways of misleading. This lesson explains the main methods, from the most accurate and expensive to the cheapest, runs small versions of two of them, and ends with how chemists choose.

</Section>

<Section id="problem" kind="definitions" title="The electronic-structure problem">

Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons and move far more slowly, so chemists solve for the electrons with the nuclei held still. This is the **Born–Oppenheimer approximation**, already used in <LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink>.

<Definition term="electronic-structure problem">

For fixed nuclear positions $\mathbf R$, find the lowest eigenvalue $E(\mathbf R)$ of the electronic Hamiltonian

$$
\hat H_\text{el} = -\sum_i \tfrac12\nabla_i^2 \;-\; \sum_{i,A}\frac{Z_A}{r_{iA}} \;+\; \sum_{i\lt j}\frac{1}{r_{ij}} \;+\; \sum_{A\lt B}\frac{Z_AZ_B}{R_{AB}}
$$

(in atomic units: kinetic energy of the electrons, their attraction to the nuclei, their repulsion of each other, and the repulsion of the nuclei). The function $E(\mathbf R)$ over all nuclear positions is the **potential energy surface**.

</Definition>

Almost everything a chemist wants is a feature of that surface. Its minima are stable structures; the curvature at a minimum gives vibrational frequencies and, through <LessonLink slug="statistical-thermodynamics">Statistical thermodynamics</LessonLink>, entropies and free energies; saddle points are transition states, and the energies of minima and saddles give equilibrium constants and, through Eyring's equation, rates. The difficulty is entirely in the term $1/r_{ij}$: without it, each electron could be solved for separately.

The target accuracy has a name. **Chemical accuracy** is about 1 kcal/mol, or 4 kJ/mol, because an error of that size changes a rate or an equilibrium constant at room temperature by a factor of about five. Total energies are enormous by comparison, millions of kJ/mol for a medium-sized molecule, so methods succeed only when their errors cancel between the things being compared.

</Section>

<Section id="hartree-fock" kind="computation" title="Hartree–Fock and the self-consistent field">

The oldest practical approach, developed by Douglas Hartree and Vladimir Fock around 1930, replaces the repulsion between every pair of electrons by the repulsion of each electron with the *average* charge cloud of all the others. Each electron then has its own orbital, as in <LessonLink slug="molecular-orbitals">Molecular orbitals</LessonLink>, and the many-electron wavefunction is the antisymmetrised product of orbitals, a single Slater determinant (<LessonLink slug="quantum-mechanics-for-chemists">Quantum mechanics for chemists</LessonLink>).

<Definition term="Roothaan–Hall equations">

Expanding each orbital in a fixed set of basis functions, $\psi_i = \sum_\mu C_{\mu i}\phi_\mu$, turns Hartree–Fock theory into a matrix equation (Clemens Roothaan and George Hall, 1951):

$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf C)\,\mathbf C = \mathbf S\,\mathbf C\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon,
$$

where $\mathbf S$ is the overlap matrix of the basis functions and the **Fock matrix** $\mathbf F$ is the one-electron Hamiltonian plus the average repulsion, which depends on the occupied orbitals $\mathbf C$ themselves.

</Definition>

Because $\mathbf F$ depends on the answer, the equations are solved by iteration: guess the orbitals, build $\mathbf F$, solve the generalised eigenvalue problem, rebuild $\mathbf F$ from the new orbitals, and repeat until nothing changes. The result is a **self-consistent field** (SCF).

The program below is a complete Hartree–Fock code for molecules whose basis functions are all s-type Gaussians, enough for H₂ and HeH⁺. It computes the overlap, kinetic, nuclear-attraction and electron-repulsion integrals from their closed-form expressions, runs the SCF loop, and compares two standard basis sets.

```python run
import numpy as np
from math import erf, pi, sqrt

# Basis functions: contractions of normalised s-type Gaussians, (exponents, coefficients) on a centre.
STO3G_H  = ([3.42525091, 0.62391373, 0.16885540], [0.15432897, 0.53532814, 0.44463454])
STO3G_He = ([6.36242139, 1.15892300, 0.31364979], [0.15432897, 0.53532814, 0.44463454])
G631_H   = [([18.7311370, 2.8253937, 0.6401217], [0.03349460, 0.23472695, 0.81375733]),
            ([0.1612778], [1.0])]                 # 6-31G for hydrogen: two s functions

def F0(t):                                        # the Boys function for s orbitals
    return 1.0 if t < 1e-12 else 0.5 * sqrt(pi / t) * erf(sqrt(t))

def norm(a):
    return (2 * a / pi) ** 0.75

def prim_integrals(a, A, b, B, nuclei):
    p, AB2 = a + b, np.sum((A - B) ** 2)
    P = (a * A + b * B) / p
    K = np.exp(-a * b / p * AB2) * norm(a) * norm(b)
    S = (pi / p) ** 1.5 * K
    T = a * b / p * (3 - 2 * a * b / p * AB2) * (pi / p) ** 1.5 * K
    V = sum(-2 * pi / p * Z * K * F0(p * np.sum((P - C) ** 2)) for Z, C in nuclei)
    return S, T, V

def prim_eri(a, A, b, B, c, C, d, D):
    p, q = a + b, c + d
    P, Q = (a * A + b * B) / p, (c * C + d * D) / q
    pre = 2 * pi ** 2.5 / (p * q * sqrt(p + q))
    e = np.exp(-a * b / p * np.sum((A - B) ** 2) - c * d / q * np.sum((C - D) ** 2))
    return pre * e * F0(p * q / (p + q) * np.sum((P - Q) ** 2)) * norm(a) * norm(b) * norm(c) * norm(d)

def scf(basis, nuclei, n_electrons, verbose=False):
    """Restricted Hartree-Fock (Roothaan) for s-only basis sets. basis: list of (contraction, centre)."""
    n = len(basis)
    S, H = np.zeros((n, n)), np.zeros((n, n))
    for i, ((ea, ca), A) in enumerate(basis):
        for j, ((eb, cb), B) in enumerate(basis):
            for a, da in zip(ea, ca):
                for b, db in zip(eb, cb):
                    s, t, v = prim_integrals(a, A, b, B, nuclei)
                    S[i, j] += da * db * s
                    H[i, j] += da * db * (t + v)
    eri = np.zeros((n, n, n, n))
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            for k in range(n):
                for l in range(n):
                    (e1, c1), A = basis[i]; (e2, c2), B = basis[j]
                    (e3, c3), C = basis[k]; (e4, c4), D = basis[l]
                    eri[i, j, k, l] = sum(d1 * d2 * d3 * d4 * prim_eri(a1, A, a2, B, a3, C, a4, D)
                                          for a1, d1 in zip(e1, c1) for a2, d2 in zip(e2, c2)
                                          for a3, d3 in zip(e3, c3) for a4, d4 in zip(e4, c4))
    w, U = np.linalg.eigh(S)
    X = U @ np.diag(w ** -0.5) @ U.T               # symmetric orthogonalisation, X = S^(-1/2)
    P = np.zeros((n, n))                           # start from no electron repulsion at all
    E_old = 0.0
    for it in range(1, 100):
        F = H + np.einsum("kl,ijkl->ij", P, eri) - 0.5 * np.einsum("kl,ikjl->ij", P, eri)
        eps, Cp = np.linalg.eigh(X.T @ F @ X)
        C = X @ Cp
        occ = C[:, : n_electrons // 2]
        P = 2 * occ @ occ.T
        E = 0.5 * np.sum(P * (H + F))
        if verbose:
            print(f"   iteration {it}: electronic energy {E:.8f} hartree")
        if abs(E - E_old) < 1e-10:
            break
        E_old = E
    E_nuc = sum(Z1 * Z2 / np.linalg.norm(C1 - C2) for i, (Z1, C1) in enumerate(nuclei) for Z2, C2 in nuclei[i + 1:])
    return E + E_nuc, eps

def h2(R, which):
    A, B = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0]), np.array([0.0, 0.0, R])
    shells = [STO3G_H] if which == "STO-3G" else G631_H
    basis = [(s, A) for s in shells] + [(s, B) for s in shells]
    return scf(basis, [(1.0, A), (1.0, B)], 2)[0]

R = 1.4                                            # bohr (74.1 pm)
print("H2 at R = 1.4 bohr, total energy in hartree")
for which in ("STO-3G", "6-31G"):
    print(f"   Hartree-Fock / {which:7s} {h2(R, which):.5f}")
print("   Hartree-Fock limit       -1.13363   (a complete basis)")
print("   exact                    -1.17447   (Kolos and Wolniewicz)")
print(f"   correlation energy        {(-1.17447 + 1.13363) * 2625.4996:.0f} kJ/mol")

# Equilibrium bond length: a parabola through the energies near the minimum
for which in ("STO-3G", "6-31G"):
    Rs = np.linspace(1.25, 1.50, 11)
    E = [h2(r, which) for r in Rs]
    a2, a1, a0 = np.polyfit(Rs, E, 2)
    print(f"{which:7s} equilibrium bond length {-a1 / (2 * a2) * 52.9177:.1f} pm   (experiment 74.1 pm)")

print("\nHeH+ (STO-3G, R = 1.4632 bohr): the SCF loop converging")
A, B = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0]), np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.4632])
E, eps = scf([(STO3G_He, A), (STO3G_H, B)], [(2.0, A), (1.0, B)], 2, verbose=True)
print(f"   total energy {E:.6f} hartree; orbital energies {np.round(eps, 4)}")
```

The minimal STO-3G basis gives −1.11671 hartree for H₂, the larger 6-31G basis −1.12674, and an infinitely large basis would give −1.13363, the **Hartree–Fock limit**. But the exact energy is −1.17447. The remaining 0.041 hartree, 107 kJ/mol, is the **correlation energy**: what the average-field picture misses because real electrons dodge each other instantaneously. No basis set, however large, can recover it within Hartree–Fock. The SCF loop for HeH⁺ shows the self-consistency at work: the first iteration ignores the repulsion between the electrons altogether (energy −5.20 hartree), and the energy then settles, to ten decimal places, within fourteen iterations.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why does an average field miss so much? Because electrons are not smeared out; each one is a point that the others feel and avoid. Two electrons in the same orbital of H₂ are, in Hartree–Fock, as likely to be on the same atom as on different ones, which means the H₂ molecule is half ionic, H⁺H⁻, at every bond length. That is harmless near equilibrium and disastrous when the bond is stretched, where Hartree–Fock wrongly predicts that H₂ falls apart into ions half of the time.

</Narrator>

**Beyond Hartree–Fock.** Methods that recover correlation start from the Hartree–Fock determinant and mix in excited determinants. Møller–Plesset perturbation theory (MP2) adds a second-order correction cheaply. Coupled-cluster theory, especially **CCSD(T)**, which includes single and double excitations fully and triple excitations by perturbation, reaches chemical accuracy for most well-behaved molecules and is called the "gold standard" of the field. Full configuration interaction (FCI), which mixes in *every* determinant, is the exact answer in a given basis, and its cost is the subject of the next section but one.

</Section>

<Section id="basis-sets" kind="human" title="Basis sets">

The orbitals of the hydrogen atom decay as $e^{-\zeta r}$, Slater-type functions. Integrals over such functions on several centres are very hard to compute. In 1950 Frank Boys proposed using **Gaussian functions**, $e^{-\alpha r^2}$, instead: a product of two Gaussians on different centres is a single Gaussian on a point between them, so every integral reduces to a closed formula, like the ones in the program. Gaussians have the wrong shape (no cusp at the nucleus, too fast a decay far away), so a basis function is a fixed **contraction** of several of them.

- **Minimal** basis sets have one function per atomic orbital. STO-3G (Warren Hehre, Robert Stewart and John Pople, 1969) fits each Slater orbital with three Gaussians.
- **Split-valence** sets such as 6-31G give each valence orbital two functions, an inner and an outer one, so orbitals can shrink or swell in a molecule.
- **Polarisation** functions (d on carbon, p on hydrogen; the "*" in 6-31G*) let orbitals bend, which bonds need. **Diffuse** functions ("+") describe anions and weak interactions.
- The **correlation-consistent** sets of Thom Dunning (1989), cc-pVDZ, cc-pVTZ, cc-pVQZ and so on, add functions in a systematic order, so correlated energies converge smoothly and can be extrapolated to the complete-basis limit.

Every standard set is available from the [Basis Set Exchange](https://www.basissetexchange.org/). Calculations on crystals usually use plane waves instead, as in [density functional theory](/en/materials/density-functional-theory/) in Lumenary Materials.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Two different errors hide in every calculation, and they do not cancel in any reliable way: the *method* error (Hartree–Fock instead of exact) and the *basis* error (6-31G instead of complete). A bigger basis removes only the second. So a result is meaningful only when both are named, as in "CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ" or "B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVP", and a claim that a method "works" holds only for the basis and the kind of molecule it was tested on.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="cost" kind="computation" title="What exactness costs">

Full configuration interaction is exact in its basis, so why not always use it? Count the determinants it needs: every way of placing the spin-up electrons in the orbitals, times every way of placing the spin-down ones. Then look at how the approximate methods' costs grow with the number of basis functions $N$.

```python run
from math import comb, log10

# Full configuration interaction (the exact answer in a given basis) uses every way of placing
# the alpha electrons and the beta electrons in the orbitals.
def n_determinants(n_orbitals, n_alpha, n_beta):
    return comb(n_orbitals, n_alpha) * comb(n_orbitals, n_beta)

print("molecule, basis           basis functions   electrons   determinants in full CI")
for name, n_bf, n_el in [("H2, 6-31G", 4, 2), ("H2O, cc-pVDZ", 24, 10), ("N2, cc-pVDZ", 28, 14),
                         ("benzene, cc-pVDZ", 114, 42)]:
    d = n_determinants(n_bf, n_el // 2, n_el // 2)
    size = f"{d:,}" if d < 1e6 else f"about 10^{log10(d):.1f}"
    print(f"{name:26s} {n_bf:10d} {n_el:12d}        {size}")

# Approximate methods trade accuracy for a cost that grows as a power of the basis size N.
methods = [("force field", 2), ("DFT (hybrid)", 4), ("Hartree-Fock", 4), ("MP2", 5), ("CCSD", 6), ("CCSD(T)", 7)]
print("\nformal scaling: cost multiplies by ... when the molecule is made")
print("method           N^k     2x bigger     10x bigger")
for name, k in methods:
    print(f"{name:15s}  N^{k}   {2**k:10,d}   {10**k:12,d}")

# Two-electron integrals (pq|rs): about N^4/8 distinct ones, 8 bytes each
for n_bf in (114, 500, 2000):
    n_int = n_bf**4 / 8
    print(f"{n_bf:5d} basis functions: {n_int:.1e} two-electron integrals, {n_int * 8 / 1e9:,.1f} GB to store them all")
```

Full CI on water in a modest basis already needs about two billion determinants; on benzene, $10^{45}$, more than any computer will ever hold. This exponential wall is why every practical method is an approximation, and why chemistry is one of the problems that motivate quantum computers (<LessonLink slug="quantum-computing-for-chemistry">Quantum computing for chemistry</LessonLink>). The polynomial methods are affordable but still steep: making a molecule twice as large multiplies the cost of CCSD(T) by 128, so it is routinely used on molecules of a few dozen atoms, while density functional theory handles hundreds. Even storing the two-electron integrals becomes impossible beyond a few hundred basis functions, so modern programs recompute them on the fly or approximate them by **density fitting**, and "local" correlation methods such as DLPNO-CCSD(T) exploit the fact that distant electrons barely correlate, bringing near-CCSD(T) accuracy to molecules of hundreds of atoms.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Put the H₂ numbers next to the target. The correlation energy of one bond, 107 kJ/mol, is twenty-five times chemical accuracy. What saves Hartree–Fock-based thinking is that most of the correlation energy is the same on both sides of many reactions; what makes CCSD(T) valuable is that it recovers the rest. Always ask how much of an energy difference is left after the errors cancel, and how that was checked.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="dft" kind="definitions" title="Density functional theory">

A wavefunction for $n$ electrons depends on $3n$ coordinates; the electron density $\rho(\mathbf r)$ depends on only three. In 1964 Pierre Hohenberg and Walter Kohn proved that the ground-state density determines everything about the ground state, including its energy.

<Theorem name="Hohenberg–Kohn">

The ground-state electron density of a system of electrons in an external potential (the field of the nuclei) determines that potential up to a constant, and hence the Hamiltonian and every ground-state property. There is a universal functional $E[\rho]$ whose minimum over densities is the ground-state energy.

</Theorem>

The theorem says the functional exists, not what it is. In 1965 Kohn and Lu Jeu Sham made it usable: they imagined a system of *non-interacting* electrons with the same density as the real one, whose kinetic energy can be computed exactly from orbitals, and they swept everything unknown into one term, the **exchange–correlation energy** $E_\text{xc}[\rho]$. The resulting Kohn–Sham equations look like Hartree–Fock and are solved by the same SCF loop, at similar or lower cost, but if $E_\text{xc}$ were exact they would give the exact energy.

$E_\text{xc}$ is not known exactly, and **density functional approximations** form a hierarchy that John Perdew called **Jacob's ladder**, each rung using more information:

| Rung | Uses | Examples | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LDA | density | SVWN | solids, historically |
| GGA | + density gradient | PBE, BLYP | solids, large systems |
| meta-GGA | + kinetic-energy density | SCAN, r²SCAN, TPSS | solids and molecules |
| hybrid | + some exact (Hartree–Fock) exchange | B3LYP, PBE0 | general molecular chemistry |
| range-separated, double hybrid | + exchange by distance, + MP2-like correlation | ωB97X-V, ωB97M-V, DSD-PBEP86 | accurate thermochemistry |

Two corrections and two cautions matter in practice. Most functionals miss **dispersion**, the weak attraction between all molecules, and are combined with an empirical correction such as Stefan Grimme's D3 or D4; without it, a DFT calculation may predict that two stacked benzene rings repel. And approximate functionals suffer **self-interaction** (or delocalisation) error, in which an electron partly repels itself, so they spread charge too widely, underestimate reaction barriers and mis-describe stretched bonds. Systems with several near-degenerate electronic configurations, such as many transition-metal complexes and molecules with breaking bonds, are hard for every single-determinant method, Hartree–Fock and DFT alike, and need **multireference** methods. Benchmarks such as [GMTKN55](https://doi.org/10.1039/C7CP04913G), with about 1500 reference energies, are how the field decides which functional to trust for what.

</Section>

<Section id="force-fields" kind="computation" title="Force fields and molecular mechanics">

For a protein of ten thousand atoms in water, even DFT is too expensive for anything but a snapshot. **Molecular mechanics** gives up on electrons altogether: atoms are balls, bonds are springs, and the energy is a sum of simple terms with parameters fitted to experiments and to quantum-chemical calculations.

Norman Allinger's MM2 (1977) made molecular mechanics a standard tool for organic conformations; AMBER, CHARMM, OPLS and GROMOS made it the basis of biomolecular simulation. Here is a small force field for cyclohexane, treating each CH₂ group as one particle (a *united-atom* model), with bond, angle and torsion terms of the size used in united-atom models of alkanes. The program minimises the energy from several starting shapes with a quasi-Newton (BFGS) method, using gradients by finite differences (<TopicLink id="math.applied.optimisation">Optimisation</TopicLink>).

```python run
import numpy as np

# A united-atom force field for cyclohexane: each CH2 group is one particle.
KCAL = 4.184                                  # kJ per kcal
r0, kb = 1.526, 260 * KCAL                    # bond: E = kb (r - r0)^2, kJ/mol/A^2
th0, kth = np.radians(112.4), 63 * KCAL       # angle: E = kth (theta - th0)^2, kJ/mol/rad^2
V1, V2, V3 = 1.411 * KCAL, -0.271 * KCAL, 3.145 * KCAL   # torsion Fourier terms, kJ/mol

def dihedral(p0, p1, p2, p3):
    b0, b1, b2 = p0 - p1, p2 - p1, p3 - p2
    b1 = b1 / np.linalg.norm(b1)
    v, w = b0 - np.dot(b0, b1) * b1, b2 - np.dot(b2, b1) * b1
    return np.arctan2(np.dot(np.cross(b1, v), w), np.dot(v, w))

def energy(flat, parts=False):
    x = flat.reshape(6, 3)
    e_b = e_a = e_t = 0.0
    for i in range(6):
        a, b, c, d = x[i], x[(i + 1) % 6], x[(i + 2) % 6], x[(i + 3) % 6]
        e_b += kb * (np.linalg.norm(b - a) - r0) ** 2
        u, v = a - b, c - b
        th = np.arccos(np.dot(u, v) / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v)))
        e_a += kth * (th - th0) ** 2
        phi = dihedral(a, b, c, d)                         # 180 degrees = anti
        e_t += 0.5 * (V1 * (1 + np.cos(phi)) + V2 * (1 - np.cos(2 * phi)) + V3 * (1 + np.cos(3 * phi)))
    return (e_b, e_a, e_t) if parts else e_b + e_a + e_t

def gradient(flat, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(flat)
    for i in range(len(flat)):
        d = np.zeros_like(flat); d[i] = h
        g[i] = (energy(flat + d) - energy(flat - d)) / (2 * h)
    return g

def minimise(flat, steps=500):
    """Quasi-Newton (BFGS) minimisation with a backtracking line search."""
    Hinv = np.eye(len(flat)) * 1e-3
    g = gradient(flat)
    for it in range(steps):
        p = -Hinv @ g
        t, e0 = 1.0, energy(flat)
        while energy(flat + t * p) > e0 + 1e-4 * t * (g @ p) and t > 1e-10:
            t *= 0.5
        new = flat + t * p
        g_new = gradient(new)
        s, y = new - flat, g_new - g
        if s @ y > 1e-12:
            rho = 1 / (s @ y)
            I = np.eye(len(flat))
            Hinv = (I - rho * np.outer(s, y)) @ Hinv @ (I - rho * np.outer(y, s)) + rho * np.outer(s, s)
        flat, g = new, g_new
        if np.max(np.abs(g)) < 1e-4:
            break
    return flat, it + 1

def describe(flat):
    x = flat.reshape(6, 3)
    angles = [np.degrees(np.arccos(np.dot(x[i] - x[(i + 1) % 6], x[(i + 2) % 6] - x[(i + 1) % 6]) /
              (np.linalg.norm(x[i] - x[(i + 1) % 6]) * np.linalg.norm(x[(i + 2) % 6] - x[(i + 1) % 6]))))
              for i in range(6)]
    tors = [np.degrees(dihedral(x[i], x[(i + 1) % 6], x[(i + 2) % 6], x[(i + 3) % 6])) for i in range(6)]
    return np.mean(angles), tors

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
ring = np.array([[1.53 * np.cos(k * np.pi / 3), 1.53 * np.sin(k * np.pi / 3), 0.0] for k in range(6)])
starts = {
    "flat ring, slightly jiggled": ring + rng.normal(0, 0.05, ring.shape),
    "boat, exactly symmetric": ring + np.array([[0, 0, z] for z in (0.5, 0, 0, 0.5, 0, 0)]),
    "boat, slightly jiggled": ring + np.array([[0, 0, z] for z in (0.5, 0, 0, 0.5, 0, 0)])
                              + rng.normal(0, 0.05, ring.shape),
}
results = {}
print("start                         E/kJ mol^-1   bonds  angles torsions   mean CCC angle   torsions (degrees)")
flat_e = energy(ring.ravel(), parts=True)
print(f"{'perfectly flat hexagon':28s} {sum(flat_e):10.1f}   {flat_e[0]:5.1f} {flat_e[1]:6.1f} {flat_e[2]:7.1f}")
for name, x0 in starts.items():
    x, n = minimise(x0.ravel())
    e = energy(x, parts=True)
    ang, tors = describe(x)
    results[name] = sum(e)
    print(f"{name:28s} {sum(e):10.1f}   {e[0]:5.1f} {e[1]:6.1f} {e[2]:7.1f}   {ang:10.1f}       "
          + " ".join(f"{t:+5.0f}" for t in tors) + f"   ({n} steps)")
chair = results["flat ring, slightly jiggled"]
for name in ("boat, exactly symmetric", "boat, slightly jiggled"):
    print(f"{name}: {results[name] - chair:.1f} kJ/mol above the chair")
```

A flat hexagon costs 142 kJ/mol, mostly from six fully eclipsed bonds and from angles of 120° instead of 112°. Given only a small random nudge, the minimiser folds it into the **chair**: torsions alternating ±57°, C–C–C angles of 110.6°, close to the measured geometry of cyclohexane (<LessonLink slug="stereochemistry">Stereochemistry</LessonLink>). Its energy falls from 142 to 24.5 kJ/mol, and almost all of what remains is in the six gauche-like torsions that a six-membered ring cannot avoid. Started from an exactly symmetric boat, it stays a boat, because a symmetric gradient cannot break the symmetry: the boat is a saddle point, not a minimum. A nudged boat relaxes to the **twist-boat**, with torsions of about +32° and −67°, a true local minimum 28.6 kJ/mol above the chair. Spectroscopic measurements ([Squillacote and colleagues, 1975](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja00844a068)) put it roughly 20 to 23 kJ/mol above the chair: the right shape and the right order, but too high by several kJ/mol.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Before trusting that 28.6, ask under what conditions the force field was built to work. This one has no hydrogen atoms, so it cannot see the flagpole hydrogens that make a real boat higher than a twist-boat by several kJ/mol; its parameters were fitted to open-chain alkanes, not rings; and, like every classical force field, it has no electrons, so it cannot break or form a bond. Force fields are excellent within the chemistry they were parameterised on and silent or wrong outside it.

</Narrator>

When a reaction must happen inside a large system, such as an enzyme's active site, chemists combine the two levels: quantum mechanics for the few dozen atoms that react, molecular mechanics for the rest. These **QM/MM** methods, introduced by Warshel and Levitt in 1976, were the core of the 2013 Nobel Prize.

</Section>

<Section id="choosing" kind="human" title="Choosing a method">

A computational chemist chooses by asking what accuracy the question needs and what the system size allows. As of 2026, a common pattern for an organic or organometallic molecule of up to about a hundred atoms is:

| Task | Typical method | Typical error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| search thousands of conformers | force field or a semiempirical method such as GFN2-xTB | rough ranking only |
| optimise geometries, compute frequencies | a hybrid or meta-GGA functional with dispersion correction, triple-zeta basis | bond lengths about 1 pm |
| final reaction and barrier energies | DLPNO-CCSD(T) or a double-hybrid functional, large basis | 4–8 kJ/mol |
| benchmark a few small molecules | CCSD(T) extrapolated to the complete basis | about 1–2 kJ/mol |
| proteins, liquids, materials over nanoseconds | force field molecular dynamics, or QM/MM where bonds change | depends on parameters |

The main programs differ in licensing and emphasis. [Gaussian](https://gaussian.com/), descended from John Pople's group, is the longest-established commercial program. [ORCA](https://www.faccts.de/orca/), from Frank Neese's group at the Max Planck Institute for Coal Research in Germany, is free for academic use and strong in local coupled-cluster and spectroscopy. [Psi4](https://psicode.org/) and [PySCF](https://pyscf.org/) are open source and driven from Python, which makes them favourites for method development and teaching; Q-Chem, Molpro, TURBOMOLE, NWChem and, for solids, CP2K and VASP fill other niches. A first PySCF calculation looks like this (not run here: PySCF is not available in the browser):

```python
# Not run here: needs PySCF installed (pip install pyscf).

hf = scf.RHF(mol).run()              # Hartree-Fock energy, in hartree
ks = dft.RKS(mol)
ks.xc = "b3lyp"                      # a hybrid functional
ks.run()
```

Whatever the program, good practice is the same: state the method and basis, check that geometries are true minima (no imaginary frequencies) or true transition states (exactly one), test the sensitivity of the conclusion to the method, and compare with experiment wherever it exists, for example with the reference data in NIST's [CCCBDB](https://cccbdb.nist.gov/).

**Machine-learned potentials.** The newest tool sits between DFT and force fields. A neural network is trained on thousands to millions of DFT or coupled-cluster energies and forces, and then predicts them for new structures nearly as accurately, at a cost closer to a force field's, while still allowing bonds to break. Jörg Behler and Michele Parrinello's [2007 paper](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.98.146401) started the approach; by 2026, potentials trained on very large public datasets are used routinely for molecular dynamics of reactive systems. How they work and where they fail is a subject of <LessonLink slug="ai-for-chemistry">AI for chemistry</LessonLink>.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A calculation that converged is a correct one."** Convergence means the SCF loop or the optimiser stopped changing. The answer can still be wrong because of the method, the basis, a missing dispersion correction, a wrong spin state or a structure that is a saddle point rather than a minimum.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"DFT is a first-principles method, so it has no empirical content."** The Hohenberg–Kohn and Kohn–Sham theory is exact, but every functional in use is an approximation, and many contain parameters fitted to data. Different functionals can disagree by tens of kJ/mol on the same reaction.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bigger basis set always gives a better answer."** It gives a better answer *for that method*. Hartree–Fock in a huge basis still misses the whole correlation energy, and a correlated method in a small basis can be closer to experiment than in a larger one by a lucky cancellation that disappears when the basis improves.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Total energies are the result."** A total energy of −76.4 hartree for water is meaningless on its own. Chemistry lies in differences between total energies computed in the same way: reaction energies, barriers, conformer energies.

</Misconception>
