<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Which way does the world run?">

Leave a glass of iced water on a summer table and the ice melts. It never happens the other way: no glass of lukewarm water has ever been seen to freeze into ice cubes while warming the air around it. A drop of food colouring in still water slowly spreads until the whole glass is tinted, and never gathers itself back into a drop. Iron rusts, but rust never turns back into shiny iron on its own.

The <LessonLink slug="thermochemistry">previous lesson</LessonLink> showed that energy is conserved, and that all of these could happen in reverse without breaking the first law. Melting ice even *absorbs* energy: it is endothermic, yet it happens by itself. So energy is not what decides the direction of change. Something else is, and its name is **entropy**.

This lesson builds entropy twice, from two directions that turn out to agree: by *counting* the ways a system can share out its energy, and by *measuring* heat and temperature. Then it combines entropy with enthalpy into the **Gibbs energy**, the single number chemists use to say whether a reaction can go, and at what temperature a reaction that cannot go becomes one that can.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Counting the ways">

Picture energy as coming in small, identical packets (quanta), shared among the atoms of a solid. Each atom can hold any number of packets. A **microstate** is one particular way of sharing them: atom 1 has three, atom 2 has none, and so on. A **macrostate** is what we can actually observe, such as "solid A holds six packets and solid B holds four", which can be realised by many different microstates.

The single assumption behind everything that follows is that, left alone, a system wanders among all its microstates with equal likelihood. Then the macrostate we observe is simply the one with the most microstates, because it is overwhelmingly the most probable. Energy "flows from hot to cold" because there are vastly more ways to share it evenly than unevenly, and the food colouring spreads for the same reason: far more arrangements have the dye spread out than have it gathered in one spot.

<Analogy>

Shuffle a new pack of cards, which comes sorted by suit and rank. There is exactly one sorted order and about $8 \times 10^{67}$ orders in all, so after a good shuffle you will never see the sorted order again, though no law forbids it. Nothing pushes the cards towards disorder; there are simply overwhelmingly more mixed-up orders than sorted ones. Molecules shuffle themselves trillions of times a second.

</Analogy>

You can watch this kind of spreading safely in the kitchen with an adult: put one drop of food colouring into a glass of still water without stirring, and another into a glass of warm water, and compare them every few minutes. The warm glass spreads faster (molecules move faster), but both end in the same place.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Entropy, two ways">

<Definition term="spontaneous (thermodynamically favourable)">

A process is **spontaneous** if, once started, it can proceed without continuous outside intervention. The word says nothing about speed: diamond turning into graphite is spontaneous at room temperature and pressure, but it is so slow that diamonds last for billions of years. Many chemists now prefer the phrase **thermodynamically favourable** to avoid the everyday meaning of "spontaneous".

</Definition>

<Definition term="statistical entropy">

If a system in a given macrostate can be in any of $W$ microstates, all equally likely, its **entropy** is

$$S = k_\text{B} \ln W,$$

where $k_\text{B} = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K is the Boltzmann constant (exact since the 2019 redefinition of the SI). Per mole, $k_\text{B}$ becomes the gas constant $R = N_\text{A} k_\text{B} = 8.314$ J/(mol K).

</Definition>

The logarithm is what makes entropy add up: two independent systems have $W_1 W_2$ microstates together, so their entropies add, $k_\text{B}\ln(W_1W_2) = k_\text{B}\ln W_1 + k_\text{B}\ln W_2$. The formula is carved on Ludwig Boltzmann's tombstone in Vienna.

<Definition term="thermodynamic entropy">

Rudolf Clausius defined entropy (and coined the word, in 1865) from heat. When a system absorbs a small amount of heat $\delta q_\text{rev}$ *reversibly* (slowly, through a sequence of equilibrium states) at temperature $T$, its entropy changes by

$$dS = \frac{\delta q_\text{rev}}{T}.$$

Entropy is a state function, so for an irreversible process we compute $\Delta S$ along any reversible path between the same two states.

</Definition>

<Theorem name="The second law of thermodynamics">

In any process, the total entropy of the system and its surroundings (the "universe") does not decrease:

$$\Delta S_\text{univ} = \Delta S_\text{sys} + \Delta S_\text{surr} \ge 0,$$

with equality only for a reversible process. A process with $\Delta S_\text{univ} \lt 0$ does not happen.

</Theorem>

<Theorem name="The third law of thermodynamics">

The entropy of a perfect crystal of a pure substance approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero.

</Theorem>

The third law, which grew out of Walther Nernst's heat theorem of 1906, makes *absolute* entropies possible. A perfect crystal at 0 K has exactly one microstate, so $S = k_\text{B} \ln 1 = 0$. Measure $C_p$ from near 0 K upwards, add $\int C_p/T\,dT$ and $\Delta H/T$ for every phase change on the way, and you have $S^\circ$ at 298 K. That is why tables list absolute entropies $S^\circ$, but only *differences* of enthalpy.

</Section>

<Section id="counting" kind="computation" title="Counting microstates in Python">

The simplest model of a solid, due to Einstein (1907), treats it as $N$ identical oscillators sharing $q$ energy quanta. The number of ways to do that is a classic counting problem (stars and bars, from <TopicLink id="math.discrete.combinatorics">combinatorics</TopicLink>): arrange $q$ identical quanta and $N - 1$ dividers in a row, so

$$W(N, q) = \binom{q + N - 1}{q}.$$

Put two such solids, A and B, in contact, so that quanta can move between them, and ask how the energy ends up shared.

```python run
from math import comb, exp, lgamma

def omega(N, q):
    """Ways to share q identical energy quanta among N oscillators."""
    return comb(q + N - 1, q)

# Two small solids in contact share q = 10 quanta: A has 6 oscillators, B has 4.
NA, NB, q = 6, 4, 10
total = omega(NA + NB, q)
print(f"{'qA':>3} {'Ω_A':>6} {'Ω_B':>6} {'Ω_A·Ω_B':>9} {'probability':>11}")
for qA in range(q + 1):
    w = omega(NA, qA) * omega(NB, q - qA)
    print(f"{qA:3d} {omega(NA, qA):6d} {omega(NB, q - qA):6d} {w:9d} {w / total:11.3f}  " + "#" * round(60 * w / total))
print("total microstates:", total)

# Bigger solids: work with ln Ω (the numbers have thousands of digits).
def ln_omega(N, q):
    return lgamma(q + N) - lgamma(q + 1) - lgamma(N)

NA, NB, q = 3000, 2000, 5000
lnw = [ln_omega(NA, k) + ln_omega(NB, q - k) for k in range(q + 1)]
peak = max(lnw)
w = [exp(x - peak) for x in lnw]                  # relative weights, largest = 1
best = lnw.index(peak)
near = sum(w[best - 100: best + 101]) / sum(w)
print(f"\nN_A = {NA}, N_B = {NB}, q = {q}: most probable q_A = {best}")
print(f"probability that q_A is within ±100 of it: {near:.4f}")
print(f"Ω(q_A = {best}) / Ω(q_A = {q // 2}) = e^{peak - lnw[q // 2]:.1f}")
```

With only ten oscillators the most probable split, six quanta in A and four in B, has probability 0.175, and every other split is quite likely too: small systems fluctuate. With 5000 oscillators the energy almost certainly settles at 3000 quanta in A, in proportion to the number of oscillators, so that each oscillator holds one quantum on average in both solids. A split that looks only a little different, 2500 each, is $e^{50.6} \approx 10^{22}$ times less likely.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Run the second half again with ten times as many oscillators and quanta, and the width of the peak shrinks relative to its position by a factor of about $\sqrt{10}$. For a mole of atoms, about $6 \times 10^{23}$, the relative fluctuations are around one part in $10^{12}$, far below anything a thermometer can see. That is why the "most probable" macrostate of a large system behaves like a law.

</Narrator>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why does the most probable split give both solids the same *temperature*? At the peak, moving one quantum from A to B changes $\ln W_A + \ln W_B$ by zero, so $\partial \ln W_A / \partial U_A = \partial \ln W_B / \partial U_B$. That shared quantity is what temperature measures: statistical mechanics *defines* $1/T = \partial S/\partial U$, and "heat flows until temperatures are equal" becomes "energy spreads until entropy is maximal".

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="measuring" kind="examples" title="Entropy from heat, and the second law at work">

For the thermodynamic route we need only Clausius's definition. Heating at constant pressure gives $\delta q_\text{rev} = C_p\,dT$, so $\Delta S = \int C_p\,dT/T = C_p \ln(T_2/T_1)$ when $C_p$ is constant. A phase change at its equilibrium temperature is reversible, with $q_\text{rev} = \Delta H$, so $\Delta S = \Delta H / T$.

```python run
from math import log

# 1. Entropy from heat: one mole of water taken from ice at 0 °C to steam at 100 °C.
dH_fus, T_fus = 6010.0, 273.15            # J/mol, K
Cp_liquid = 75.3                          # J/(mol K), roughly constant
dH_vap, T_vap = 40660.0, 373.15           # J/mol, K
steps = [("melt the ice", dH_fus / T_fus),
         ("heat the liquid", Cp_liquid * log(T_vap / T_fus)),
         ("boil the water", dH_vap / T_vap)]
for name, dS in steps:
    print(f"{name:16s} ΔS = {dS:6.1f} J/(mol K)")
print(f"{'total':16s} ΔS = {sum(dS for _, dS in steps):6.1f} J/(mol K)")

# Trouton's rule: ΔvapS is about 85-88 J/(mol K) for many liquids, but not for water.
print(f"\nbenzene: ΔvapS = {30720 / 353.24:.1f} J/(mol K);  water: {dH_vap / T_vap:.1f} J/(mol K)")

# 2. The second law decides: does water freeze at -10 °C, and at +10 °C?
for T in (263.15, 273.15, 283.15):
    dS_sys = -dH_fus / T_fus              # freezing (approximation: ignores the small Cp difference)
    dS_surr = +dH_fus / T                 # the released heat warms surroundings at temperature T
    total = dS_sys + dS_surr
    verdict = "freezes" if total > 1e-9 else ("equilibrium" if abs(total) < 1e-9 else "does not freeze")
    print(f"T = {T:6.2f} K: ΔS_sys = {dS_sys:6.2f}, ΔS_surr = {dS_surr:+6.2f}, ΔS_univ = {total:+5.2f} J/(mol K) -> {verdict}")
```

Boiling contributes far more entropy than melting (109.0 against 22.0 J/(mol K)): a gas has vastly more room, and so more microstates, than a liquid. Many liquids have an entropy of vaporisation near 85 to 88 J/(mol K) (Trouton's rule; benzene gives 87.0), but water's is larger, 109.0, because hydrogen bonds hold liquid water in a more ordered arrangement than most liquids, so it gains more entropy when it escapes.

The second half explains the ice on the table. Freezing lowers the water's entropy by 22.0 J/(mol K), but the heat it releases raises the entropy of the surroundings by $\Delta H/T$, which is larger when the surroundings are colder. At −10 °C the surroundings gain more than the water loses, so the water freezes; at +10 °C they gain less, so it does not, and ice melts instead. At exactly 0 °C the two balance: that *is* the melting point.

<Example title="The surroundings' share">

At constant temperature and pressure, the heat released by the system, $-\Delta H_\text{sys}$, flows into surroundings so large that their temperature hardly changes, and the transfer is effectively reversible for them. So $\Delta S_\text{surr} = -\Delta H_\text{sys}/T$. For ammonia synthesis at 298 K, $\Delta_\text{r}H^\circ = -91.8$ kJ/mol warms the surroundings and gives them $+91\,800/298.15 = +307.9$ J/(mol K), while the system loses $198.1$ J/(mol K) (four moles of gas become two). The total is positive, so the reaction is favourable at 298 K. At 600 K the surroundings would gain only 153 J/(mol K), less than the system loses, and it would not be.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="gibbs" kind="human" title="The Gibbs energy">

Keeping track of the surroundings every time is tedious. Josiah Willard Gibbs, working at Yale in the 1870s, showed how to fold them into a property of the system alone. At constant temperature and pressure, $\Delta S_\text{univ} = \Delta S_\text{sys} - \Delta H_\text{sys}/T$. Multiply by $-T$:

$$-T\,\Delta S_\text{univ} = \Delta H - T\,\Delta S \equiv \Delta G.$$

<Definition term="Gibbs energy">

The **Gibbs energy** (Gibbs free energy) of a system is $G = H - TS$. At constant temperature and pressure, a process is favourable if $\Delta G \lt 0$, at equilibrium if $\Delta G = 0$, and unfavourable if $\Delta G > 0$ (the reverse process is then favourable). $-\Delta G$ is also the largest amount of non-expansion work, such as electrical work, the process can deliver.

</Definition>

<Narrator who="gideon">

Every word of "at constant temperature and pressure" matters. The criterion $\Delta G \lt 0$ comes from $\Delta S_\text{univ} > 0$ only when $T$ and $p$ are fixed and no work other than expansion is exchanged; at constant volume the right function is the Helmholtz energy $A = U - TS$. And a negative $\Delta G$ means *allowed*, not *fast*: diamond's $\Delta G$ for turning into graphite is −2.9 kJ/mol, and engagement rings are safe.

</Narrator>

Because $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, the signs of $\Delta H$ and $\Delta S$ sort reactions into four kinds:

| $\Delta H$ | $\Delta S$ | Favourable | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| negative | positive | at every temperature | oxidising glucose to CO₂ and water |
| positive | negative | at no temperature | making ozone from oxygen |
| negative | negative | at low temperature | freezing, ammonia synthesis |
| positive | positive | at high temperature | melting, boiling, decomposing limestone |

In the last two rows there is a crossover temperature $T^* = \Delta H / \Delta S$ where $\Delta G = 0$. If $\Delta H^\circ$ and $\Delta S^\circ$ change little with temperature (as Kirchhoff's law suggests over moderate ranges), we can estimate $T^*$ from 298 K tables.

```python run
# Standard data at 298.15 K, 1 bar (rounded; NIST Chemistry WebBook, NIST-JANAF).
#            ΔfH° kJ/mol   S° J/(mol K)
data = {
    "N2(g)":      (0.0,     191.6),
    "H2(g)":      (0.0,     130.7),
    "O2(g)":      (0.0,     205.2),
    "NH3(g)":     (-45.9,   192.8),
    "CaCO3(s)":   (-1206.9,  92.9),
    "CaO(s)":     (-635.1,   39.8),
    "CO2(g)":     (-393.5,  213.8),
    "H2O(l)":     (-285.8,   69.9),
    "H2O(g)":     (-241.8,  188.8),
    "C(graphite)": (0.0,      5.7),
    "C(diamond)":  (1.9,      2.4),
}

def dH_dS(reaction):
    dH = sum(nu * data[s][0] for s, nu in reaction.items())          # kJ/mol
    dS = sum(nu * data[s][1] for s, nu in reaction.items()) / 1000   # kJ/(mol K)
    return dH, dS

reactions = {
    "N2 + 3 H2 -> 2 NH3":   {"N2(g)": -1, "H2(g)": -3, "NH3(g)": 2},
    "CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2":   {"CaCO3(s)": -1, "CaO(s)": 1, "CO2(g)": 1},
    "H2O(l) -> H2O(g)":     {"H2O(l)": -1, "H2O(g)": 1},
    "diamond -> graphite":  {"C(diamond)": -1, "C(graphite)": 1},
}
print(f"{'reaction':22s} {'ΔH°':>7} {'ΔS°':>7} {'ΔG°(298)':>9} {'ΔG°(1000)':>10}  ΔG° changes sign at")
for name, r in reactions.items():
    dH, dS = dH_dS(r)
    G = lambda T: dH - T * dS                      # assumes ΔH°, ΔS° independent of T
    cross = f"{dH / dS:6.0f} K" if dH * dS > 0 else "never"
    print(f"{name:22s} {dH:7.1f} {1000 * dS:7.1f} {G(298.15):9.1f} {G(1000):10.1f}  {cross}")

# Coupling in the cell (standard biochemical values at pH 7, kJ/mol):
glucose_phosphorylation = +13.8     # glucose + phosphate -> glucose 6-phosphate + H2O
atp_hydrolysis = -30.5              # ATP + H2O -> ADP + phosphate
print(f"\ncoupled: glucose + ATP -> glucose 6-phosphate + ADP: ΔG°' = {glucose_phosphorylation + atp_hydrolysis:+.1f} kJ/mol")
```

The estimates are good. Boiling water should become favourable at 373 K; from room-temperature data alone we get 370 K. Limestone (calcium carbonate) should start to give off CO₂ at 1 bar above about 1110 K, roughly 840 °C, which is why lime kilns run at around 900 °C and above. Ammonia synthesis is favourable only below about 463 K, yet industrial plants run near 700 K because at lower temperatures the reaction is far too slow: a tension resolved in <LessonLink slug="chemical-equilibrium">Chemical equilibrium</LessonLink>.

<Section id="coupling" kind="examples" title="Coupled reactions: how life pays its bills">

Living cells build proteins, DNA and sugars, all of which have higher Gibbs energy than the small molecules they come from. They do it by **coupling**: pairing an unfavourable reaction with a strongly favourable one through a shared intermediate, so that the *sum* has $\Delta G \lt 0$. The most common partner is the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate, ATP, to adenosine diphosphate, ADP, and phosphate.

<Example title="Phosphorylating glucose">

The first step in using glucose is to attach a phosphate group to it. On its own, glucose + phosphate → glucose 6-phosphate + water has a standard biochemical Gibbs energy $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = +13.8$ kJ/mol (the prime means standard conditions at pH 7), so it is unfavourable. The enzyme hexokinase instead transfers the phosphate directly from ATP: glucose + ATP → glucose 6-phosphate + ADP. Because $G$ is a state function, its $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ is the sum of the two steps, $13.8 + (-30.5) = -16.7$ kJ/mol, as the last line of the block above shows. The enzyme matters because it couples the two steps *physically*: it never lets ATP hydrolyse uselessly without the glucose present.

</Example>

Inside a real cell, ATP is kept far from equilibrium, with much more ATP than ADP, which makes its hydrolysis even more favourable, roughly −50 kJ/mol rather than the standard −30.5. How concentrations change $\Delta G$ is the subject of the <LessonLink slug="chemical-equilibrium">next lesson</LessonLink>, and the cell's whole energy economy, in which a person turns over roughly their own body mass of ATP every day, is in <LessonLink slug="biochemistry">Biochemistry</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="third-law" kind="human" title="Absolute entropies, residual entropy and modern practice">

Absolute entropies are measured by low-temperature calorimetry: cool a sample towards absolute zero, measure its heat capacity as it warms, and integrate $C_p/T$. William Giauque, who won the 1949 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for such work, noticed that for a few substances the calorimetric entropy came out *smaller* than the entropy computed from spectroscopy. The missing entropy is **residual entropy**: the crystal never reached a single microstate as it cooled. In solid carbon monoxide, the nearly symmetric molecules can freeze in pointing either way, CO or OC, giving up to $R\ln 2 = 5.76$ J/(mol K). In ice, each oxygen has two near and two far hydrogens in many possible arrangements; Linus Pauling's 1935 counting argument gave $R \ln \tfrac{3}{2} = 3.37$ J/(mol K), very close to the measured value. The third law is not violated; those crystals are not perfect.

Today, standard entropies of gases are usually computed from molecular data (masses, bond lengths, vibration frequencies) by statistical thermodynamics, which turns the counting of this lesson into a precise tool (<LessonLink slug="statistical-thermodynamics">Statistical thermodynamics</LessonLink>). In drug discovery, computers estimate how strongly a candidate molecule binds to a protein by computing a *free-energy difference* with molecular simulation, gradually "transforming" one molecule into another in the computer. Open-source projects such as [Open Free Energy](https://openfree.energy/), built on toolkits like [OpenMM](https://openmm.org/), make such calculations widely available, and as of 2026 they routinely rank candidate molecules to within about 4 kJ/mol (1 kcal/mol) for favourable cases. Engineers apply the same second law to engines and refrigerators in [the second law and entropy](/en/me/second-law-and-entropy/) in Lumenary ME.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Entropy is disorder."** It is a useful first picture, but it misleads. Entropy measures the number of microstates, which includes how energy is spread among molecular motions, not only how messy the arrangement looks. Some spontaneous processes produce visible *order*: crystals grow from solution, and oil and water separate into layers, because the entropy gained elsewhere (in the solvent, or in the surroundings) more than pays for it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Living things violate the second law by making order."** The second law limits the entropy of the system *plus* its surroundings. A cell lowers its own entropy while releasing heat and small molecules (CO₂, water) that raise its surroundings' entropy by more. A plant runs on sunlight, and the Earth sends back many more, lower-energy infrared photons than the visible photons it receives.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A negative ΔG means the reaction will happen quickly."** Thermodynamics says where a reaction can go, not how fast. Petrol and air, sugar and oxygen, and paper and air all have hugely negative $\Delta G$ and sit unreacted for years. The rate depends on the energy barrier along the way (<LessonLink slug="chemical-kinetics">Chemical kinetics</LessonLink>).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If ΔG° is positive, the reaction does not happen at all."** $\Delta G^\circ$ refers to every substance in its standard state. Water's $\Delta G^\circ$ for vaporisation at 25 °C is +8.5 kJ/mol, meaning liquid water will not produce vapour *at 1 bar*; yet puddles evaporate, because the vapour in air is far below 1 bar. The next lesson shows how $\Delta G^\circ$ sets an equilibrium, here the vapour pressure of water.

</Misconception>
