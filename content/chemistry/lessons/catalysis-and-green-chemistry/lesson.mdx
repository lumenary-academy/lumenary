<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="More product, less of everything else">

Most materials around you passed over a catalyst on their way to you. The fertiliser that grew your food was made from ammonia over promoted iron; estimates suggest that the Haber–Bosch process now feeds roughly half the world's people ([Erisman et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1038/ngeo325)). Petrol and diesel were cracked and reshaped on zeolites; the polyethylene of a milk bottle was grown on titanium or chromium catalysts; the exhaust from a car passes over platinum, palladium and rhodium; and many medicines are assembled with palladium-catalysed couplings or with enzymes evolved in the laboratory.

A catalyst, as <LessonLink slug="chemical-kinetics">Chemical kinetics</LessonLink> showed, offers a route with lower barriers and is regenerated at the end. That makes it the central tool of a second idea, **green chemistry**: designing chemical products and processes that use less material and energy, make less waste and are less hazardous from the start, rather than cleaning up afterwards. The two belong together because a stoichiometric reagent, used once and thrown away, is waste by design, while a catalyst used a million times is not.

How much waste is at stake? Roger Sheldon's **E-factor**, kilograms of waste per kilogram of product, is below 0.1 for oil refining and around 1 to 5 for bulk chemicals, but commonly 25 to more than 100 for pharmaceuticals, where many steps, purifications and solvents are needed for every kilogram of medicine ([Sheldon, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1039/C6GC02157C)). Lowering those numbers is chemistry, not public relations, and this lesson shows how chemists compute them.

</Section>

<Section id="kinds" kind="definitions" title="Three kinds of catalyst">

<Definition term="catalyst, turnover number and turnover frequency">

A **catalyst** increases the rate of a reaction without being consumed overall; it takes part in the mechanism and is regenerated in a **catalytic cycle**. The **turnover number** (TON) is the number of moles of product made per mole of catalyst (or of active sites) before the catalyst dies. The **turnover frequency** (TOF) is the number of turnovers per active site per unit time, usually per second; for an enzyme it is $k_\text{cat}$.

</Definition>

A catalyst can only speed up a reaction that is already thermodynamically allowed, and because it lowers the forward and reverse barriers alike, it never moves the equilibrium. What it can change is **selectivity**: when several products are possible, a catalyst that speeds up one route much more than the others decides which product forms. Much of modern catalysis is about selectivity rather than raw speed.

**Homogeneous catalysts** are dissolved in the same phase as the reactants, usually as metal complexes of the kind met in <LessonLink slug="coordination-chemistry">Coordination chemistry</LessonLink>. Their ligands can be tuned atom by atom, so they can be extremely selective, even producing one enantiomer of a chiral product (<LessonLink slug="stereochemistry">Stereochemistry</LessonLink>), and their mechanisms can be studied in detail. Methanol is turned into acetic acid by rhodium and iridium complexes; palladium complexes join carbon fragments in the cross-coupling reactions recognised by the [2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2010/summary/) (Richard Heck, Ei-ichi Negishi and Akira Suzuki); ruthenium and molybdenum complexes swap the ends of carbon–carbon double bonds in olefin metathesis ([2005](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2005/summary/)). Their weakness is separation: the catalyst must be recovered, and residual metals in medicines must be kept below strict limits.

**Heterogeneous catalysts** are solids, usually metals or oxides, over which gases or liquids flow. Reaction happens only at the surface, so the active material is spread as nanoparticles a few nanometres across on a porous support such as alumina or silica, giving hundreds of square metres of surface per gram. Solids are easy to separate and survive high temperatures, which is why they run almost all of the bulk chemical industry, but their active sites are varied and hard to study; Gerhard Ertl's work on clean single-crystal surfaces, recognised by the [2007 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2007/summary/), showed how to do it. The crystal structures of these solids are the subject of [crystal structures](/en/materials/crystal-structures/) in Lumenary Materials.

**Enzymes** are nature's catalysts (<LessonLink slug="biochemistry">Biochemistry</LessonLink>): proteins that work in water near room temperature, often with exquisite selectivity. Since Frances Arnold showed how to improve them by directed evolution ([2018 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2018/summary/)), enzymes have been redesigned for industrial reactions they never met in nature. In a well-known case, an evolved transaminase replaced a high-pressure rhodium-catalysed hydrogenation in the manufacture of the diabetes medicine sitagliptin, removing the heavy metal and cutting total waste by 19 per cent ([Savile et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1188934)).

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why can't a catalyst shift an equilibrium? Because it would then be a perpetual-motion machine. If a catalyst made more product at equilibrium, you could remove it, let the mixture relax back and release energy, add it again and repeat for ever. Thermodynamics forbids that, so any route that speeds the forward reaction must speed the reverse by the same factor.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="turnover" kind="computation" title="Turnover, and the Sabatier principle">

The first part of the block below works out turnover numbers and frequencies from a hydrogenation run with one molecule of catalyst for every ten thousand of substrate (0.01 mol %). The second part asks the most important question in catalyst design: how strongly should the catalyst hold the reactant?

In 1913 the French chemist Paul Sabatier gave the qualitative answer: a good catalyst binds a reactant **neither too weakly nor too strongly**. Too weak, and little reactant is held on the surface; too strong, and what is held never leaves, so the surface fills up and stops working. The model has a reactant A that adsorbs on a site (equilibrium constant $K$, larger for stronger binding) and is then converted (rate constant $k$). Its barrier follows the Brønsted–Evans–Polanyi relation, a widely observed linear link between how stable an intermediate is and how high the barrier out of it is: the deeper the well, the higher the barrier.

```python run
import numpy as np

# 1. Turnover number and frequency for a hydrogenation run
n_sub, n_cat = 1.00, 1.0e-4                     # mol substrate, mol catalyst (0.01 mol %)
t_min = np.array([0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160])   # minutes
conv = np.array([0, 0.21, 0.39, 0.64, 0.88, 0.985, 0.999])
ton = conv * n_sub / n_cat
print("  t/min  conversion    TON")
for t, c, n in zip(t_min, conv, ton):
    print(f"{t:7d} {c:10.3f} {n:8.0f}")
tof0 = ton[1] / (t_min[1] * 60)                 # initial rate, per second
print(f"initial TOF = {tof0:.1f} per second; average over the run = {ton[-1] / (t_min[-1] * 60):.2f} per second")

# 2. The Sabatier principle: a toy model of A + * <=> A*,  A* -> product + *
R, T, p = 8.314e-3, 600.0, 1.0                  # kJ/(mol K), K, bar
dE = np.arange(-140, 1, 10.0)                   # adsorption energy of A, kJ/mol (negative = stronger)
K = 1e-6 * np.exp(-dE / (R * T))                # adsorption equilibrium constant, 1/bar
theta = K * p / (1 + K * p)                     # fraction of sites holding A
Ea = 100 - 0.5 * dE                             # stronger binding -> deeper well -> higher barrier
k = 1e13 * np.exp(-Ea / (R * T))                # rate constant of the surface step, 1/s
tof = k * theta
print("\n dE/kJ/mol  coverage   TOF/s^-1   (bar: log scale)")
for e, th, r in zip(dE, theta, tof):
    print(f"{e:9.0f} {th:9.2g} {r:10.2g}  " + "#" * int(round(8 * (np.log10(r) + 2.5))))
best = dE[np.argmax(tof)]
print(f"fastest at dE = {best:.0f} kJ/mol, where K*p = {1e-6 * np.exp(-best / (R * T)) * p:.2f}")
```

The hydrogenation reaches a TON of 9990 in under three hours. Its initial TOF is 7.0 per second, but the average over the run is only about 1 per second, because the rate falls as the substrate runs out. Quote a TOF with the conditions and conversion at which it was measured, or it means little.

The second table is a **volcano**. With weak binding (bottom rows) the coverage is tiny, and the rate climbs as binding strengthens; with strong binding (top rows) the surface is full, and the rate falls because the barrier out of the deep well keeps rising. The best catalyst sits near the point where about half the sites are occupied ($Kp \approx 1$), with a TOF several hundred times higher than at either end of the range.

Volcanoes turn catalyst searching into calculation. Density functional theory (<LessonLink slug="computational-chemistry">Computational chemistry</LessonLink>) can compute one adsorption energy for a candidate surface in hours, and that single number places it on the volcano. Screening thousands of alloys this way, then making only the few near the summit, is how catalyst discovery has been run since the early 2000s; machine-learned models trained on such calculations, such as those of the [Open Catalyst Project](https://opencatalystproject.org/), now do the first pass in seconds.

<Section id="haber-bosch" kind="examples" title="Case study: the Haber–Bosch catalyst">

<LessonLink slug="chemical-equilibrium">Chemical equilibrium</LessonLink> explained the conditions of ammonia synthesis: 400 to 500 °C, 100 to 300 bar and a recycle loop. Here is the catalyst that makes those conditions work.

Fritz Haber's laboratory process of 1909 used osmium and uranium, neither of which could be supplied at industrial scale. At BASF, Carl Bosch's engineers and Alwin Mittasch's chemists tested thousands of compositions and settled on **magnetite** (Fe₃O₄), fused with small amounts of potassium, aluminium and calcium oxides and then reduced in the converter to porous metallic iron. Versions of that recipe are still used. Each additive has a job:

- **Aluminium oxide** is a *structural promoter*: it sits between the iron crystallites and stops them sintering into larger, less active grains at 450 °C.
- **Potassium oxide** is an *electronic promoter*: it donates electron density to the iron, which strengthens the back-donation into N₂'s antibonding orbitals (<LessonLink slug="molecular-orbitals">Molecular orbitals</LessonLink>) and helps break the triple bond. It also weakens the binding of ammonia, so the product leaves the surface more easily.
- **Calcium oxide** improves stability and resistance to poisons.

Ertl's experiments on single crystals of iron in ultra-high vacuum established the mechanism: N₂ adsorbs and **dissociates** into nitrogen atoms on the surface, and this dissociation is the slow, rate-determining step; the atoms are then hydrogenated one hydrogen at a time to NH, NH₂ and NH₃, which desorbs. Some crystal faces of iron are hundreds of times more active than others, because only certain arrangements of surface atoms bind N₂ in the way that splits it.

This is exactly the volcano's trade-off. A metal must bind nitrogen strongly enough to split N₂, yet weakly enough that the nitrogen atoms are hydrogenated and leave. Iron is slightly too strong; ruthenium is near the summit and is used, promoted with caesium or barium on carbon or oxide supports, in some modern plants because it works at lower pressure. Jacobsen and colleagues used the volcano to reason that cobalt (too weak) and molybdenum (too strong) should combine into something close to ideal, and the cobalt–molybdenum nitride they then made confirmed it ([2001](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja010963d)).

The industrial iron catalyst lasts a decade or more if it is protected from **poisons**. Oxygen-containing molecules (water, CO, CO₂) and especially sulfur compounds bind to the active sites and block them, so the synthesis gas is purified to low parts per million before it reaches the converter. Nature solves the same problem differently: the enzyme nitrogenase reduces N₂ at room temperature using a cluster of iron, molybdenum and sulfur, at the cost of a large amount of ATP for every N₂.

</Section>

<Section id="contact" kind="examples" title="Case study: the contact process">

Sulfuric acid is made in larger tonnage than almost any other chemical, mostly for phosphate fertilisers. Much of the sulfur comes from cleaning sulfur compounds out of natural gas and crude oil, so the industry turns a pollutant into a product. The key step is the catalytic oxidation of sulfur dioxide:

$$\mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \tfrac12\,\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{SO_3(g)}, \qquad \Delta_\text{r}H^\circ = -98.9\ \text{kJ/mol}.$$

Without a catalyst it is immeasurably slow. The industrial catalyst is vanadium(V) oxide with potassium or caesium sulfate on porous silica; under operating conditions the active phase is a thin film of molten salt on the support. Vanadium changes oxidation state through the cycle: V(V) oxidises SO₂ to SO₃ and becomes V(IV), which oxygen then reoxidises. Because the oxygen in the product comes from the catalyst's own lattice and is replaced from the gas, this is called a **Mars–van Krevelen** mechanism; it is common for oxide catalysts.

The reaction is exothermic and reduces the number of gas molecules, so the equilibrium favours SO₃ at low temperature, yet the catalyst needs about 400 °C to work quickly. And because the gas heats itself as it reacts, a single bed would run hot and stop far from complete conversion. Plants therefore use several **adiabatic beds** with cooling between them, and the most effective trick of all is to remove the SO₃ before the last bed. The block models this, treating each bed as reaching equilibrium.

```python run
import math

# SO2 + 1/2 O2 <=> SO3, standard data at 298 K (NIST): ΔrH° = -98.9 kJ/mol, ΔrS° = -94.0 J/(mol K)
R, dH, dS = 8.314, -98.9e3, -94.0
P, cp = 1.2, 32.0          # bar in the converter; J/(mol K), mean heat capacity of the gas (mostly N2)

def lnK(T):                # ΔH° and ΔS° taken as constant: a fair estimate over this range
    return -(dH - T * dS) / (R * T)

def excess(gas, xi, T):
    """ln Q - ln K after a further extent xi (mol) of reaction; increases with xi."""
    so2, o2, so3, n2 = gas["SO2"] - xi, gas["O2"] - xi / 2, gas["SO3"] + xi, gas["N2"]
    n = so2 + o2 + so3 + n2
    return math.log((so3 / n) / ((so2 / n) * math.sqrt(o2 / n * P))) - lnK(T)

def react(gas, T_in, adiabatic):
    """Extent of reaction reached at equilibrium, at fixed T or along the adiabatic line."""
    n = sum(gas.values())
    T_of = lambda xi: T_in + (xi * -dH / (n * cp) if adiabatic else 0.0)
    lo, hi = 1e-12, gas["SO2"] * (1 - 1e-12)
    for _ in range(100):                                  # bisection
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if excess(gas, mid, T_of(mid)) < 0 else (lo, mid)
    xi = (lo + hi) / 2
    return xi, T_of(xi)

feed = {"SO2": 10.0, "O2": 11.0, "SO3": 0.0, "N2": 79.0}  # mol per 100 mol of burner gas
print("T/°C   K/bar^-1/2   equilibrium conversion of the feed")
for T in range(673, 974, 50):
    print(f"{T - 273:4d} {math.exp(lnK(T)):11.3g} {100 * react(feed, T, False)[0] / feed['SO2']:14.1f} %")

for absorb in (False, True):
    print("\nfour adiabatic beds, gas cooled to 420 °C before each" + (", SO3 absorbed before bed 4" if absorb else ""))
    gas = dict(feed)
    for bed in range(1, 5):
        if absorb and bed == 4:
            gas["SO3"] = 0.0                              # interpass absorption
        xi, T_out = react(gas, 693.0, True)
        gas["SO2"] -= xi; gas["O2"] -= xi / 2; gas["SO3"] += xi
        print(f"  bed {bed}: outlet {T_out - 273:4.0f} °C, SO2 converted so far {100 * (1 - gas['SO2'] / feed['SO2']):6.2f} %")
```

At 400 °C the equilibrium would allow 99.4 per cent conversion, but the first bed heats from 420 °C to about 630 °C as it reacts and stops at 67 per cent, where the hot gas reaches equilibrium. Cooling and a second bed bring the total to 94 per cent, a third to 98.5 per cent. A fourth bed then adds almost nothing, because the SO₃ already present holds the equilibrium back. Remove that SO₃ first, in an absorption tower, and the same fourth bed takes the overall conversion to 99.98 per cent in this idealised model. That **double-absorption** design, Le Chatelier's principle at industrial scale, is why modern plants emit only a small fraction of the sulfur dioxide that older single-absorption plants did. The heat released is not wasted either: sulfuric acid plants usually export steam to generate electricity.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The model lets every bed reach equilibrium, which real beds of finite size and finite catalyst activity never quite do; it also holds $\Delta_\text{r}H^\circ$, $\Delta_\text{r}S^\circ$ and the heat capacity constant. Real double-absorption plants reach about 99.7 to 99.9 per cent. The model's value is the shape of the answer: why there are several beds, why they are cooled between, and why removing the product before the last bed matters so much more than adding a fifth bed.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="green-chemistry" kind="human" title="Green chemistry: principles and metrics">

In 1998 Paul Anastas and John Warner set out twelve principles of green chemistry, listed on the [ACS Green Chemistry Institute's site](https://www.acs.org/green-chemistry-sustainability/principles/12-principles-of-green-chemistry.html). In short, and in this lesson's own words, they ask a chemist to:

| Aim | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Prevent waste | Design so that waste is not formed, rather than treating it afterwards. |
| Maximise atom economy | Choose reactions whose reactant atoms mostly end up in the product. |
| Use and make less hazardous substances | Prefer reagents, intermediates and products of low toxicity. |
| Design safer products | A product should do its job with as little toxicity as possible. |
| Choose safer solvents, or none | Solvents and other helpers are most of the mass; avoid or replace the harmful ones. |
| Save energy | Work near room temperature and pressure where you can. |
| Use renewable feedstocks | Start from biomass, CO₂ or waste where that is practical. |
| Avoid needless steps | Protecting groups and temporary modifications cost reagents and make waste. |
| Prefer catalysts | A catalytic reagent used many times beats a stoichiometric one used once. |
| Design for degradation | Products should break down into harmless substances after use. |
| Measure as you go | Monitor processes in real time so that problems are caught before waste forms. |
| Make accidents less likely | Choose substances and conditions that minimise the risk of releases, fires and explosions. |

The last aim has a grim history. In 1984 a leak of methyl isocyanate, a toxic intermediate stored in large tanks at a pesticide plant in Bhopal, India, killed thousands of people. A process that makes such an intermediate only as it is needed, in small amounts, or that avoids it altogether, is **inherently safer**, and no procedure or alarm is as reliable as a hazard that is not there.

To act on principles, chemists need numbers. <LessonLink slug="the-mole-and-stoichiometry">The mole and stoichiometry</LessonLink> introduced **atom economy**, the molar mass of the desired product as a percentage of the total molar mass of the reactants in the overall equation; Barry Trost made it a design goal ([1991](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1962206)). The **E-factor** counts actual waste, $E = (\text{mass of everything in} - \text{mass of product})/\text{mass of product}$, and the pharmaceutical industry's **process mass intensity**, $\text{PMI} = (\text{mass in})/(\text{mass of product}) = E + 1$, counts everything used, including solvents and water. The classic comparison is between two industrial routes to ibuprofen: the original six-step route of the Boots company in the 1960s, and the three-step route of the BHC Company, which won a US [Green Chemistry Challenge Award](https://www.epa.gov/greenchemistry) in 1997 and uses catalysts in all three steps.

```python run
import re

MASS = {"C": 12.011, "H": 1.008, "N": 14.007, "O": 15.999, "Cl": 35.45, "Na": 22.990}

def molar_mass(formula):
    """Molar mass from a simple formula such as 'C13H18O2' (no brackets)."""
    return sum(MASS[el] * int(n or 1) for el, n in re.findall(r"([A-Z][a-z]?)(\d*)", formula))

def atom_economy(product, reactants):
    return 100 * molar_mass(product) / sum(molar_mass(f) * k for f, k in reactants)

ibuprofen = "C13H18O2"
# Every reagent whose atoms appear in the overall stoichiometric equation of each route
boots = [("C10H14", 1), ("C4H6O3", 1), ("C4H7ClO2", 1), ("C2H5ONa", 1),   # isobutylbenzene, acetic anhydride,
         ("H3O", 1), ("NH3O", 1), ("H2O", 2)]                             # ethyl chloroacetate, ethoxide, acid, hydroxylamine, water
bhc = [("C10H14", 1), ("C4H6O3", 1), ("H2", 1), ("CO", 1)]               # + hydrogen and carbon monoxide, both on catalysts
print(f"ibuprofen {molar_mass(ibuprofen):.2f} g/mol")
print(f"Boots route, 6 steps: atom economy {atom_economy(ibuprofen, boots):5.1f} %")
print(f"BHC route,   3 steps: atom economy {atom_economy(ibuprofen, bhc):5.1f} %")
recovered = molar_mass("C2H4O2")                                           # acetic acid by-product, sold
print(f"BHC, counting the acetic acid by-product as useful: {100 * (molar_mass(ibuprofen) + recovered) / sum(molar_mass(f) * k for f, k in bhc):5.1f} %")

# E-factor and process mass intensity (PMI) for one step, from an illustrative mass balance (kg per batch)
inputs = {"reactants": 142.0, "catalyst and reagents": 6.0, "solvent": 950.0, "process water": 600.0}
product = 100.0
for recycled in (0.0, 0.9):
    total = sum(inputs.values()) - recycled * inputs["solvent"]
    E = (total - product) / product
    E_no_water = (total - inputs["process water"] - product) / product
    print(f"solvent recycled {recycled:4.0%}: PMI {total / product:5.1f}, E-factor {E:5.1f} ({E_no_water:4.1f} excluding water)")
```

The Boots route puts only 40 per cent of its reactant mass into ibuprofen; the rest leaves as salts, ethanol, carbon dioxide, ammonia and spent reagents, and its Friedel–Crafts step consumed at least a mole of aluminium chloride per mole of product, which became aluminium waste. The BHC route reaches 77.5 per cent, and since its only by-product, acetic acid, is recovered and sold, essentially every atom is used. Its first step uses hydrogen fluoride as both catalyst and solvent, recovered and reused, which shows that "green" is a judgement about the whole process: hydrogen fluoride is very hazardous, and the route depends on handling it in a closed system with excellent engineering.

The mass balance shows where waste really comes from. Reactants are 142 kg of a 1698 kg batch; the solvent and the water used in washing and extraction dominate. Recycling 90 per cent of the solvent halves the PMI. That is why the second half of green chemistry is about solvents.

**Choosing a solvent.** Solvents are judged on worker health (toxicity, especially chronic), safety (flammability, and peroxide formation in ethers), environment (persistence, contribution to smog and ozone loss) and the energy needed to make, recover and dispose of them. Industry guides such as the [CHEM21 guide](https://doi.org/10.1039/C5GC01008J) and those of the [ACS GCI Pharmaceutical Roundtable](https://www.acsgcipr.org/) rank common solvents from "recommended" (water, ethanol, ethyl acetate, acetone) to "highly hazardous" (benzene, chloroform, tetrachloromethane). Dichloromethane and the dipolar aprotic solvents DMF and NMP, once everywhere, now carry regulatory restrictions in Europe or the United States, and replacements such as 2-methyltetrahydrofuran, which can be made from agricultural waste, have taken some of their place. Supercritical carbon dioxide decaffeinates coffee, water is increasingly used for reactions once thought impossible in it, and ball-milling (mechanochemistry) runs some reactions with no solvent at all.

**Life-cycle thinking.** A process that looks green at the reactor can still be harmful overall. **Life-cycle assessment** (standardised in ISO 14040 and 14044) adds up the inputs and emissions of a product from extraction of its raw materials (the "cradle") to the factory gate or to its disposal (the "grave"), across impacts such as climate change, water use, land use and toxicity. It regularly overturns intuition: a solvent made from crops may carry more fertiliser and land-use burden than one made from oil, and hydrogen from electrolysis is low-carbon only if the electricity is. The results depend on where the system boundary is drawn and how burdens are shared between co-products, so an honest assessment states both. Lumenary Materials takes the same view of materials in [sustainable materials](/en/materials/sustainable-materials/), and <LessonLink slug="sustainable-chemistry">Sustainable chemistry</LessonLink> follows these ideas to the frontier.

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Catalysis in industry and research, 2026">

**Catalysts die, and plants are designed around it.** Heterogeneous catalysts lose activity by sintering (small particles merging into large ones), poisoning (sulfur, chlorine and metals binding to sites), coking (carbon deposits, burned off in regeneration) and mechanical wear. Fluid catalytic cracking units regenerate their zeolite catalyst continuously, every few minutes; ammonia catalysts last years; car catalysts must survive more than 150 000 km. Measuring and slowing deactivation is a large part of industrial catalysis.

**Metals and supply.** Platinum-group metals are scarce and mined in few countries, chiefly South Africa and Russia, so there is steady work on catalysts based on earth-abundant metals such as iron, cobalt, nickel and copper, on single-atom catalysts that use every precious-metal atom, and on recycling, which already recovers a large share of the platinum, palladium and rhodium in old car catalysts.

**New energy, new catalysis.** Making hydrogen by electrolysis, converting CO₂ to fuels and chemicals, and making ammonia electrochemically or in small plants run on renewable electricity all depend on **electrocatalysts**, the subject of <LessonLink slug="electrochemistry">Electrochemistry</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="energy-chemistry">The chemistry of energy</LessonLink>. As of 2026 these are active areas of research and early deployment, with catalyst durability and cost as the main obstacles, and the lithium-mediated route to ammonia among the most studied laboratory approaches.

**How the work is done.** Catalyst development combines parallel testing of hundreds of formulations, operando spectroscopy and microscopy that watch a catalyst while it works (see [microscopy](/en/materials/microscopy/) in Lumenary Materials), and computation. Pharmaceutical process chemists track PMI from the first route to the commercial one and share benchmarks through the ACS GCI Pharmaceutical Roundtable.

<Section id="try-it" kind="human" title="Something safe to try at home">

**An enzyme at work in the kitchen.** Fresh pineapple contains bromelain, a mixture of enzymes that cut proteins. With an adult, make two small bowls of jelly from gelatine (a protein) following the packet, and stir a few pieces of *fresh* pineapple into one and the same amount of *tinned* pineapple into the other. The bowl with tinned fruit sets; the bowl with fresh fruit stays runny, because the enzyme catalyses the breaking of the gelatine's protein chains. Tinned pineapple has been heated, which unfolds the enzyme and destroys its activity. Kiwi fruit and papaya contain similar enzymes. Use only food and clean kitchen equipment, and wash up afterwards.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The best catalyst binds the reactants as strongly as possible."** Binding that is too strong leaves the surface covered with intermediates that never react or leave. The Sabatier principle and the volcano plot show that the best catalysts bind at an intermediate strength.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"All of a solid catalyst does the work."** Only surface atoms, and often only a small fraction of those at particular sites such as steps and edges, take part. That is why catalysts are made as nanoparticles on high-surface-area supports, and why their activity is reported per active site.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"High atom economy means little waste."** Atom economy comes from the balanced equation and ignores yield, excess reagents, solvents and purification. The E-factor or PMI of the real process can be large even when the atom economy is 100 per cent.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Natural or bio-based means green."** A substance's origin does not decide its impact. Only a life-cycle assessment with stated boundaries can compare two routes fairly, and its answer is sometimes the opposite of the label's promise.

</Misconception>
