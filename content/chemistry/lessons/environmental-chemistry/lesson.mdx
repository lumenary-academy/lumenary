<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A thin shell">

Squeeze the whole atmosphere down to the density of the air at sea level and it would be a layer about 8 km deep, less than the height of Everest. Squeeze the ozone layer the same way and it would be about 3 mm thick. On these thin films depend the air we breathe, the ultraviolet light that does not reach us, and the temperature of the planet. Below them, the oceans hold fifty times as much carbon as the atmosphere, and life on land depends on liquid fresh water, less than 1 per cent of all the water on Earth.

Environmental chemistry studies how substances move and react in air, water, soil and living things, and how human activity changes those flows. It is a measuring science first: almost everything known about the ozone hole, greenhouse gases and pollutants comes from careful analysis at parts per million, billion or trillion (<LessonLink slug="analytical-chemistry">Analytical chemistry</LessonLink>). It is also a science with a remarkable success story, the repair of the ozone layer, and with problems still being worked on. This lesson takes them in turn, with the calculations that make them concrete.

</Section>

<Section id="atmosphere" kind="definitions" title="What the air is made of">

<Definition term="mole fraction, mixing ratio and number density">

The **mole fraction** (or mixing ratio) of a gas is its share of the molecules in a sample of air, often given in parts per million (ppm, $10^{-6}$), billion (ppb, $10^{-9}$) or trillion (ppt). For an ideal gas it equals the volume fraction and the share of the pressure. The **number density** is molecules per cubic metre, $n = p/(k_\text{B} T)$ for the whole gas and $x_i\,n$ for a gas of mole fraction $x_i$.

</Definition>

Dry air is 78.08 per cent nitrogen, 20.95 per cent oxygen and 0.93 per cent argon; everything else together is about 0.04 per cent. Water vapour, from almost nothing to about 4 per cent, is usually counted separately. The trace gases matter far beyond their amounts: carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide absorb infrared radiation, ozone absorbs ultraviolet, and reactive species at parts per trillion, such as the hydroxyl radical, clean the air by oxidising pollutants.

The temperature of the atmosphere does not simply fall with height. It falls through the **troposphere**, where weather happens, to about −56 °C at the **tropopause** (about 11 km at mid-latitudes), then *rises* through the **stratosphere**, because ozone there absorbs solar ultraviolet and heats the air. The warm layer on top of a cold one is stable, so the stratosphere mixes slowly with the air below: what gets up there stays for years.

The block puts numbers on the shell: molecules per cubic metre, the height of the atmosphere at sea-level density (its **scale height**), the ozone column in **Dobson units** (1 DU is a layer of pure ozone 0.01 mm thick at 0 °C and 1 atm), and the conversion between ppb and the µg/m³ used in air-quality standards.

```python run
k_B, N_A, R, g = 1.380649e-23, 6.02214076e23, 8.314, 9.81
p0, T0, M_air = 101325.0, 288.15, 0.02897          # sea-level pressure (Pa), temperature (K), kg/mol

# Dry air: mole fractions (CO2, CH4 and N2O are NOAA global means for 2025)
air = {"N2": 0.78084, "O2": 0.20946, "Ar": 0.00934, "CO2": 425.6e-6,
       "Ne": 18.18e-6, "He": 5.24e-6, "CH4": 1.936e-6, "N2O": 0.339e-6}
n = p0 / (k_B * T0)                                 # molecules per cubic metre at sea level
print(f"molecules per m^3 at sea level: {n:.3e}")
for gas, x in air.items():
    print(f"  {gas:4s} {x:10.3e} mole fraction  {x * n:10.2e} per m^3")

# The whole atmosphere, and the ozone layer, squeezed to sea-level conditions
column = p0 / (g * M_air) * N_A                     # molecules above each square metre
print(f"\nmolecules above 1 m^2 of ground: {column:.2e}")
print(f"thickness at sea-level density (scale height RT/Mg): {R * T0 / (M_air * g) / 1000:.1f} km")
DU = 2.687e20                                       # molecules per m^2 in one Dobson unit (0.01 mm at 0 °C, 1 atm)
for du in (300, 220, 100):
    print(f"ozone column {du} DU = {du * 0.01:.1f} mm of pure ozone; column-average mole fraction {du * DU / column * 1e6:.2f} ppm")

# Air-quality units: µg/m^3 = ppb x M / 24.45 at 25 °C and 1 atm (24.45 L/mol)
for gas, M, guideline in [("NO2 (annual)", 46.01, 10), ("O3 (8-hour)", 48.00, 100), ("SO2 (24-hour)", 64.07, 40)]:
    print(f"WHO 2021 guideline {gas:14s} {guideline:4d} µg/m^3 = {guideline * 24.45 / M:5.1f} ppb")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

Even a trace gas is a crowd. At 425.6 ppm there are about $10^{22}$ CO₂ molecules in every cubic metre of air, and methane at under 2 ppm still supplies $5 \times 10^{19}$. The whole ozone layer averages only 0.38 ppm of the air above us, yet those 3 mm of ozone stop almost all of the Sun's most damaging ultraviolet; the Antarctic ozone hole is conventionally defined by columns below 220 DU.

</Narrator>

<Section id="ozone" kind="human" title="The ozone layer, CFCs and the Montreal Protocol">

In 1930 Sydney Chapman explained the ozone layer with four reactions. Ultraviolet light below 242 nm splits O₂ into atoms; an O atom joins an O₂ (with a third molecule M carrying away the energy) to make O₃; ozone absorbs longer-wavelength ultraviolet, 200–310 nm, and falls apart again; and O + O₃ gives back two O₂. The balance between them sets a layer peaking at around 20–25 km. Ozone is constantly made and destroyed, and the absorbed ultraviolet ends up as heat, which is what warms the stratosphere.

Chapman's reactions alone predict more ozone than is observed, because **catalytic cycles** destroy it faster. In 1974 Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland pointed out that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), the unreactive gases then used in refrigerators, aerosol cans and foams, would drift up to the stratosphere, be broken by hard ultraviolet, and release chlorine atoms ([Molina and Rowland, 1974](https://doi.org/10.1038/249810a0)). A chlorine atom is a catalyst for ozone destruction:

The warning was confirmed dramatically. In 1985 Joseph Farman, Brian Gardiner and Jonathan Shanklin of the British Antarctic Survey reported that springtime ozone over Halley station had fallen by about a third since the 1960s ([Farman et al., 1985](https://doi.org/10.1038/315207a0)), far more than gas-phase chemistry predicted. The explanation came from laboratory and field chemistry: in the extreme cold of the Antarctic winter, **polar stratospheric clouds** form, and on the surfaces of their particles the "reservoir" compounds HCl and ClONO₂, which normally lock chlorine away, react to release Cl₂. When the Sun returns in spring, Cl₂ is split and the catalytic destruction runs at full speed. Molina, Rowland and Paul Crutzen, who had shown the parallel role of nitrogen oxides, shared the [1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1995/summary/).

The **Montreal Protocol** of 1987, strengthened by later amendments, phased out the production of CFCs, halons and other ozone-depleting substances; it became the first United Nations treaty ratified by every member state ([Ozone Secretariat](https://ozone.unep.org/)). Replacements came from chemistry too: hydrochlorofluorocarbons as a stopgap, then hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), which contain no chlorine. Because HFCs are strong greenhouse gases, the 2016 Kigali Amendment phases them down in turn, in favour of hydrofluoroolefins, hydrocarbons, ammonia and CO₂ as refrigerants.

Monitoring keeps the treaty honest. In 2018 the global networks that measure trace gases reported that CFC-11 was declining more slowly than it should, implying new production in spite of the ban ([Montzka et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0106-2)); atmospheric measurements traced much of it to eastern China, enforcement followed, and later measurements showed the excess emissions falling away within a few years. The [2022 scientific assessment](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/) projected that, if current policies hold, the ozone layer should return to its 1980 values around 2040 for most of the world, around 2045 over the Arctic and around 2066 over Antarctica. The slowness is chemistry, as the next block shows: a CFC-11 molecule survives about 52 years.

</Section>

<Section id="greenhouse" kind="computation" title="Greenhouse gases and radiative forcing, in outline">

The Earth absorbs sunlight and must radiate the same power back to space as infrared. A planet without an atmosphere at Earth's distance from the Sun, reflecting 30 per cent of the sunlight that reaches it, would balance at about 255 K. The real surface averages about 288 K. The difference is the **greenhouse effect**: water vapour, CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone absorb infrared emitted by the surface (molecules whose vibrations change their dipole moment, as <LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink> explained) and re-emit it, partly downwards, so the planet radiates to space from higher, colder layers, and the surface must be warmer to push the same power out.

<Definition term="radiative forcing">

**Radiative forcing** is the change in the net energy flux at the top of the atmosphere, in W/m², caused by a change such as more CO₂, before the surface temperature has responded. A positive forcing makes the planet gain energy until it warms enough to restore the balance. The IPCC uses the **effective radiative forcing**, which includes fast adjustments of the atmosphere ([IPCC AR6 WG1](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/)).

</Definition>

For CO₂, a convenient fit to detailed radiative-transfer calculations is $\Delta F = 5.35 \ln(C/C_0)$ W/m² ([Myhre et al., 1998](https://doi.org/10.1029/98GL01908)), where $C_0$ is a reference concentration. The block computes the effective temperature, this forcing, and how much warming it would cause with no feedbacks at all, then looks at how long gases stay in the air.

```python run
import math

sigma, S0, albedo, T_surface = 5.670e-8, 1361.0, 0.30, 288.0
T_e = (S0 * (1 - albedo) / (4 * sigma)) ** 0.25          # temperature at which Earth radiates to space
print(f"effective radiating temperature {T_e:.1f} K; surface {T_surface:.0f} K; greenhouse effect {T_surface - T_e:.0f} K")

# Radiative forcing of CO2, simplified expression of Myhre et al. (1998): ΔF = 5.35 ln(C/C0) W/m^2
C0 = 280.0                                                # pre-industrial, ppm
for C in (340.0, 425.6, 560.0):
    print(f"CO2 {C:5.1f} ppm: forcing {5.35 * math.log(C / C0):4.2f} W/m^2")

planck = 4 * sigma * T_e**3                               # extra emission per kelvin of warming, W/(m^2 K)
F2x = 5.35 * math.log(2)
print(f"\nwithout feedbacks: {F2x:.2f} W/m^2 / {planck:.2f} W/(m^2 K) = {F2x / planck:.2f} K per doubling")
F2x_ar6, ecs_ar6 = 3.93, 3.0                              # IPCC AR6: forcing of doubled CO2, best-estimate warming
print(f"IPCC AR6 best estimate, {ecs_ar6:.0f} K per doubling: net feedback parameter {F2x_ar6 / ecs_ar6:.2f} W/(m^2 K)")

# One-box model: after emissions stop, a gas with lifetime tau decays as exp(-t/tau)
for gas, tau in [("methane", 11.8), ("CFC-11", 52.0), ("CFC-12", 102.0), ("N2O", 109.0)]:
    print(f"{gas:8s} lifetime {tau:5.1f} y: half gone after {tau * math.log(2):5.1f} y, {100 * math.exp(-50 / tau):4.1f} % left after 50 y")
```

Rising from 280 to 425.6 ppm, CO₂ exerts a forcing of about 2.2 W/m², close to the IPCC's assessed 2.16 W/m² for 1750–2019. Doubling CO₂ would give 3.7 W/m² by this formula (3.9 W/m² in the IPCC's fuller assessment), and if the planet only radiated more as it warmed, that would mean about 1 K of warming. The IPCC's best estimate of the equilibrium warming for doubled CO₂ is 3 K, with a likely range of 2.5 to 4 K, because **feedbacks** amplify the response: warmer air holds more water vapour, a strong greenhouse gas; melting ice and snow reflect less sunlight; clouds change. Those feedbacks are physics and meteorology more than chemistry, and their quantification is the subject of the IPCC report.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why a logarithm? The strongest CO₂ absorption bands are already saturated: in their centres, almost no surface infrared escapes, so adding CO₂ there changes little. The extra absorption comes from the weaker *wings* of the bands, which widen only slowly as the concentration grows. Each doubling therefore adds roughly the same forcing, whether from 280 to 560 ppm or from 560 to 1120.

</Narrator>

Lifetimes decide how long a decision lasts. Methane is removed mainly by the hydroxyl radical in about 12 years, so cutting its emissions slows warming within decades; per kilogram, it traps about 27 to 30 times as much heat as CO₂ over a century (the **global warming potential**), and nitrous oxide about 273 times (IPCC AR6). CFC-11 and N₂O persist for half a century or more.

<Narrator who="gideon">

CO₂ has no single lifetime, so the one-box model does not apply to it. An extra pulse of CO₂ is shared among the atmosphere, the ocean surface and the biosphere within decades, but a substantial fraction stays in the air for centuries to millennia, until the deep ocean and the slow reactions of rocks take it up. That long tail is why the warming from CO₂ depends on cumulative emissions.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="ocean" kind="computation" title="Ocean acidification from carbonate equilibria">

The ocean has absorbed roughly a quarter of the CO₂ emitted by human activity, which has slowed climate change but changed the ocean's chemistry. Dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions; most of them combine with carbonate ions to form hydrogencarbonate:

$$\mathrm{CO_2(aq) + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + HCO_3^-}, \qquad \mathrm{H^+ + CO_3^{2-} \rightleftharpoons HCO_3^-}.$$

So pH falls and, just as importantly, the **carbonate ion** concentration falls. Corals, molluscs, sea urchins and many plankton build shells and skeletons of calcium carbonate, and they find it harder as the **saturation state** $\Omega = [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}][\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]/K_\text{sp}$ drops; below $\Omega = 1$ the mineral tends to dissolve.

<LessonLink slug="acids-bases-and-buffers">Acids, bases and buffers</LessonLink> treated carbonic acid in blood. Seawater needs three changes. The equilibrium constants are measured in seawater itself, because its high ionic strength changes the activities. Borate is a second buffer. And the quantity held fixed is not a concentration but the **total alkalinity**, the excess of proton acceptors over donors, $A_\text{T} = [\mathrm{HCO_3^-}] + 2[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] + [\mathrm{B(OH)_4^-}] + [\mathrm{OH^-}] - [\mathrm{H^+}]$, which adding CO₂ does not change (CO₂ adds an acid and its conjugate base together) and which is set over millennia by the weathering of rocks. Given the CO₂ in the air (Henry's law, from <LessonLink slug="solutions">Solutions</LessonLink>) and the alkalinity, the pH follows by solving one equation.

```python run
import math

# Surface seawater at 25 °C and salinity 35. Equilibrium constants on the total pH scale (mol/kg):
T, S = 298.15, 35.0
K0 = 0.02839                     # CO2 solubility, mol/(kg atm)               (Weiss 1974)
K1 = 10 ** -(3633.86 / T - 61.2172 + 9.6777 * math.log(T) - 0.011555 * S + 0.0001152 * S**2)   # Lueker 2000
K2 = 10 ** -(471.78 / T + 25.9290 - 3.16967 * math.log(T) - 0.01781 * S + 0.0001122 * S**2)    # Lueker 2000
KB, BT = 10 ** -8.597, 416e-6    # boric acid, and total boron                (Dickson 1990; Uppström 1974)
KW = 10 ** -13.22                # water
CA, KSP_ARAG = 0.01028, 6.65e-7  # calcium, and aragonite solubility product (Mucci 1983)
TA = 2300e-6                     # total alkalinity, mol/kg: fixed by rock weathering, not by CO2

def carbonate_system(pco2_uatm):
    """pH and carbonate species of seawater in equilibrium with air at a given pCO2."""
    co2 = K0 * pco2_uatm * 1e-6                       # dissolved CO2, fixed by Henry's law
    def alk_error(pH):
        h = 10 ** -pH
        hco3, co3 = K1 * co2 / h, K1 * K2 * co2 / h**2
        borate = KB * BT / (KB + h)
        return hco3 + 2 * co3 + borate + KW / h - h - TA     # increases with pH
    lo, hi = 6.0, 10.0
    for _ in range(60):                                       # bisection on pH
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if alk_error(mid) < 0 else (lo, mid)
    pH = (lo + hi) / 2
    h = 10 ** -pH
    hco3, co3 = K1 * co2 / h, K1 * K2 * co2 / h**2
    return pH, co2, hco3, co3, co2 + hco3 + co3

print(f"pK1 = {-math.log10(K1):.3f}, pK2 = {-math.log10(K2):.3f}")
print(" pCO2/µatm    pH   CO2   HCO3-  CO3^2-   DIC  (µmol/kg)  Ω aragonite")
base = None
for p in [280, 425, 560, 800]:
    pH, co2, hco3, co3, dic = carbonate_system(p)
    omega = CA * co3 / KSP_ARAG
    print(f"{p:9d} {pH:6.3f} {1e6*co2:5.1f} {1e6*hco3:7.0f} {1e6*co3:6.0f} {1e6*dic:6.0f}  {omega:16.2f}")
    if p == 280:
        base = (pH, co3)
    if p == 425:
        print(f"   since 280 µatm: pH {pH - base[0]:+.3f}, [H+] {100 * (10**(base[0] - pH) - 1):+.0f} %, carbonate ion {100 * (co3 / base[1] - 1):+.0f} %")

# The Revelle factor: a 1 % rise in pCO2 needs only about a tenth as large a rise in DIC
p1, p2 = 425.0, 425.0 * 1.001
d1, d2 = carbonate_system(p1)[4], carbonate_system(p2)[4]
print(f"Revelle factor = (dpCO2/pCO2) / (dDIC/DIC) = {(p2 / p1 - 1) / (d2 / d1 - 1):.1f}")
```

In this warm surface water, raising CO₂ from 280 to 425 µatm lowers the pH from 8.17 to 8.02, which means 40 per cent more hydrogen ions, and lowers the carbonate ion concentration by 22 per cent. The saturation state for aragonite, the form of calcium carbonate that corals build, falls from 4.1 to 3.2, and to 2.0 at 800 µatm. The often-quoted fall "from 8.2 to 8.1" describes the change up to about the year 2000; the measured decline continues at about 0.02 pH units per decade at ocean time-series stations such as [Station ALOHA](https://hahana.soest.hawaii.edu/hot/) near Hawaii. Most of the dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) is hydrogencarbonate, and the **Revelle factor** of about 10 says that a 10 per cent rise in atmospheric CO₂ raises the ocean's dissolved carbon by only about 1 per cent at equilibrium; as the carbonate ion is used up, the factor grows and the ocean takes up CO₂ less readily ([Doney et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.marine.010908.163834)).

<Narrator who="gideon">

The block is a model of one water sample: 25 °C, salinity 35, fixed alkalinity, and seawater exactly in equilibrium with the air. Cold water dissolves more CO₂ and starts with lower carbonate, so polar and deep waters reach aragonite undersaturation first, and parts of the Southern Ocean and Arctic already do at some seasons. Oceanographers use the same equations with temperature- and pressure-dependent constants, in software such as the open-source [PyCO2SYS](https://pyco2sys.readthedocs.io/).

</Narrator>

<Section id="water" kind="human" title="Water chemistry and treatment">

Natural water is a solution shaped by the rocks it has crossed and the life it supports. Rain is slightly acidic from dissolved CO₂ (pH about 5.6) and dissolves calcium and magnesium carbonates, giving the **hardness** that analysts measure by EDTA titration (<LessonLink slug="analytical-chemistry">Analytical chemistry</LessonLink>). Dissolved oxygen, only about 8 mg/L at 25 °C, is what fish and most aquatic life depend on; decomposing organic matter consumes it, which is why wastewater is characterised by its **biochemical oxygen demand**. Excess nitrate and phosphate from fertilisers and sewage feed algal blooms whose decay strips the oxygen from lakes and coastal seas (**eutrophication**). And groundwater can carry natural toxins: arsenic from sediments has contaminated wells used by tens of millions of people in Bangladesh, India and elsewhere, and fluoride occurs at harmful levels in parts of the East African Rift and South Asia ([WHO on arsenic](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/arsenic)).

Treating water for drinking is chemistry in sequence:

1. **Coagulation and flocculation.** Aluminium or iron(III) salts are added; at near-neutral pH they hydrolyse to gelatinous hydroxides that neutralise the charges on fine clay and organic particles and sweep them into flocs.
2. **Sedimentation and filtration** through sand, and often activated carbon to adsorb organic compounds.
3. **Disinfection**, by chlorine, chloramine, ozone or ultraviolet light. Chlorine in water forms hypochlorous acid, HOCl, a weak acid whose neutral form crosses microbial membranes and is a far stronger disinfectant than the hypochlorite ion, so the pH of the water matters. A small residual of chlorine protects water in the pipes; its reaction with natural organic matter forms by-products such as trihalomethanes, which regulations limit.

Where fresh water is scarce, from the Gulf states to Spain, Israel, Singapore and Australia, **reverse osmosis** pushes seawater through a membrane that passes water but rejects salt, at a pressure above the osmotic pressure (<LessonLink slug="solutions">Solutions</LessonLink>). The block computes both pieces of chemistry, and one number for the next section.

```python run
import math

# 1. Chlorine disinfection: HOCl <=> H+ + OCl-, pKa 7.5 at 25 °C. HOCl is the far stronger disinfectant.
pKa = 7.5
print(" pH   fraction as HOCl")
for pH in (6.0, 6.5, 7.0, 7.5, 8.0, 8.5):
    print(f"{pH:4.1f}   {1 / (1 + 10 ** (pH - pKa)):6.1%}")

# 2. Desalination by reverse osmosis: osmotic pressure of seawater (van 't Hoff) and minimum energy
R, T = 8.314, 298.15
osmolarity = 1.12e3                        # mol of dissolved particles per m^3 of seawater
pi = osmolarity * R * T                    # Pa
print(f"\nosmotic pressure of seawater = {pi / 1e5:.0f} bar")
for r in (0.01, 0.5):                      # recovery: fraction of the feed turned into fresh water
    w = pi * math.log(1 / (1 - r)) / r     # J per m^3 of fresh water, ideal reversible process
    print(f"minimum energy at {r:.0%} recovery: {w / 3.6e6:.2f} kWh per m^3 of fresh water")

# 3. How much is a trace? PFOA at 4 ng/L in drinking water
M_pfoa, N_A = 414.07, 6.022e23
molecules = 4e-9 / M_pfoa * N_A
print(f"\n4 ng/L of PFOA = {molecules:.1e} molecules per litre ({molecules * 0.25:.1e} in a 250 mL glass)")
```

At pH 7, three quarters of the free chlorine is HOCl; at pH 8.5, less than a tenth, which is why treatment plants and swimming pools control pH as carefully as chlorine dose. Seawater's osmotic pressure is about 28 bar, so plants run at 55 to 80 bar; thermodynamics sets a minimum of about 1.1 kWh per cubic metre at 50 per cent recovery, and the best plants today use two to four times that, even after energy-recovery devices have reclaimed most of the pressure energy of the outgoing brine ([Elimelech and Phillip, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1200488)). The concentrated brine must be returned to the sea without harming the ecosystems near the outfall.

<Section id="persistent" kind="human" title="Persistent pollutants: from DDT to PFAS">

Some molecules are dangerous not because they are acutely toxic but because they do not go away. **Persistent organic pollutants** resist breakdown, travel long distances in air and water, dissolve in fat and **bioaccumulate**, reaching higher concentrations at each step up a food chain. The insecticide DDT, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) from electrical equipment and the dioxins formed in poor combustion are the classic examples. The [Stockholm Convention](https://www.pops.int/) of 2001 bans or restricts them internationally, and its list grows as evidence accumulates.

**PFAS** (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are the newest large class. Their chains of CF₂ groups make them repel water and oil and survive heat, which is why they have been used in non-stick and stain-resistant coatings, food packaging, firefighting foams and industrial processes. The carbon–fluorine bond, the strongest single bond carbon makes (<LessonLink slug="solid-state-and-main-group">The solid state and main-group chemistry</LessonLink>), is also why they barely degrade in the environment: hence the nickname "forever chemicals". PFOS, PFOA and PFHxS are now listed under the Stockholm Convention. In 2024 the United States EPA set enforceable drinking-water limits of 4 ng/L each for PFOA and PFOS ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas)), and the European Union's drinking-water directive limits the sum of 20 PFAS to 0.1 µg/L from 2026; as of 2026, broader restrictions on the whole class are under discussion in Europe.

At 4 ng/L the block counts about $6 \times 10^{12}$ molecules in every litre, so "trace" does not mean "absent", but the limit is set by toxicology, not by counting. Measuring at that level is analytical chemistry at its most demanding: solid-phase extraction to concentrate the sample, liquid chromatography to separate dozens of PFAS, tandem mass spectrometry to detect them, and isotope-labelled internal standards for each, exactly the tools of <LessonLink slug="analytical-chemistry">Analytical chemistry</LessonLink>. Contamination is a constant danger, since PFAS can leach from laboratory tubing and equipment, so laboratories run blanks with every batch. Water utilities remove PFAS with granular activated carbon, ion-exchange resins or reverse osmosis, which concentrates rather than destroys them; destruction needs high-temperature incineration or newer methods such as supercritical water oxidation, electrochemical oxidation and the low-temperature chemistry reported in 2022 for one family of PFAS ([Trang et al.](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abm8868)).

</Section>

<Section id="air-quality" kind="human" title="Air quality">

Outdoor air pollution is among the largest environmental risks to health: the WHO estimates it caused about 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide in 2019 ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ambient-(outdoor)-air-quality-and-health)). Its 2021 [air quality guidelines](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240034228) set annual means of 5 µg/m³ for fine particles (**PM2.5**, particles under 2.5 µm across, which reach deep into the lungs) and 10 µg/m³ (5.3 ppb) for nitrogen dioxide.

Two kinds of smog show the chemistry. The London smog of December 1952, sulfur dioxide and soot from coal trapped under cold, still air, killed thousands of people in days and led to the United Kingdom's Clean Air Act of 1956. The **photochemical smog** first analysed in Los Angeles by Arie Haagen-Smit around 1950 is different: sunlight splits NO₂ into NO and an oxygen atom, which forms ozone; NO then destroys ozone again, so on its own the cycle builds up little. But volatile organic compounds (VOCs), oxidised by hydroxyl radicals, turn NO back into NO₂ *without* using up ozone, so with NOx, VOCs and sunshine, ground-level ozone accumulates. Ozone high in the stratosphere protects; near the ground it damages lungs and crops.

Much PM2.5 is **secondary**: it forms in the air when SO₂, NOx, ammonia from agriculture and VOCs are oxidised to sulfate, nitrate, ammonium and organic particles. Reducing it therefore means controlling gases from power plants, vehicles, farms and homes, not only visible smoke. Catalytic converters, low-sulfur fuels, flue-gas desulfurisation and the move to electric vehicles and cleaner cooking fuels have cut pollution sharply in many cities, while it remains severe in others, especially where solid fuels are burned indoors.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How environmental chemistry is done in 2026">

Environmental chemistry rests on long, careful records. The Mauna Loa CO₂ record started by Charles David Keeling in 1958 continues alongside [NOAA's global network](https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/), the AGAGE network of stations measuring dozens of halogenated gases, and the World Meteorological Organization's Global Atmosphere Watch. Satellites map NO₂, methane, ozone and aerosols daily, and are now used to find individual large methane leaks from oil and gas facilities and landfills. In the ocean, thousands of autonomous Argo floats, some carrying pH and oxygen sensors, profile the upper 2000 m.

Measurements feed **models**: chemical transport models that couple hundreds of reactions to winds and weather, run to forecast air quality, attribute emissions to their sources ("inverse modelling", as in the CFC-11 case) and test policies. In the laboratory, high-resolution mass spectrometry now finds thousands of unidentified compounds in a single water or dust sample ("non-target screening"), and toxicologists and chemists work together to decide which matter.

The chemistry of the energy transition is taken up in <LessonLink slug="energy-chemistry">The chemistry of energy</LessonLink>, and materials and processes that reduce environmental burdens in <LessonLink slug="sustainable-chemistry">Sustainable chemistry</LessonLink>.

</Section>

**Ocean acidification in a glass.** If you made the red-cabbage indicator from <LessonLink slug="acids-and-bases-around-us">Acids and bases around us</LessonLink>, ask an adult to help you pour a little into two clear glasses of tap water. <Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The ozone hole causes global warming."** They are different problems with different chemistry: the ozone hole is about chlorine and ultraviolet light, global warming about infrared-absorbing gases. They are linked mainly because CFCs and HFCs are also greenhouse gases, so the Montreal Protocol has helped the climate too.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Ocean acidification means the sea will become acidic."** Surface seawater remains alkaline, with pH around 8. "Acidification" describes the direction of change: more hydrogen ions and, crucially, fewer carbonate ions, which is what makes calcification harder.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If CO₂ is only 0.04 per cent of the air, it cannot matter."** Effect is not proportional to amount: nitrogen and oxygen do not absorb thermal infrared at all, while CO₂ absorbs strongly in a band near the peak of the Earth's emission. A trace of a substance that interacts strongly can dominate, as 3 mm of ozone does for ultraviolet.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Not detected means not there."** It means below the method's detection limit. As methods improve, substances such as PFAS are found in more places, not necessarily because they have spread, but because we can now see lower concentrations.

</Misconception>
