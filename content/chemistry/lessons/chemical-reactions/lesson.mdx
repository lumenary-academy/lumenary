<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Things that change for good">

Put a slice of bread in a toaster and it comes out brown, crisp and smelling of toast. Leave a bicycle out in the rain and orange-brown rust creeps over its chain. Light a candle and the wax slowly disappears. Stir yeast into dough and it swells up. Plants on a windowsill grow, out of little more than air, water and light. Your body is doing the same kind of thing right now, turning your breakfast into energy and into you.

All of these are **chemical reactions**: changes in which new substances are made. You can't un-toast toast. Compare that with melting an ice cube: the water can be frozen again, and it is still water.

In <LessonLink slug="molecules-and-compounds">Molecules and compounds</LessonLink> you saw that a substance's properties come from which atoms it has and how they are joined. A chemical reaction is what happens when those joins are broken and new ones are made.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Hello, I'm Chloe! Here's the question that drives this whole lesson: in a reaction, the substances change completely, but what happens to the **atoms**? Are any made, or destroyed, or lost? The answer turns out to be one of the most useful rules in all of chemistry.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Reactants, products and equations">

<Definition term="chemical reaction">

A **chemical reaction** is a change in which substances, called the **reactants**, are turned into new substances, called the **products**. During the reaction, bonds between atoms break and new bonds form, so the atoms are rearranged into new combinations. The atoms themselves are not changed.

</Definition>

<Definition term="physical change">

A **physical change** alters the form of a substance, but not what it is made of: melting, freezing, boiling, dissolving, cutting, bending. No new substance is made, and the change can often be reversed easily.

</Definition>

Chemists write reactions as **equations**. A **word equation** names the substances:

$$\text{methane} + \text{oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water}$$

This is the reaction in a gas cooker's flame. The reactants are on the left, the products on the right, and the arrow means "react to form". A **symbol equation** uses formulas instead:

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + 2\,O_2 \longrightarrow CO_2 + 2\,H_2O}$$

You will see where the 2s come from in a moment. Chemists often add **state symbols** in brackets: (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas and (aq) for "dissolved in water", from the Latin *aqua*. In a flame, all four substances above are gases: CH₄(g), O₂(g), CO₂(g), H₂O(g).

### The signs of a reaction

How can you tell that a reaction has happened? Look for clues:

- **a colour change**: a cut apple turning brown, bread toasting;
- **a gas given off**: bubbles appearing in a liquid that is not boiling, such as a fizzing antacid tablet in water;
- **a temperature change**: the mixture gets warmer or colder by itself;
- **light or a flame**: a candle, a firework in the sky;
- **a solid appearing in a liquid**: milk curdling when lemon juice is added, or white specks of limescale forming in a kettle;
- **a new smell**, such as toast just starting to burn. Notice it if it reaches you, but never sniff a reaction on purpose.

These are clues, not proof. Boiling water makes bubbles, but it is a physical change. The surest sign is that a **new substance**, with new properties, has formed.

</Section>

<Section id="conservation" kind="intuition" title="Nothing is lost, nothing is created">

When a log burns in a fire, a big heavy log turns into a small pile of light ash. Where did the rest of the wood go? For centuries, people thought burning destroyed matter, or released a mysterious substance called *phlogiston*. Yet chemists had noticed a strange thing: when metals were heated in air and turned into powdery "calx" (what we now call oxides), they got **heavier**, not lighter.

The puzzle was solved by careful weighing. In 1756 the Russian scientist **Mikhail Lomonosov** heated metals in sealed glass vessels and found that the total mass did not change. In the 1770s and 1780s the French chemist **Antoine Lavoisier** made the same kind of measurements with very precise balances, and worked out the explanation. His wife and scientific partner, **Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier**, recorded their experiments, drew the detailed illustrations of their apparatus, and translated chemists' writings from English, so that the couple knew everything being argued abroad. Lavoisier showed that burning and rusting are reactions with **oxygen** from the air: a burning or rusting metal gains mass because oxygen atoms join it.

<Theorem name="The law of conservation of mass">

In a chemical reaction, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. Atoms are rearranged, but none is created or destroyed.

</Theorem>

So the burning log does not lose matter: most of it turns into carbon dioxide and water vapour, which go up the chimney. If you could catch and weigh every bit of gas, the products would weigh exactly as much as the wood plus the oxygen it used. A rusting nail does the opposite: it takes oxygen (and water) from the air, so it gets heavier.

<Example title="Burning carbon">

When 12 g of pure carbon burns completely in oxygen, it uses 32 g of oxygen. What mass of carbon dioxide is made? By the conservation of mass, all of the atoms end up in the product, so it is $12 + 32 = 44$ g of carbon dioxide.

</Example>

### A balloon on a bottle: something to try with an adult

You can test the law in a kitchen, with an adult, using vinegar and baking soda, which react to make carbon dioxide gas.

1. Pour about three tablespoons of vinegar into a small, empty plastic bottle.
2. Using a dry funnel, put one teaspoon of baking soda into a balloon.
3. Stretch the neck of the balloon over the top of the bottle, letting the balloon hang down to the side so that no powder falls in yet.
4. Put the whole thing on a kitchen scale and write down the mass.
5. Lift the balloon so the baking soda falls into the vinegar. Watch it fizz and the balloon swell. When the fizzing stops, weigh it again.

The mass should be the same, or nearly so: the gas is still there, inside the balloon. Keep your face away from the bottle while it fizzes, and **never put a cap on a bottle with a fizzing mixture inside**: the gas has to go somewhere, which is why a stretchy balloon is used. Afterwards, pour the liquid down the sink with plenty of water.

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon, and here is a hidden assumption. As the balloon swells, it pushes aside some air, and the air pushes back up on it, just as water pushes up on a swimmer. A litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 g, so the scale may read a gram or so *less* even though no mass has gone anywhere. Lavoisier avoided this by using rigid, sealed glass vessels that could not swell.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="balancing" kind="examples" title="Balancing equations">

Because atoms are never created or destroyed, a symbol equation must have **the same number of each kind of atom on both sides**. Making that true is called **balancing** the equation.

There is one strict rule: you may change only the **coefficients**, the big numbers in front of the formulas, which count molecules. You must never change the small subscripts, because that would change the substance (as you saw with H₂O and H₂O₂).

<Example title="Hydrogen and oxygen make water">

Start with the formulas: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.

Count the atoms. Left: 2 H, 2 O. Right: 2 H, 1 O. The oxygen is unbalanced.

Put a 2 in front of the water to get 2 oxygen atoms on the right: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Now the right has 4 H, 2 O, so the hydrogen is unbalanced.

Put a 2 in front of the hydrogen: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**. Left: 4 H, 2 O. Right: 4 H, 2 O. Balanced.

</Example>

<Example title="Burning natural gas">

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. The carbon is already balanced: 1 on each side. Hydrogen: 4 on the left, 2 on the right, so put a 2 in front of the water: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Now count oxygen on the right: 2 in CO₂ and 2 in 2H₂O, which is 4. On the left, O₂ gives 2, so put a 2 in front of it: **CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O**. Check: left 1 C, 4 H, 4 O; right 1 C, 4 H, 4 O.

</Example>

A good habit is to balance the element that appears in the fewest formulas first, and leave single elements, like O₂, until last.

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="A balancing machine">

Balancing can also be done by brute force: try coefficients 1, 1, 1; then 1, 1, 2; then 1, 1, 3; and so on, counting the atoms each time, until the two sides match. That would be dull by hand, but a computer can try thousands of possibilities in a blink. This program does exactly that, for coefficients from 1 to 8:

```python run
import re
from itertools import product

def count_atoms(formula):
    """Read 'C2H5OH' into {'C': 2, 'H': 6, 'O': 1}."""
    counts = {}
    for symbol, number in re.findall(r"([A-Z][a-z]?)(\d*)", formula):
        counts[symbol] = counts.get(symbol, 0) + (int(number) if number else 1)
    return counts

def side_total(formulas, coefficients):
    """Count every atom on one side of an equation."""
    total = {}
    for formula, k in zip(formulas, coefficients):
        for symbol, n in count_atoms(formula).items():
            total[symbol] = total.get(symbol, 0) + k * n
    return total

def balance(reactants, products, biggest=8):
    """Try every set of coefficients from 1 to `biggest`; return the first that works."""
    n = len(reactants) + len(products)
    for coefficients in product(range(1, biggest + 1), repeat=n):
        left = side_total(reactants, coefficients[:len(reactants)])
        right = side_total(products, coefficients[len(reactants):])
        if left == right:
            return coefficients
    return None

def show(reactants, products):
    k = balance(reactants, products)
    term = lambda c, f: f if c == 1 else f"{c}{f}"
    left = " + ".join(term(c, f) for c, f in zip(k, reactants))
    right = " + ".join(term(c, f) for c, f in zip(k[len(reactants):], products))
    print(f"{left:22} -> {right}")

show(["H2", "O2"], ["H2O"])                     # hydrogen burning
show(["CH4", "O2"], ["CO2", "H2O"])             # natural gas burning
show(["C3H8", "O2"], ["CO2", "H2O"])            # camping gas burning
show(["Fe", "O2"], ["Fe2O3"])                   # iron rusting (simplified)
show(["C6H12O6"], ["C2H5OH", "CO2"])            # yeast fermenting sugar
show(["CO2", "H2O"], ["C6H12O6", "O2"])         # photosynthesis
```

The program finds 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O, C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O, 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃, C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ and 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Because it tries the smallest coefficients first, the answers come out in the smallest whole numbers.

<Narrator who="margaret">

I'm Margaret. Brute force has a cost! With four formulas and coefficients up to 8, there are $8 \times 8 \times 8 \times 8 = 4096$ combinations to try, which is nothing for a computer. But a big equation with ten formulas would have $8^{10}$, more than a thousand million. Chemists' software balances equations with algebra instead: one equation for each element, solved all at once.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="everyday" kind="human" title="Burning, rusting, cooking and baking">

**Burning** (or **combustion**) is a fast reaction between a fuel and oxygen that gives out heat and light. Fuels such as natural gas, petrol, candle wax and wood contain carbon and hydrogen, which end up as carbon dioxide and water. A fire needs three things, often drawn as a **fire triangle**: fuel, oxygen and heat. Take away any one and the fire goes out. That is why a fire blanket works (it cuts off oxygen) and why water works on a wood fire (it takes away heat).

**Rusting** is a slow reaction of iron with oxygen **and** water, making hydrated iron oxide, the flaky orange-brown rust. Salt water speeds it up, which is why cars rust faster where roads are salted in winter and near the sea. Paint, oil, plastic coatings and a thin layer of zinc ("galvanising") protect iron by keeping air and water away. Engineers spend a great deal of effort on stopping corrosion; you can read more in [fatigue, creep and corrosion](/en/materials/fatigue-creep-and-corrosion/) in Lumenary Materials, and about the electricity involved in <LessonLink slug="electrochemistry">Electrochemistry</LessonLink>.

**Cooking** is full of reactions. When bread toasts, onions brown or meat is grilled, sugars and proteins in the food react together to make hundreds of new substances with new colours and flavours. These are called **Maillard reactions**, after the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard, who studied them in 1912. When an egg cooks, the proteins in the clear egg white unfold and link up with each other, which is why it turns white and solid and can never go back.

**Baking** uses gas-making reactions. Baking powder contains baking soda and a dry acid; once they are wet and warm, they react and release carbon dioxide, whose bubbles make a cake rise. Bread uses living yeast instead: it **ferments** sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide (C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂), the carbon dioxide puffs up the dough, and the ethanol evaporates in the oven.

### Energy out, energy in

Every reaction involves energy, because breaking bonds takes energy and making bonds releases it.

<Definition term="exothermic and endothermic">

An **exothermic** reaction gives out energy to its surroundings, usually as heat (and sometimes light), so the surroundings warm up. An **endothermic** reaction takes in energy from its surroundings, so they cool down, or it needs a constant supply of heat or light to keep going.

</Definition>

Burning is exothermic, and so is **respiration**, the reaction in your cells that releases energy from food: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O. Hand warmers contain iron powder that rusts quickly inside its packet, giving out heat. **Photosynthesis**, the reverse of respiration, is endothermic: plants take in the energy of sunlight to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. Even the vinegar and baking-soda reaction is slightly endothermic: the mixture feels a little colder.

The hump explains something familiar. Paper and wood are exothermic fuels, yet they do not burst into flames by themselves: they need a push, such as the heat of a match, to get the reaction started. Once started, the energy given out keeps it going. How big the hump is, and how to make it smaller, is the subject of <LessonLink slug="chemical-kinetics">Chemical kinetics</LessonLink>; how much energy comes out is measured in <LessonLink slug="thermochemistry">Thermochemistry</LessonLink>.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"When something burns, it is destroyed."** The atoms are all still there, mostly as invisible gases (carbon dioxide and water vapour). Matter changes form; it does not vanish.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Dissolving sugar in tea is a chemical reaction."** Dissolving is usually a physical change: the sugar molecules spread out among the water molecules but are not changed. Evaporate the water and you get the sugar back.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"To balance an equation, change the small numbers."** Changing a subscript changes the substance: H₂O₂ is not water. Balance only by changing the coefficients in front.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Rust is made by air alone."** Iron needs both oxygen and water to rust. In very dry air, such as in a desert or a sealed box with a drying agent, iron stays shiny for a very long time.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A reaction that gives out heat should start by itself."** Most exothermic reactions still need a push to get started: that is the hump in the energy diagram.

</Misconception>

</Section>
