<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Left hands and right hands">

Hold your hands up, palms facing you. They are made of the same parts, connected in the same order, and each is the mirror image of the other; yet no amount of turning will make a left hand fit a right-handed glove. Many molecules have the same property. Carvone, a small ketone found in plants, comes in two mirror-image forms: one gives spearmint its characteristic odour and the other gives caraway seeds theirs. Our noses can tell them apart because the receptors in them are made of proteins, and proteins are built from amino acids that are all of one handedness.

The same is true of medicines. A drug acts by fitting into a protein, an enzyme or a receptor, and a protein is a handed object, like a glove. Two mirror-image forms of a drug can therefore have different potency, different side effects or different fates in the body. Since the 1990s regulators in many countries have expected drug developers to study each mirror-image form separately, and most new medicines whose molecules are handed are now developed as a single form.

<LessonLink slug="carbon-and-functional-groups">The previous lesson</LessonLink> counted molecules by their connections. This one adds the third dimension: how a molecule's parts twist relative to each other (conformations), how the same connections can be arranged in space in different, non-interconverting ways (configurations), and how chemists name, count and measure those arrangements.

</Section>

<Section id="conformations" kind="intuition" title="Conformations: molecules that twist">

Rotation about a C–C single bond does not break the bond, so the two ends of ethane can spin relative to each other. The arrangements reached by such rotations are **conformations**. They are not equally comfortable. Looking along the C–C bond (a **Newman projection**), the hydrogens on the front carbon can sit between those on the back one (**staggered**) or line up with them (**eclipsed**). Eclipsed ethane is higher in energy by about 12 kJ/mol, mostly because the C–H bonding electrons on the two carbons repel each other.

For butane, CH₃CH₂–CH₂CH₃, the rotation about the central bond matters more, because the two methyl groups can bump into each other.

**Rings** cannot rotate freely, so their shapes are compromises between bond angles, eclipsing and crowding. Cyclopropane's 60° angles are forced far from 109.5°, and the ring carries about 115 kJ/mol of strain, which is why it opens so readily. Cyclohexane escapes strain almost completely by puckering into a **chair**, in which every angle is close to tetrahedral and every bond is staggered. Each carbon of the chair has one bond parallel to the ring's axis (**axial**) and one pointing outwards around its equator (**equatorial**).

The two chairs interconvert by a **ring flip** over a barrier of about 45 kJ/mol, around a hundred thousand times a second at room temperature. In cyclohexane itself the two chairs are identical, but a substituent makes them different: an axial group is crowded by the two other axial hydrogens on the same face, so most substituents prefer to be equatorial. The free-energy preference is called the substituent's **A-value**: about 7.3 kJ/mol for a methyl group and about 20 kJ/mol for the bulky *tert*-butyl group. Statistical mechanics (<LessonLink slug="statistical-thermodynamics">statistical thermodynamics</LessonLink>) turns those energies into populations through the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\Delta E/RT}$, and transition-state theory (<LessonLink slug="chemical-kinetics">chemical kinetics</LessonLink>) turns barriers into rates through the Eyring equation $k = (k_\mathrm{B}T/h)\,e^{-\Delta G^\ddagger/RT}$.

```python run
import numpy as np

R = 8.314462618        # gas constant, J/(mol K)
kB = 1.380649e-23      # Boltzmann constant, J/K
h = 6.62607015e-34     # Planck constant, J s
T = 298.15

# 1. Ethane: torsional energy V(phi) = (V3/2)(1 + cos 3 phi), barrier V3 about 12 kJ/mol.
V3 = 12.0e3
for phi in range(0, 121, 15):
    V = V3 / 2 * (1 + np.cos(np.radians(3 * phi)))
    print(f"phi = {phi:3d} deg  V = {V / 1000:5.1f} kJ/mol  " + "#" * int(round(V / 500)))

# How often does a methyl group turn past the barrier? Eyring: k = (kB T / h) exp(-dG / RT)
k = kB * T / h * np.exp(-V3 / (R * T))
print(f"ethane: about {k:.0e} turns per second at 25 C")

# 2. Butane: one anti conformer and two gauche conformers, 3.8 kJ/mol higher.
dE = 3.8e3
for TC in [-100, 25, 200]:
    w = 2 * np.exp(-dE / (R * (TC + 273.15)))     # two gauche, Boltzmann-weighted
    print(f"butane at {TC:4d} C: {1 / (1 + w):.0%} anti, {w / (1 + w):.0%} gauche")

# 3. Cyclohexanes: a substituent prefers the equatorial position by its A-value.
for group, A in [("methyl", 7.3e3), ("tert-butyl", 20e3)]:
    K = np.exp(A / (R * T))
    print(f"{group:10}: K(equatorial/axial) = {K:6.0f}, so {K / (1 + K):.2%} equatorial")

# 4. What barrier makes two forms separable? Ask for a half-life of 1000 s at 300 K.
k_slow = np.log(2) / 1000
dG = R * 300 * np.log(kB * 300 / (h * k_slow))
print(f"barrier for a 1000 s half-life at 300 K: {dG / 1000:.0f} kJ/mol")
```

At room temperature butane is about 70% anti and 30% gauche, and methylcyclohexane is 95% equatorial; a *tert*-butyl group is equatorial 99.97% of the time, so firmly that chemists use it to lock a ring in one chair.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The rate is the striking number: an ethane molecule turns past its barrier about $5 \times 10^{10}$ times a second. That is why conformers cannot be bottled separately at room temperature. To keep two forms apart for even a quarter of an hour (a half-life of 1000 s at 300 K), the barrier must be about 92 kJ/mol, nearly eight times ethane's.

</Narrator>

That threshold separates **conformations**, which interconvert by rotation, from **configurations**, which interconvert only by breaking bonds (barriers of several hundred kJ/mol) and so stay put indefinitely. Between them lie **atropisomers**: molecules such as some crowded biaryls whose rotation is so hindered that the two twisted forms can be separated. Their handedness has been exploited in chiral catalysts such as BINAP, which we meet again in <LessonLink slug="synthesis-and-retrosynthesis">synthesis</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="chirality" kind="definitions" title="Chirality and enantiomers">

<Definition term="chirality">

An object or molecule is **chiral** if it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image, and **achiral** if it can. A chiral molecule and its mirror image are **enantiomers**. Stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other are **diastereomers**.

</Definition>

The commonest source of chirality is a **stereocentre**: a carbon (or other atom) bonded to four different groups. Butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃, has one: its C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and CH₂CH₃. Swapping any two of those groups produces the mirror image, and no rotation can undo the swap.

Enantiomers have identical boiling points, melting points, solubilities, spectra and reaction rates with achiral partners: in a mirror-symmetric world they are indistinguishable. They differ only in how they interact with other chiral things, such as plane-polarised light, a chiral catalyst or a protein. Diastereomers, by contrast, are simply different compounds, with different physical properties, and can be separated by ordinary methods such as crystallisation or chromatography.

<Narrator who="gideon">

"Four different groups" is a sufficient test for a single stereocentre, not the definition of chirality. The precise criterion is symmetry: a rigid molecule is achiral exactly when it has an improper rotation axis (a mirror plane, a centre of inversion, or a rotation followed by a reflection). Molecules with two or more stereocentres can be achiral (the meso compounds below), and molecules with none can be chiral, like the twisted biaryls and the helical hexahelicene.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="cip" kind="examples" title="Naming configurations: the CIP rules">

To say which enantiomer we mean, we need a label that anyone can work out from the structure. Robert Cahn, Christopher Ingold and Vladimir Prelog published the system still used today in 1956, refined in 1966 and 1982.

**Step 1: rank the four groups.**

1. Higher atomic number of the atom directly attached outranks lower: I > Br > Cl > S > F > O > N > C > H. (For isotopes, higher mass wins: D > H.)
2. If two attached atoms tie, compare the sets of atoms attached to *them*, each set listed in decreasing order, at the first point of difference; if they still tie, move outwards one sphere at a time.
3. A double or triple bond counts as two or three single bonds to duplicate atoms: –CH=O is ranked as C(O, O, H), and –C≡N as C(N, N, N).

**Step 2: look at the molecule with the lowest-ranked group pointing away from you**, and follow the other three in order 1 → 2 → 3. Clockwise is **R** (Latin *rectus*, right); anticlockwise is **S** (*sinister*, left).

<Example title="Butan-2-ol">

The groups on C2 are OH, CH₂CH₃, CH₃ and H. Oxygen beats carbon, so OH is 1 and H is 4. The two carbons tie at the first atom, so compare what they carry: the ethyl carbon has (C, H, H) and the methyl carbon (H, H, H); at the first point of difference C beats H, so ethyl is 2 and methyl is 3. In the left-hand molecule of the figure, H points away (dashed bond), and OH (top) → ethyl (lower right) → methyl (lower left) runs clockwise: **(R)-butan-2-ol**.

</Example>

<Example title="Comparing groups one sphere further out">

Rank –CH₂OH, –CH(CH₃)₂, –CHO and –COOH. All four attach through carbon, so list each carbon's substituents: –COOH is C(O, O, O) (the C=O is counted as two oxygens), –CHO is C(O, O, H), –CH₂OH is C(O, H, H) and –CH(CH₃)₂ is C(C, C, H). Comparing the highest atom first, then the next: **–COOH > –CHO > –CH₂OH > –CH(CH₃)₂**. Notice that a single oxygen beats two carbons: the comparison is made at the first point of difference, not by adding up masses.

</Example>

Chemists also draw stereocentres as **Fischer projections**: a cross in which the horizontal bonds point towards the viewer and the vertical bonds away. Natural amino acids are written with COOH at the top and the side chain at the bottom; when NH₂ is on the left, the amino acid is called L. L-Alanine turns out to be (S), and so are all the common L-amino acids except cysteine, where the CH₂SH side chain outranks COOH because sulfur beats oxygen. The D/L labels come from comparison with glyceraldehyde and are still used for sugars and amino acids; R/S is the general system.

A computer assigns R or S from three-dimensional coordinates without "looking" at anything. Put the stereocentre at the origin and let $\mathbf a, \mathbf b, \mathbf c, \mathbf d$ be the positions of the groups ranked 1 to 4. The sign of the **signed volume** $(\mathbf a - \mathbf d)\cdot[(\mathbf b - \mathbf d)\times(\mathbf c - \mathbf d)]$ says which way $1 \to 2 \to 3$ turns when seen from the side opposite group 4.

```python run
import numpy as np

def descriptor(p1, p2, p3, p4):
    """R or S for a stereocentre at the origin, given the positions of its four
    substituents in CIP priority order (p1 highest, p4 lowest)."""
    a, b, c, d = (np.asarray(p, dtype=float) for p in (p1, p2, p3, p4))
    # Signed volume of the tetrahedron 1-2-3 measured from 4: its sign says whether
    # 1 -> 2 -> 3 runs clockwise or anticlockwise when 4 points away from the viewer.
    volume = np.dot(a - d, np.cross(b - d, c - d))
    return "R" if volume < 0 else "S"

# A Fischer projection: vertical bonds go behind the page (z < 0), horizontal ones come out (z > 0).
up, down = (0, 1, -0.7), (0, -1, -0.7)
left, right = (-1, 0, 0.7), (1, 0, 0.7)

# L-alanine: COOH up, CH3 down, NH2 left, H right.  Priorities: NH2 > COOH > CH3 > H.
print("L-alanine:", descriptor(left, up, down, right))
# D-glyceraldehyde: CHO up, CH2OH down, OH right, H left.  Priorities: OH > CHO > CH2OH > H.
print("D-glyceraldehyde:", descriptor(right, up, down, left))

# A mirror flips the sign of one coordinate, and so flips the descriptor.
mirror = lambda p: (-p[0], p[1], p[2])
print("mirror image of L-alanine:", descriptor(*(mirror(p) for p in (left, up, down, right))))

# Turning the whole molecule (a proper rotation) never changes the descriptor.
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(3, 3)))
if np.linalg.det(Q) < 0:
    Q[:, 0] = -Q[:, 0]                      # make it a rotation, not a reflection
print("L-alanine, turned at random:", descriptor(*(Q @ np.array(p) for p in (left, up, down, right))))

# Swapping any two substituents gives the enantiomer.
print("swap CH3 and H:", descriptor(left, up, right, down))
```

The program confirms that L-alanine is (S) and D-glyceraldehyde is (R), and it checks the two facts the whole system rests on: a rotation (determinant +1) never changes the label, and a reflection, or the swap of two groups, always does.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why does a triple product detect handedness? Because it is a determinant, and the sign of a determinant is exactly what rotations preserve and reflections reverse. Chirality is the chemist's name for the fact that the group of all symmetries of space has two pieces, the rotations and the rotations combined with a reflection, and that a chiral molecule's symmetries all lie in the first (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.groups">groups</TopicLink>).

</Narrator>

<Section id="diastereomers" kind="examples" title="Diastereomers, meso compounds and counting">

A molecule with $n$ stereocentres can have at most $2^n$ stereoisomers, since each centre is independently R or S. Cholesterol has eight stereocentres, so $2^8 = 256$ possible stereoisomers, of which living things make just one. Two stereoisomers with *every* centre inverted are enantiomers; any other pair are diastereomers.

The bound $2^n$ is reached only when the molecule has no internal symmetry. **Tartaric acid**, HOOC–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–COOH, has two stereocentres but only three stereoisomers: the (2R,3R) and (2S,3S) enantiomers, and a (2R,3S) form that has a mirror plane through the middle of the molecule. A molecule like that, with stereocentres but achiral overall, is a **meso compound**. Tartaric acid has a special place in the history of the subject: in 1848 Louis Pasteur noticed that crystals of a salt of the optically inactive "racemic acid" from wine-making came in two mirror-image shapes, separated them by hand under a microscope, and found that their solutions rotated polarised light in opposite directions. It was the first separation of enantiomers, and the first strong evidence that molecules themselves are handed.

Double bonds are stereogenic too. In but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃, the two methyl groups can be on the same side of the double bond or on opposite sides, and the π bond prevents rotation between them. Ranking the two groups on each carbon by the CIP rules, the isomer with the two higher-ranked groups on the same side is **Z** (German *zusammen*, together) and the other **E** (*entgegen*, opposite). Vision depends on exactly this: absorbing a photon converts the 11-*cis* (11Z) form of retinal in the eye's rhodopsin into the all-*trans* form, and that change of shape starts the nerve signal.

For a chain whose two ends are identical, counting needs care. In a Fischer projection, a chain of stereocentres is a string of "OH on the left" and "OH on the right". If the ends differ, as in the aldose sugars, every string is a different molecule. If the ends are the same, as in the sugar alcohols (alditols) HOCH₂–(CHOH)$_n$–CH₂OH, turning the drawing upside down reverses the string and moves every OH to the other side, yet gives the same molecule, so some strings must be counted together.

```python run
from itertools import product

# A chain with n stereocentres in a Fischer projection: each centre's OH is on the
# left (L) or the right (R) of the vertical chain, read from top to bottom.
flip = {"L": "R", "R": "L"}

def mirror(s):            # reflect in a vertical mirror: every OH changes side
    return tuple(flip[c] for c in s)

def turn(s):              # rotate the drawing 180 degrees in the page: same molecule
    return tuple(reversed(mirror(s)))

def count(n, symmetric):
    """Stereoisomers of a chain with n stereocentres. `symmetric` means the two ends
    are identical (HOCH2-...-CH2OH), so turning the drawing upside down is allowed."""
    seen, total, meso = set(), 0, 0
    for s in product("LR", repeat=n):
        same = {s, turn(s)} if symmetric else {s}
        key = min(same)
        if key in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(key)
        total += 1
        if mirror(s) in same:           # its mirror image is itself: achiral
            meso += 1
    return total, meso

print("centres   ends differ (aldoses)   ends the same (alditols)")
for n in range(2, 7):
    a, _ = count(n, symmetric=False)
    t, m = count(n, symmetric=True)
    pairs = (t - m) // 2
    print(f"{n:5}     {a:6}                  {t:4} = {m} meso + {pairs} enantiomer pair" + "s" * (pairs != 1))
```

The counts match what chemists have isolated. With four stereocentres there are 16 aldohexoses (D- and L-glucose, mannose, galactose and the rest) but only 10 hexitols: two meso compounds (allitol and galactitol) and four enantiomer pairs (glucitol, also called sorbitol; mannitol; iditol; and altritol). With three centres the 4 pentitols are ribitol and xylitol, both meso, and the two arabinitols. Xylitol, the sweetener, is achiral even though it has stereocentres.

<Narrator who="chloe">

This is Burnside's lemma in disguise. The symmetry group here has two elements, "do nothing" and "turn upside down", and the number of distinct molecules is the average number of strings each element leaves unchanged: $(2^n + 2^{n/2})/2$ for even $n$ and $2^n/2$ for odd $n$, since a turned string can equal itself only when the length is even. For $n = 4$ that is $(16 + 4)/2 = 10$.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="optical-activity" kind="human" title="Optical rotation and enantiomeric excess">

Light whose electric field oscillates in a single plane is **plane-polarised**. Passing through a solution of one enantiomer, its plane turns by a small angle; the other enantiomer turns it by the same angle in the opposite direction, and a 50:50 mixture (a **racemate**) does not turn it at all. Jean-Baptiste Biot discovered the effect in solutions of natural substances in 1815.

The observed rotation $\alpha$ is proportional to the path length and the concentration, so a compound is characterised by its **specific rotation**

$$[\alpha]^{T}_{\lambda} = \frac{\alpha}{l\,c},$$

with $l$ in decimetres and $c$ in grams per millilitre, measured at a stated temperature and wavelength (usually the sodium D line, 589 nm). Sucrose, for example, has $[\alpha]_\mathrm{D} = +66.5$. A compound that turns the plane clockwise, as seen by an observer looking towards the light source, is **dextrorotatory**, written (+); anticlockwise is **laevorotatory**, (−).

A sample that is mostly one enantiomer is described by its **enantiomeric excess**, $ee = |x_\text{major} - x_\text{minor}|$, the excess of one form over the racemic background. Because the minor enantiomer cancels an equal amount of the major one, $ee$ equals the measured specific rotation divided by that of the pure enantiomer. A sample with $ee = 60\%$ is 80% of one enantiomer and 20% of the other.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Two warnings about polarimetry. The sign of rotation is a measured property, not a consequence of the R/S label: (R)-glyceraldehyde is (+) while (R)-lactic acid is (−), and a single compound's sign can even change with solvent or wavelength. And the "specific rotation" is conventionally quoted without units even though $\alpha/(lc)$ has units of degrees per (dm·g/mL); quoting it in other units without saying so gives numbers that look a hundred times wrong.

</Narrator>

In modern laboratories $ee$ is rarely measured by polarimetry, which needs pure reference samples and is sensitive to impurities. It is measured by **chiral chromatography**, in which the column itself is made of a chiral material that holds one enantiomer slightly longer than the other, so the two emerge as separate peaks whose areas give the ratio directly.

</Section>

<Section id="medicine" kind="human" title="Why chirality matters in medicine">

Enzymes and receptors are chiral, so the two enantiomers of a drug are, to the body, two different compounds. Sometimes one is active and the other is simply inactive weight: the anti-inflammatory naproxen is sold as its (S)-enantiomer. Sometimes the body converts one into the other: ibuprofen is usually sold as the racemate, and the body turns much of the less active (R)-enantiomer into the active (S)-form by a one-way metabolic inversion. Several medicines were later re-developed as single enantiomers of earlier racemic drugs (so-called chiral switches), for example esomeprazole from omeprazole and escitalopram from citalopram.

**Thalidomide** is the case that changed medicine, and its history deserves to be told accurately. It was developed by the West German company Chemie Grünenthal and marketed from 1957 as a sedative, and was widely taken by pregnant women for morning sickness. By 1961 doctors, notably Widukind Lenz in Germany and William McBride in Australia, had linked it to severe birth defects, especially shortened or missing limbs; it was withdrawn from most markets in 1961 and 1962. More than ten thousand children were affected worldwide, many of whom did not survive infancy; many survivors are alive today and have campaigned for recognition and support. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration reviewer Frances Oldham Kelsey had refused to approve the drug without better safety evidence, and the 1962 Kefauver–Harris Amendments that followed required drugs to be shown effective as well as safe, with much stronger testing. Most countries' modern drug regulation traces back to this episode.

The stereochemical part of the story is often told wrongly. Thalidomide has one stereocentre and was sold as the racemate. Some animal studies associated the birth defects mainly with the (S)-enantiomer, and it is tempting to conclude that selling only the (R)-enantiomer would have prevented the tragedy. It would not: the hydrogen on thalidomide's stereocentre is acidic, and in the body the two enantiomers interconvert within hours, so a dose of either becomes a mixture of both. In 2010 Japanese researchers identified the protein cereblon as thalidomide's main target. Under strict pregnancy-prevention programmes, thalidomide and its relatives lenalidomide and pomalidomide are now used to treat multiple myeloma and a complication of leprosy, and the way they redirect cereblon has inspired a new class of medicines, the targeted protein degraders discussed in <LessonLink slug="medicinal-chemistry">medicinal chemistry</LessonLink>.

Today, regulators such as the US FDA (whose 1992 policy statement on stereoisomeric drugs set the pattern) and the European Medicines Agency expect a company developing a chiral drug to know the stereochemistry of what it is making, to study the enantiomers' activity and fate in the body, and to justify any decision to market a racemate. Making single enantiomers efficiently, by asymmetric catalysis rather than by separating mixtures, is a central theme of <LessonLink slug="synthesis-and-retrosynthesis">modern synthesis</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="reading" title="Stereochemistry in practice">

How do chemists know which enantiomer they have? **X-ray crystallography** of a single crystal gives the full three-dimensional structure, and by measuring the small differences that anomalous scattering creates between mirror-related reflections, the absolute configuration too; Johannes Bijvoet first did this in 1951, for a salt of tartaric acid, confirming that the configurations chemists had guessed decades earlier happened to be right. **Vibrational circular dichroism**, the difference in infrared absorption of left- and right-circularly polarised light, compared with a quantum-chemical calculation (see <LessonLink slug="computational-chemistry">computational chemistry</LessonLink>), assigns configurations of molecules that will not crystallise. **NMR** distinguishes diastereomers directly and enantiomers after they are attached to a chiral partner.

Software handles the bookkeeping. Structure files store stereochemistry as wedge bonds or as atom parities, SMILES writes it with `@` and `@@`, and toolkits perceive the stereocentres and assign CIP labels:

```python
# Not run here: needs RDKit (pip install rdkit), which is not available in the browser.
from rdkit import Chem

for smiles in ["C[C@@H](O)CC", "C[C@H](N)C(=O)O", "O[C@@H]([C@@H](O)C(=O)O)C(=O)O"]:
    mol = Chem.MolFromSmiles(smiles)
    print(smiles, Chem.FindMolChiralCenters(mol, includeUnassigned=True, useLegacyImplementation=False))
```

The CIP rules have subtle corners, especially for large and symmetric molecules. A 2018 study by developers of several cheminformatics packages found that widely used programs disagreed with each other, and with the rules, on a noticeable fraction of complicated structures; it proposed clarifications of the rules, and several programs have since adopted new implementations. For everyday molecules the hand method of this lesson is reliable.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"R means it rotates light to the right."** R and S come from a ranking rule; (+) and (−) come from a measurement. They are unrelated, and the same configuration can be (+) in one compound and (−) in the next.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Gauche butane and anti butane are two isomers."** They are two conformations of one compound, interconverting billions of times a second. Isomers, including stereoisomers, are different compounds that do not interconvert under ordinary conditions.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A molecule with stereocentres is always chiral."** Meso compounds such as (2R,3S)-tartaric acid and xylitol have stereocentres yet are superimposable on their mirror images, because an internal mirror plane relates the two halves.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Thalidomide's harm came from the wrong enantiomer, so single-enantiomer drugs are always safe."** Thalidomide racemises in the body, so a pure enantiomer would not have helped; and many single-enantiomer drugs carry their own risks. Chirality is one part of drug safety, not a substitute for testing.

</Misconception>
