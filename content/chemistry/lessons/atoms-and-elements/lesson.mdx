<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="How small can you go?">

Take a gold ring and imagine cutting it in half. Then cut one half in half again, and again, and again. Could you go on for ever, or would you reach a smallest possible piece of gold, one that could not be cut without stopping being gold?

The answer is that there is a smallest piece. It is called an **atom** of gold, and it is unimaginably small: a gold atom is about 0.29 millionths of a millimetre across, so a line of some 35 million of them, side by side, would stretch just one centimetre. Everything you have ever seen, touched or eaten is made of atoms. More surprising still, there are only a little over a hundred different kinds of atom. Every substance in the universe, from water to wood to the stars, is built from them, in the same way that every word in this lesson is built from 26 letters.

Each kind of atom is called an **element**. This lesson is about what atoms are, what is inside them, and how chemists organise the elements into one of the most useful charts ever made: the periodic table.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="From Democritus to Dalton">

The idea of atoms is very old. In Greece, about 2,400 years ago, the philosopher **Democritus** and his teacher Leucippus argued that matter could not be divided for ever: in the end you would reach tiny, solid pieces that could not be cut. They called them *atomos*, "uncuttable". In ancient India, the Vaisheshika school of philosophy, traditionally traced to the sage Kanada, also taught that the world is built from tiny, eternal particles called *paramāṇu*.

These were brilliant guesses, but nobody could test them, and for nearly two thousand years most thinkers followed Aristotle instead, who taught that everything is made of four "elements": earth, water, air and fire. Meanwhile, practical chemists were learning a great deal. Scholars in the Islamic world, such as Jabir ibn Hayyan and Abu Bakr al-Razi, developed careful laboratory methods like distillation and crystallisation, and sorted substances into groups by their properties.

Slowly, experiments replaced guesses. In 1661 the Irish-born chemist **Robert Boyle** argued that an element should mean a substance that cannot be broken down into anything simpler. In 1789 the French chemist **Antoine Lavoisier** published a list of 33 such "simple substances". Many are on today's list, such as oxygen, hydrogen, sulfur and gold, though his list also included light and heat, which we now know are not substances at all.

Then, in 1808, the English schoolteacher **John Dalton** brought the two ideas together. He proposed that:

1. every element is made of its own kind of atom;
2. atoms of different elements have different masses;
3. compounds are made when atoms of different elements join in small whole-number ratios;
4. in a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed.

Dalton's atomic theory explained why substances always combine in fixed proportions, and it made the atom the centre of chemistry. It also needed a correction a century later, as you will see in the section on isotopes.

</Section>

<Section id="inside" kind="definitions" title="Inside the atom">

Dalton thought atoms were solid little balls. Around 1900, physicists discovered that atoms have parts. In 1897 J. J. Thomson found the **electron**, a tiny particle with a negative electric charge. In 1911 Ernest Rutherford, from New Zealand, working in England with Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, showed that almost all of an atom's mass is packed into a tiny, positively charged centre: the **nucleus**. The positive particles in the nucleus were named **protons**, and in 1932 James Chadwick found the **neutron**, which sits in the nucleus too but has no charge.

<Definition term="protons, neutrons and electrons">

An atom has a tiny central **nucleus** made of **protons** (each with a charge of +1) and **neutrons** (no charge), surrounded by **electrons** (each with a charge of −1). A proton and a neutron have almost the same mass; an electron is about 1836 times lighter than a proton, so nearly all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus.

</Definition>

Two numbers describe an atom:

<Definition term="atomic number and mass number">

The **atomic number**, written $Z$, is the number of protons in the nucleus. The **mass number**, written $A$, is the number of protons plus the number of neutrons. So the number of neutrons is $A - Z$. An atom is written with its mass number after its name, as in carbon-12, or as a small number before its symbol: ¹²C.

</Definition>

Here is the answer to Chloe's question. **The atomic number decides the element.** Every atom with 79 protons is gold, every atom with 6 protons is carbon, and every atom with 1 proton is hydrogen. Change the number of protons and you have a different element.

In a whole atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the positive and negative charges balance and the atom has no overall charge. An atom can lose or gain electrons, though, and then it has a charge and is called an **ion**. A sodium atom (11 protons) that loses one electron becomes a sodium ion, Na⁺, with 10 electrons. It is still sodium, because it still has 11 protons. Ions are important in <LessonLink slug="molecules-and-compounds">Molecules and compounds</LessonLink>.

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon, and here's the edge case that trips people up. Electrons can come and go (that makes ions), and neutrons can differ (that makes isotopes, coming next), but the element stays the same. Only the number of **protons** fixes the element. Change it, which happens only in nuclear reactions, never in chemistry, and the atom becomes a different element.

</Narrator>

How empty is an atom? If an atom were blown up to the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot, with the electrons spread through the whole stadium.

</Section>

<Section id="isotopes" kind="examples" title="Isotopes, and masses that aren't whole numbers">

Dalton thought all atoms of an element are identical. They are not quite. Atoms of the same element always have the same number of protons, but they can have different numbers of neutrons.

<Definition term="isotopes">

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (the same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, and so different mass numbers. Isotopes of an element behave almost identically in chemical reactions, but have slightly different masses.

</Definition>

<Example title="Three carbons">

- **Carbon-12**: 6 protons, 6 neutrons. About 99 out of every 100 carbon atoms.
- **Carbon-13**: 6 protons, 7 neutrons. About 1 in 100.
- **Carbon-14**: 6 protons, 8 neutrons. A tiny trace, made in the upper atmosphere. It is **radioactive**: its nuclei slowly break down, half of them every 5,730 years or so. Living things take in carbon-14 while they are alive; after they die, it slowly disappears. Measuring how much is left lets archaeologists date bones, wood and cloth up to about 50,000 years old. This is **radiocarbon dating**, developed by the American chemist Willard Libby in the 1940s.

</Example>

Because most elements are a mixture of isotopes, the average mass of their atoms is usually not a whole number. Masses of atoms are measured in **atomic mass units** (u); a proton or a neutron has a mass of about 1 u.

<Example title="Why chlorine's atomic mass is 35.45">

Natural chlorine is about 76 % chlorine-35 and 24 % chlorine-37. The average mass of a chlorine atom is therefore between 35 and 37, and closer to 35, because there is more chlorine-35. The program below works it out: **35.45 u**, the number you will see under chlorine in a periodic table. The same idea, an average weighted by how common each isotope is, gives every element its **relative atomic mass**.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Counting the particles">

The rules are simple enough for a computer. For each atom, the program finds the protons from the atomic number, the neutrons by subtracting it from the mass number, and the electrons (for a neutral atom) from the protons. Then it computes chlorine's average mass, and counts the atoms in a drop of water. Press **Run**:

```python run
# (name, symbol, atomic number Z, mass number A)
atoms = [
    ("hydrogen-1", "H", 1, 1),
    ("helium-4", "He", 2, 4),
    ("carbon-12", "C", 6, 12),
    ("carbon-14", "C", 6, 14),
    ("oxygen-16", "O", 8, 16),
    ("iron-56", "Fe", 26, 56),
    ("gold-197", "Au", 79, 197),
    ("uranium-238", "U", 92, 238),
]

print(f"{'atom':12} {'symbol':>6} {'protons':>8} {'neutrons':>9} {'electrons':>10}")
for name, symbol, Z, A in atoms:
    protons = Z               # the atomic number IS the number of protons
    neutrons = A - Z          # the rest of the mass number
    electrons = Z             # a neutral atom: as many electrons as protons
    print(f"{name:12} {symbol:>6} {protons:8d} {neutrons:9d} {electrons:10d}")

# Chlorine in nature is a mixture of two isotopes
cl35_mass, cl35_share = 34.968853, 0.7576   # mass in u, fraction of atoms
cl37_mass, cl37_share = 36.965903, 0.2424
average = cl35_mass * cl35_share + cl37_mass * cl37_share
print(f"\nAverage mass of a chlorine atom: {average:.2f} u")

# How many atoms in one drop of water (0.05 g)?
u_in_grams = 1.66054e-24                    # one atomic mass unit, in grams
water_molecule = 18.015 * u_in_grams        # H2O: two H and one O
molecules = 0.05 / water_molecule
print(f"Water molecules in a 0.05 g drop: {molecules:.1e}")
print(f"Atoms in that drop (3 per molecule): {3 * molecules:.1e}")

years = 3 * molecules / (365.25 * 24 * 3600)  # counting one atom per second
print(f"Years to count them, one per second: {years:.1e}")
print(f"That is {years / 13.8e9:,.0f} times the age of the universe")
```

Notice that heavy atoms have more neutrons than protons: gold-197 has 79 protons but 118 neutrons, and uranium-238 has 92 protons and 146 neutrons. The extra neutrons help hold a large nucleus together.

<Section id="periodic-table" kind="human" title="The periodic table: a map of the elements">

By the 1860s, chemists knew about 63 elements, and had noticed families among them. Lithium, sodium and potassium, for example, are all soft, shiny metals that react quickly with water and air (so quickly that they are stored under oil). Fluorine, chlorine and bromine are all poisonous, strongly reacting non-metals. Several chemists tried to arrange the elements so that the families lined up.

In 1869 the Russian chemist **Dmitri Mendeleev** succeeded. He wrote the elements in order of their atomic masses, but started a new row whenever the properties began to repeat, so that elements with similar properties fell into the same column. (The German chemist Lothar Meyer made a similar table at about the same time.) Mendeleev's boldest step was to **leave gaps**. Where no known element fitted, he said that an element must exist but had not been discovered yet, and he predicted its properties from its neighbours.

The predictions came true. In 1875 the French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered **gallium**, Mendeleev's "eka-aluminium". In 1879 the Swedish chemist Lars Fredrik Nilson found **scandium**, and in 1886 the German chemist Clemens Winkler found **germanium**, which Mendeleev had predicted would have an atomic mass of about 72 (it is 72.6) and a density of about 5.5 grams per cubic centimetre (it is about 5.3). Gallium even gave a famous twist: its discoverer first measured its density as 4.7 g/cm³, but Mendeleev had predicted about 5.9. When the metal was purified and measured again, the answer was 5.9. A chart had corrected an experiment.

Today's table is arranged by **atomic number** rather than atomic mass. In 1913 the young English physicist Henry Moseley used X-rays to measure the charge on each nucleus, and showed that the atomic number, not the mass, is what really orders the elements. That explained a few places where Mendeleev had had to swap elements to make the families fit.

How to read the map:

- The rows are called **periods**. Moving along a period, the atomic number goes up by one each step.
- The columns are called **groups**, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in the same group have similar properties. Group 1 holds the reactive soft metals (lithium, sodium, potassium); group 17 the halogens (fluorine, chlorine); group 18 the **noble gases** (helium, neon, argon), which hardly react with anything.
- **Metals** fill the left and the middle: shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity. **Non-metals** are on the right. Along the border between them sit the **metalloids**, such as silicon, which have some properties of each; silicon's in-between ability to conduct electricity is why computer chips are made of it.

Why the families repeat, and why the table has its shape, comes from how the electrons are arranged. That is the story of <LessonLink slug="atomic-structure-and-the-periodic-table">Atomic structure and the periodic table</LessonLink>.

### Elements around you

As of 2026, **118 elements** are known. About 90 are found in nature on Earth; the rest have been made in laboratories, a few atoms at a time, by smashing nuclei together in particle accelerators in Japan, Russia, the United States and Germany. The four newest were named in 2016: nihonium (after Japan), moscovium (after Moscow), tennessine (after Tennessee) and oganesson (after Yuri Oganessian, a Russian physicist of Armenian descent who led much of this work). Laboratories in several countries are trying to make elements 119 and 120.

A handful of elements make up most of the world you know:

| Where | Main elements |
| --- | --- |
| air | nitrogen (78 %) and oxygen (21 %), with argon (about 1 %) |
| Earth's crust, by mass | oxygen (46 %), silicon (28 %), aluminium (8 %), iron (5 %) |
| your body, by mass | oxygen (65 %), carbon (18 %), hydrogen (10 %), nitrogen (3 %), calcium and phosphorus (in bones and teeth) |
| a mobile phone | dozens of elements: silicon in its chips, copper in its wires, lithium and often cobalt in its battery, indium in its touchscreen, neodymium in the magnets of its speaker, a little gold on its contacts |

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Electrons orbit the nucleus like planets round the Sun."** Drawings with neat circles are useful for counting, but electrons do not follow paths. They are spread out in fuzzy regions, more like clouds, where they are likely to be found. You will see why when you meet quantum chemistry.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"An atom of gold is gold-coloured, and an atom of copper is copper-coloured."** A single atom has no colour in the everyday sense. The colour of a lump of gold comes from the way enormous numbers of atoms together interact with light.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Atoms of the same element are all exactly the same."** They all have the same number of protons, but isotopes differ in their number of neutrons, and so in mass. This was the correction to Dalton's theory.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The atomic mass of chlorine is 35.45, so some chlorine atoms must have half a neutron."** No atom has part of a neutron. 35.45 is an *average* over a mixture of chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 atoms, just as a family can have an average of 2.4 children without any family having 0.4 of a child.

</Misconception>
