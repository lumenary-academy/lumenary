<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Sour, slippery and in between">

Squeeze a lemon and you get a sharply sour juice. Rub soapy water between your fingers and it feels slippery. Your stomach holds a liquid strong enough to break down a meal; a tablet from a pharmacy can calm it when it causes heartburn. A gardener in Japan or Cornwall finds that the same hydrangea bush flowers blue in one garden and pink in another. And red cabbage, cooked with a splash of vinegar, turns a brighter red.

All of these are about two great families of substances: **acids** and **bases**. They are everywhere, in your kitchen, your cleaning cupboard, your body, the soil and the sea. This lesson shows how chemists recognise them, measure them with the **pH scale**, and use one to cancel out the other.

Long before anyone knew what was inside an atom, people sorted these substances by everyday clues: acidic foods such as lemons, vinegar and sour milk are sour; alkaline things such as soap feel slippery. Chemists never use clues like these to identify an unknown chemical. Many acids and alkalis burn skin and eyes, which is why the corrosion pictogram from <LessonLink slug="safety-in-chemistry">Working safely with chemicals</LessonLink> appears on strong ones. Instead, chemists use colour-changing **indicators** and electronic pH meters.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Acids, bases and alkalis">

In the 1880s the Swedish chemist **Svante Arrhenius** suggested a simple explanation for what acids have in common.

<Definition term="acid">

An **acid** is a substance that releases **hydrogen ions**, H⁺, when it dissolves in water. Examples are citric acid (in lemons and oranges), ethanoic acid (in vinegar), carbonic acid (in fizzy water) and hydrochloric acid (in your stomach).

</Definition>

<Definition term="base and alkali">

A **base** is a substance that reacts with an acid and cancels out its acidity. A base that dissolves in water is called an **alkali**; alkalis release **hydroxide ions**, OH⁻, in water. Examples are sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda), calcium carbonate (chalk, and many antacid tablets), soap, and the sodium hydroxide in oven cleaners.

</Definition>

A solution that is neither acidic nor alkaline, such as pure water or salt water, is **neutral**.

### The pH scale

In 1909 the Danish chemist **Søren Sørensen**, working at the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen, where chemists studied the enzymes used in brewing, introduced a neat way of describing how acidic or alkaline a solution is: its **pH**.

<Definition term="pH">

The **pH** of a solution is a number, usually between 0 and 14, that measures how acidic or alkaline it is. At 25 °C, a pH below 7 is **acidic**, exactly 7 is **neutral**, and above 7 is **alkaline**. The lower the pH, the more hydrogen ions the solution contains: **each step down the scale means ten times as many hydrogen ions**.

</Definition>

That "ten times" is what makes pH so useful, and so easy to misread. Lemon juice, at pH 2, is not a little more acidic than black coffee at pH 5: it has $10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1000$ times as many hydrogen ions.

At body temperature, 37 °C, perfectly neutral water has a pH of about 6.8. And the scale does not really stop at 0 and 14: very strong acids and alkalis can go beyond it, which is one more reason they belong only in a laboratory.

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Counting in tens">

This program takes some typical pH values and works out how many hydrogen ions each liquid has compared with pure water. Each pH step below 7 multiplies the number by 10, and each step above 7 divides it by 10, so the comparison is $10^{(7 - \text{pH})}$.

```python run
# Typical pH values (they vary from sample to sample)
liquids = [
    ("lemon juice", 2.0),
    ("vinegar", 3.0),
    ("black coffee", 5.0),
    ("milk", 6.5),
    ("pure water", 7.0),
    ("sea water", 8.1),
    ("baking-soda solution", 8.3),
    ("soapy water", 10.0),
]

print(f"{'liquid':22} {'pH':>4}   hydrogen ions compared with pure water")
for name, pH in liquids:
    times = 10 ** (7 - pH)          # each pH step is a factor of 10
    if pH == 7.0:
        print(f"{name:22} {pH:4.1f}   the same: neutral")
    elif times > 1:
        print(f"{name:22} {pH:4.1f}   {times:,.0f} times as many")
    else:
        print(f"{name:22} {pH:4.1f}   1/{1 / times:,.0f} as many")

# The ocean: surface pH fell from about 8.2 to about 8.1 since the 1800s
change = 10 ** (8.2 - 8.1)
print(f"\nOcean, pH 8.2 -> 8.1: {change:.2f} times as many hydrogen ions,")
print(f"that is {100 * (change - 1):.0f} % more")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

I'm Margaret. Look at the first and last lines of the table: lemon juice has 100,000 times as many hydrogen ions as pure water, and soapy water only a thousandth as many. Five pH steps make a factor of a hundred thousand! And the last two lines show why a change of "only" 0.1 in the ocean's pH matters: it means 26 % more hydrogen ions.

</Narrator>

Try adding your own liquids: stomach acid at about pH 1.5, or blood at 7.4.

<Section id="indicators" kind="examples" title="Indicators, and a red-cabbage experiment">

<Definition term="indicator">

An **indicator** is a substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acid or an alkali, or, for some indicators, on the exact pH.

</Definition>

People have used plant colours as indicators for centuries. In the 1660s the Irish-born chemist Robert Boyle described how the purple juice of violets turns red in acids and green in alkalis. **Litmus**, a dye made from lichens, turns red in acid and blue in alkali, and is still used in school laboratories as paper strips. **Universal indicator** is a mixture of dyes that gives a different colour for almost every pH, like a rainbow. Nature is full of indicators: the colours of red cabbage, red onions, blackberries and hydrangea flowers all depend on pH, and a yellow turmeric stain from a curry turns reddish-brown when it is washed with alkaline soap.

### Make a red-cabbage indicator, with an adult

This is the one experiment in this lesson. It uses only food from the kitchen, and it needs an adult for the knife and the hot water.

**You need:** about a quarter of a red cabbage; a heatproof jug or bowl; hot water from a kettle; a sieve; three clear glasses or jars; labels; vinegar; lemon juice; baking soda; a teaspoon; a tray to work on.

1. An adult chops the cabbage into small pieces and puts them in the heatproof jug.
2. An adult pours hot water from the kettle over the cabbage, just enough to cover it. Leave it for about 15 minutes, stirring now and then, until the water is a deep purple and has cooled down.
3. Pour it through the sieve into a clean jug. The purple liquid is your indicator.
4. Pour indicator into the three glasses until each is about a quarter full, and label them "vinegar", "lemon juice" and "baking soda".
5. Add a teaspoon of vinegar to the first glass and a teaspoon of lemon juice to the second. Stir half a teaspoon of baking soda into a little water, and add a teaspoon of that solution to the third.
6. Compare the colours with each other and with the purple indicator, and write down what you see.

**What to expect.** The vinegar and lemon juice turn the indicator **pink to red**, because they are acidic; the baking-soda solution turns it **blue to blue-green**, because it is alkaline. The exact shades depend on the cabbage and on your tap water. The colour comes from pigments called **anthocyanins**, whose molecules change shape, and so colour, as they gain or lose hydrogen ions.

**Staying safe.** Test only these three kitchen liquids. Never test cleaning products, such as bleach, oven cleaner or drain cleaner, with your indicator: they are corrosive and can burn skin and eyes. Don't drink the mixtures; they are an experiment, not food. When you have finished, pour everything down the sink with plenty of water, wash the glasses, wipe up any drips (cabbage juice stains) and wash your hands.

<Section id="neutralisation" kind="human" title="Neutralisation: cancelling acids out">

When an acid and a base react, they cancel each other out. The hydrogen ions from the acid join the hydroxide ions from the alkali to make water, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, and what is left is a **salt** (to a chemist, "salt" means any compound made this way, not only table salt).

<Definition term="neutralisation">

**Neutralisation** is the reaction of an acid with a base, which makes a salt and water (and, if the base is a carbonate or hydrogencarbonate, carbon dioxide too):

$$\text{acid} + \text{base} \longrightarrow \text{salt} + \text{water}$$

</Definition>

The textbook example is hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, which makes ordinary table salt and water. Both reactants are corrosive and are used only in laboratories, but neutralisation happens safely all around you:

- **Antacids.** Heartburn and indigestion can come from too much stomach acid. Antacid tablets contain bases such as calcium carbonate, magnesium hydroxide or sodium hydrogencarbonate, which neutralise some of it. With calcium carbonate, the reaction is CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂; that carbon dioxide is why antacids can make you burp.
- **Teeth.** Bacteria in your mouth turn sugar into acids. Below about pH 5.5, those acids start to dissolve tooth enamel. Saliva slowly neutralises them, and brushing with toothpaste removes the bacteria and adds fluoride, which makes enamel tougher.
- **Gardens and farms.** Many crops grow poorly in very acidic soil. Farmers and gardeners spread powdered limestone (calcium carbonate) or lime to neutralise it. In acidic soil, hydrangeas can take up aluminium, which makes their flowers blue; in alkaline soil, they are pink.
- **Baking.** Baking powder is a base (baking soda) and a dry acid mixed together. Once wet, they neutralise each other and release the carbon dioxide that makes a cake rise, as you saw in <LessonLink slug="chemical-reactions">Chemical reactions</LessonLink>.

### Acids and bases in your body

Your body keeps very different pH values in different places. Your **stomach** is strongly acidic, around pH 1.5 to 3.5, which helps to break down proteins and kills most of the microbes you swallow; its lining is protected by a layer of mucus. Your **skin** is mildly acidic, around pH 5, which helps to keep harmful bacteria at bay. Your **blood**, by contrast, is kept between pH 7.35 and 7.45 all the time, by dissolved substances called buffers that soak up extra acid or base. How buffers work is explained in <LessonLink slug="acids-bases-and-buffers">Acids, bases and buffers</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="planet" kind="human" title="Acid rain and an acidifying ocean">

Even clean rain is slightly acidic, at about pH 5.6, because carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it to form a weak acid, carbonic acid.

**Acid rain** is rain that is much more acidic than that, often with a pH between 4 and 5. It happens when power stations, factories and vehicles burn coal and oil that contain sulfur, and when hot engines and furnaces make nitrogen oxides. These gases dissolve in clouds to make sulfuric and nitric acids. In the 1970s and 1980s acid rain damaged forests, killed fish in lakes in Scandinavia, Canada and the north-eastern United States, and ate away at limestone buildings and statues. Countries responded together: in 1979 they signed the Convention on Long-range Transboundary Air Pollution, through the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe; power stations fitted equipment to remove sulfur from their smoke, and fuels were made with much less sulfur. Sulfur pollution, and the acidity of rain, fell steeply in Europe and North America, and more recently in China. Acid rain is one of the great success stories of environmental chemistry.

**The ocean** is a different story, and still unfolding. The sea absorbs roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide that people release by burning fuels, and that carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid in seawater. Since the 1800s the average pH of the surface ocean has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1, which, as the program showed, is about 26 % more hydrogen ions. This is called **ocean acidification**. It makes it harder for corals, oysters, mussels and tiny sea snails to build their shells and skeletons from calcium carbonate. Scientists around the world measure it continuously, from ships and from buoys moored in the open sea. You can read more in <LessonLink slug="environmental-chemistry">Environmental chemistry</LessonLink>.

<Misconception>

**"Ocean acidification means the sea is turning into acid."** Seawater is still alkaline, at about pH 8.1. "Acidification" means it is moving *towards* the acidic end of the scale. Even so, a small change in pH is a large change in hydrogen ions, and sea life is sensitive to it.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"All acids are dangerous and burn."** Some do: concentrated acids are corrosive, and they belong in a laboratory. But many acids are weak or very dilute: you eat citric acid in oranges and ascorbic acid, vitamin C, every day. Equally, alkalis are not safe just because they are not acids: oven cleaner and drain cleaner are among the most dangerous products in a home.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"pH 4 is just a little more acidic than pH 5."** Each step is a factor of ten: pH 4 has ten times as many hydrogen ions as pH 5, and pH 3 has a hundred times as many.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Neutralising means making something harmless."** Neutralisation makes a salt and water, and the salt has its own properties. Most are harmless, but not all, and the reaction can release heat. That is why, for a splash on skin or in the eyes, the rule is plenty of water, never another chemical.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Pure water has no ions in it."** Even the purest water splits very slightly into H⁺ and OH⁻ ions, in equal numbers. That is what makes it neutral, and it is why pure water has a pH at all.

</Misconception>

</Section>
