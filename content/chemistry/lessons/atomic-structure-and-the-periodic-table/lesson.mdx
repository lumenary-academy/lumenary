<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why does the table have that shape?">

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the 63 elements then known in order of atomic weight and noticed that their properties repeated. Lithium, sodium and potassium are soft metals that react briskly with water; fluorine, chlorine and bromine form salts with them in the same one-to-one ratio. By lining up elements with similar behaviour in columns, he produced a table, and he trusted the pattern enough to leave gaps in it. For three of the gaps he predicted the properties of elements nobody had yet seen. When gallium (1875), scandium (1879) and germanium (1886) were found, their densities, atomic weights and oxides came out close to his predictions.

Mendeleev did not know *why* the pattern existed. Nobody did: the electron was discovered in 1897 and the nucleus in 1911. In 1913 Henry Moseley measured the X-rays given off by different elements and found that their frequencies increased in neat steps from one element to the next. The step counted something inside the atom: the charge of the nucleus, the **atomic number** $Z$. Ordering by $Z$ instead of by weight fixed the few places where Mendeleev had had to swap elements by hand (tellurium and iodine, for example), and it told chemists exactly how many elements were still missing.

This lesson answers Mendeleev's question with what came after: the arrangement of electrons in shells and subshells. By the end you will be able to predict, from $Z$ alone, where an element sits in the table, which ions it tends to form, roughly how big it is and how tightly it holds its electrons, and you will know where those predictions fail.

In <LessonLink slug="atoms-and-elements">Atoms and elements</LessonLink> you met the atom as a tiny nucleus surrounded by electrons. Now we look more closely at how those electrons are arranged.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Shells: the evidence first">

An atom is mostly empty space. The nucleus, made of $Z$ protons and some neutrons, has a radius of a few femtometres ($10^{-15}$ m), while the electrons spread over a region tens of thousands of times wider, around $10^{-10}$ m, or 100 picometres. Almost all the mass is in the nucleus; almost all the chemistry is in the electrons. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are **isotopes**: they have the same $Z$, the same electrons and nearly identical chemistry, but different masses.

How do we know electrons are arranged in shells? Not by looking: no microscope resolves an electron's position inside an atom. The evidence is energetic. Two kinds of measurement point the same way.

**Line spectra.** Hot hydrogen gas does not glow in a smooth rainbow but at a few sharp colours. Each line is a photon emitted when the electron drops from one allowed energy to a lower one, so the allowed energies must be separate steps, not a continuum. Every element has its own set of lines, which is how astronomers know what stars are made of.

**Ionisation energies.** The energy needed to pull electrons off an atom one at a time jumps sharply at certain points.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Here are sodium's first four ionisation energies from the NIST database: 5.14, 47.29, 71.62 and 98.94 eV. The second is 9.2 times the first, while the later steps grow by factors of only about 1.5. For magnesium (7.65, 15.04, 80.14, 109.27 eV) the big jump, a factor of 5.3, comes one step later. Sodium has one electron sitting far outside the rest, and magnesium has two.

</Narrator>

Sodium's first electron is easy to remove; the second comes from a full, tightly held inner shell. That jump is the fingerprint of a shell structure, and its position tells us how many electrons are in the outermost shell. Those outer electrons, the **valence electrons**, are the ones that take part in bonding, so this is the first link between electron arrangement and chemical behaviour.

<Analogy>

Think of a theatre with tiers of seats. The stalls (nearest the stage) have few seats and fill first; each tier further back has more seats and costs less to leave. The audience members in the back row decide how quickly the theatre empties at the interval. Like any analogy this one breaks somewhere: electrons are not little people sitting at fixed points, as the next section explains.

</Analogy>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Shells, subshells, orbitals and spin">

Quantum mechanics, which you will meet properly in <LessonLink slug="quantum-mechanics-for-chemists">Quantum mechanics for chemists</LessonLink>, describes each electron in an atom by a **wavefunction**, and labels the allowed states with four quantum numbers. For now we need only their names and what they count.

<Definition term="shell">

A **shell** is the set of electron states with the same **principal quantum number** $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ Larger $n$ means, on average, farther from the nucleus and higher in energy.

</Definition>

<Definition term="subshell">

Within shell $n$, the **angular momentum quantum number** $l$ takes the values $0, 1, \ldots, n-1$, labelled **s, p, d, f** for $l = 0, 1, 2, 3$. A **subshell** is a pair $(n, l)$, written 1s, 2p, 3d and so on.

</Definition>

<Definition term="orbital">

An **orbital** is one spatial state of a single electron: a standing wave around the nucleus, whose square gives the probability of finding the electron in each small region. A subshell with angular momentum $l$ has $2l + 1$ orbitals (labelled by $m_l = -l, \ldots, +l$): one s orbital, three p, five d, seven f.

</Definition>

<Definition term="spin and the Pauli exclusion principle">

Each electron also has a **spin**, $m_s = +\tfrac12$ or $-\tfrac12$ ("up" or "down"). The **Pauli exclusion principle**: no two electrons in an atom share all four quantum numbers. So an orbital holds at most two electrons, and then with opposite spins.

</Definition>

Counting follows at once: a subshell holds $2(2l+1)$ electrons (2, 6, 10, 14 for s, p, d, f), and shell $n$ holds $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1} 2(2l+1) = 2n^2$ electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32.

An orbital is not a path. The electron does not travel round the lobes of a p orbital; the drawing shows where a measurement would be likely to find it. The sign (+ or −) is the sign of the wave, which matters when orbitals on neighbouring atoms overlap to make bonds, as you will see in <LessonLink slug="chemical-bonding">Chemical bonding</LessonLink>.

Three rules decide which orbitals the electrons of an atom occupy in its lowest-energy state, the **ground state**:

<Definition term="aufbau principle (and the Madelung rule)">

**Aufbau** (German for "building up"): electrons fill subshells in order of increasing energy. For neutral atoms the order follows the **Madelung rule**: lower $n + l$ first, and for equal $n + l$, lower $n$ first. This gives 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, **4s, 3d**, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Hund's rule">

Within a subshell, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before any orbital takes a second electron. Nitrogen's three 2p electrons sit one in each 2p orbital, all spin-up.

</Definition>

<Definition term="electron configuration">

An **electron configuration** lists the occupied subshells with their electron counts as superscripts, for example nitrogen 1s² 2s² 2p³. A filled noble-gas core is abbreviated in brackets: sodium is [Ne] 3s¹.

</Definition>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why do the rows of the table have 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 elements, and not $2n^2$ = 2, 8, 18, 32? Because a period fills the subshells with the same value of $n + l$ (plus, for the first row, $n + l = 1$ alone): $n + l = 3$ means 3s and 2p, which is 8 electrons, and so is $n + l = 4$ (4s, 3p). The counting rule that makes the table is the Madelung order, not the shells.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="configurations" kind="examples" title="Writing configurations">

<Example title="Oxygen, iron and the iron ions">

**Oxygen** ($Z = 8$): 1s² 2s² 2p⁴, or [He] 2s² 2p⁴. By Hund's rule the four 2p electrons occupy all three 2p orbitals, one of them doubly, so oxygen has **two unpaired electrons**. (This is related to, but not the same as, the reason oxygen gas is attracted by a magnet, which needs molecular orbitals to explain.)

**Iron** ($Z = 26$): following the Madelung order, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶, usually written [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², with subshells in order of $n$. The six 3d electrons fill five orbitals: five go in singly, the sixth pairs up, leaving **four unpaired electrons**.

**Iron ions.** Iron forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺. You might expect to remove the last electrons added, from 3d. In fact the 4s electrons go first: Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵. Once the atom is positively charged, the 3d subshell drops below 4s in energy. This is true across the transition metals: their ions have no 4s electrons.

</Example>

The Madelung rule is an empirical rule for neutral atoms in their ground state, not a law. Python makes it easy to apply, and to see where it fails.

```python run
SYMBOLS = ("H He Li Be B C N O F Ne Na Mg Al Si P S Cl Ar K Ca Sc Ti V Cr Mn Fe Co Ni Cu Zn "
           "Ga Ge As Se Br Kr Rb Sr Y Zr Nb Mo Tc Ru Rh Pd Ag Cd In Sn Sb Te I Xe").split()
LETTER = "spdf"

# Every subshell up to n = 7, sorted by the Madelung rule: lower n + l first; on a tie, lower n first.
SUBSHELLS = sorted(((n, l) for n in range(1, 8) for l in range(min(n, 4))),
                   key=lambda s: (s[0] + s[1], s[0]))
print("filling order:", " ".join(f"{n}{LETTER[l]}" for n, l in SUBSHELLS[:15]))

def aufbau(Z):
    """Ground-state configuration predicted by the Madelung rule, as {(n, l): electrons}."""
    config, left = {}, Z
    for n, l in SUBSHELLS:
        if left == 0:
            break
        k = min(left, 2 * (2 * l + 1))      # a subshell holds 2(2l + 1) electrons
        config[(n, l)] = k
        left -= k
    return config

def written(config):
    """Noble-gas core in brackets, then the rest by n and l (so 3d is written before 4s)."""
    core = max(z for z in (0, 2, 10, 18, 36) if z < sum(config.values()))
    rest = {s: k for s, k in config.items() if s not in aufbau(core)} if core else config
    text = " ".join(f"{n}{LETTER[l]}{k}" for (n, l), k in sorted(rest.items()))
    return f"[{SYMBOLS[core - 1]}] {text}" if core else text

# Measured ground states that break the rule (from atomic spectra), among Z <= 54.
EXCEPTIONS = {"Cr": "[Ar] 3d5 4s1", "Cu": "[Ar] 3d10 4s1", "Nb": "[Kr] 4d4 5s1",
              "Mo": "[Kr] 4d5 5s1", "Ru": "[Kr] 4d7 5s1", "Rh": "[Kr] 4d8 5s1",
              "Pd": "[Kr] 4d10", "Ag": "[Kr] 4d10 5s1"}

for sym in ["O", "Na", "Ar", "K", "Ca", "Fe", "Cr", "Cu", "Br", "Pd"]:
    Z = SYMBOLS.index(sym) + 1
    note = f"   measured: {EXCEPTIONS[sym]}" if sym in EXCEPTIONS else ""
    print(f"{sym:2} Z={Z:2}  {written(aufbau(Z))}{note}")

misses = [s for s in SYMBOLS if s in EXCEPTIONS]
print(f"\nThe rule is right for {len(SYMBOLS) - len(misses)} of the first {len(SYMBOLS)} elements;",
      "exceptions:", ", ".join(misses))
```

Run it: in the first 54 elements the rule fails eight times. Every exception is a transition metal, where the $(n-1)$d and $n$s subshells are so close in energy that small effects decide the winner. Chromium moves one electron from 4s to 3d and ends with six unpaired electrons; copper completes its 3d subshell. The usual explanation, that "half-filled and filled subshells are extra stable", is a summary rather than a cause: the real reason is a balance between the repulsion of electrons sharing an orbital and the **exchange energy** that favours parallel spins, and it has to be computed. Further down the table the exceptions multiply (platinum and gold behave like palladium and silver, and several lanthanoids and actinoids have a d electron where the rule predicts f), and in the heaviest elements relativistic effects reorder the levels.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Note the fine print in "ground-state configuration": it describes an *isolated atom in the gas phase*, the state measured by spectroscopy. In a compound or a metal, the electrons are shared and rearranged, and a configuration like "Cr: 3d⁵ 4s¹" says nothing direct about chromium in steel or in a pigment. And the 4s-before-3d order holds for the neutral atoms of potassium and calcium, not for transition-metal ions, as the iron example showed.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="table" kind="human" title="Reading the periodic table">

Put the elements in order of $Z$, start a new row every time a new shell begins to fill, and the table assembles itself. The **period** (row) number is the highest $n$ occupied in the ground state. The **group** (column), numbered 1 to 18 in IUPAC's convention, collects elements with the same arrangement of valence electrons, and so similar chemistry.

- **s-block** (groups 1 and 2): the last electron enters an s orbital. Group 1, the **alkali metals** (lithium to caesium), has one valence electron, ns¹, and forms 1+ ions; group 2, the **alkaline-earth metals**, has ns² and forms 2+ ions. Hydrogen (1s¹) is placed above lithium for its configuration, but it is a gas whose chemistry is its own. Helium (1s²) is s-block by configuration but sits above neon in group 18 because, like the other noble gases, it has a full outer shell.
- **p-block** (groups 13 to 18): ns² np¹ to ns² np⁶. It contains all the non-metals except hydrogen, the metalloids such as silicon and germanium, and some metals such as aluminium, tin and lead. Group 17 are the **halogens** (ns² np⁵), one electron short of a full shell; group 18 are the **noble gases**, with full shells.
- **d-block** (groups 3 to 12): the **transition metals**, filling $(n-1)$d. Many form ions of several charges and coloured compounds, which <LessonLink slug="coordination-chemistry">Coordination chemistry</LessonLink> explains.
- **f-block**: the **lanthanoids** (filling 4f) and **actinoids** (5f), printed separately. Which elements belong in group 3 of the main table, lanthanum and actinium or lutetium and lawrencium, has been debated for decades, and IUPAC has studied the question; the chemistry does not depend on how we draw it.

Elements in the same group behave alike because they share a valence configuration, but they are not identical: lithium reacts gently with water, potassium vigorously. The next section explains why.

</Section>

<Section id="trends" kind="human" title="Periodic trends and effective nuclear charge">

One idea explains most periodic trends. An outer electron is attracted by the $Z$ protons of the nucleus, but it is repelled by the other electrons, especially those in inner shells, which spend most of their time between it and the nucleus. The net pull is as if the nucleus had a smaller charge, the **effective nuclear charge** $Z_\text{eff} = Z - S$, where $S$ is the **shielding** by the other electrons.

In 1930 John Slater proposed simple rules for estimating $S$. For an s or p electron in shell $n$: each other electron in the same shell shields 0.35 (0.30 in 1s), each electron in shell $n - 1$ shields 0.85, and each electron deeper in shields 1.00. (The full rules treat d and f electrons separately.) Crude as they are, they capture the pattern:

```python run
def slater_zeff(Z, shells):
    """Effective nuclear charge on one outermost s or p electron, by Slater's 1930 rules.

    shells lists the electrons in each shell n = 1, 2, 3, ... (all s and p here)."""
    n = len(shells)                       # the outermost shell
    same = shells[-1] - 1                 # the other electrons in the same shell
    s = (0.30 if n == 1 else 0.35) * same
    if n >= 2:
        s += 0.85 * shells[-2]            # each electron one shell down
    s += 1.00 * sum(shells[:-2])          # each electron two or more shells down
    return Z - s

PERIOD_2 = ["Li", "Be", "B", "C", "N", "O", "F", "Ne"]
PERIOD_3 = ["Na", "Mg", "Al", "Si", "P", "S", "Cl", "Ar"]
print("element  Z  shells       Z_eff")
for k, el in enumerate(PERIOD_2):
    Z = 3 + k
    print(f"{el:7} {Z:2}  {str([2, k + 1]):12} {slater_zeff(Z, [2, k + 1]):5.2f}")
for k, el in enumerate(PERIOD_3):
    Z = 11 + k
    print(f"{el:7} {Z:2}  {str([2, 8, k + 1]):12} {slater_zeff(Z, [2, 8, k + 1]):5.2f}")
print(f"{'K':7} {19:2}  {str([2, 8, 8, 1]):12} {slater_zeff(19, [2, 8, 8, 1]):5.2f}")
```

Across a period, each step adds one proton but only 0.35 of shielding, so $Z_\text{eff}$ rises by 0.65: from 1.30 for lithium to 5.20 for fluorine, and from 2.20 for sodium to 6.10 for chlorine. Down a group, $Z_\text{eff}$ barely changes (lithium 1.30, sodium and potassium 2.20) but the valence electron is in a higher shell, much farther out. Those two facts give three trends.

**Atomic radius** decreases across a period (a stronger pull draws the same shell in) and increases down a group (each new shell is farther out). "Radius" needs a definition, because an atom has no hard edge: the **covalent radius** is half the length of a single bond between two identical atoms, the **metallic radius** half the distance between neighbours in a metal, and the **van der Waals radius** half the closest approach of non-bonded atoms. They differ by tens of picometres for the same element, so compare like with like.

**First ionisation energy**, the energy to remove one electron from a gas-phase atom, X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻, rises across a period and falls down a group, the mirror image of the radius.

**Electronegativity** measures how strongly an atom in a molecule pulls shared electrons towards itself. Linus Pauling defined the first scale in 1932 from bond energies; it runs from about 0.8 (caesium, potassium) to 3.98 (fluorine), rises across a period and falls down a group. It is not directly measurable for an isolated atom, and other scales (Robert Mulliken's average of ionisation energy and electron affinity, Leland Allen's average valence-electron energy) agree on the trend but not on every value. The noble gases helium, neon and argon have no Pauling value at all, because they form no bonds from which to compute one.

The data below are typed in from the sources named in the comments. Run it and look at the bar chart, which has one `#` per electronvolt.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Typed in by hand, with sources:
#   ie  first ionisation energy, eV: NIST Atomic Spectra Database (rounded to 3 decimals)
#   r   single-bond covalent radius, pm: Cordero et al., Dalton Trans. 2008 (C as sp3)
#   en  Pauling electronegativity (Allred's 1961 values); None where none is defined
DATA = [  # symbol,   ie,   r,   en
    ("H", 13.598, 31, 2.20), ("He", 24.587, 28, None),
    ("Li", 5.392, 128, 0.98), ("Be", 9.323, 96, 1.57), ("B", 8.298, 84, 2.04),
    ("C", 11.260, 76, 2.55), ("N", 14.534, 71, 3.04), ("O", 13.618, 66, 3.44),
    ("F", 17.423, 57, 3.98), ("Ne", 21.565, 58, None),
    ("Na", 5.139, 166, 0.93), ("Mg", 7.646, 141, 1.31), ("Al", 5.986, 121, 1.61),
    ("Si", 8.152, 111, 1.90), ("P", 10.487, 107, 2.19), ("S", 10.360, 105, 2.58),
    ("Cl", 12.968, 102, 3.16), ("Ar", 15.760, 106, None),
    ("K", 4.341, 203, 0.82), ("Ca", 6.113, 176, 1.00),
]
sym = [d[0] for d in DATA]
ie = np.array([d[1] for d in DATA])
r = np.array([d[2] for d in DATA])

print(" Z  el   IE/eV  r/pm   EN   first ionisation energy")
for Z, (s, e, rad, en) in enumerate(DATA, start=1):
    bar = "#" * round(e)                          # one # per eV
    print(f"{Z:2}  {s:2} {e:7.3f} {rad:5} {f'{en:.2f}' if en else '  - ':>4}  {bar}")

# Where does the ionisation energy *fall* from one element to the next?
drops = [f"{sym[i]}->{sym[i + 1]}" for i in np.flatnonzero(np.diff(ie) < 0)]
print("\nIE falls at:", ", ".join(drops))

# Across period 2 and period 3 (Li..F and Na..Cl): how much do radius and IE change?
for name, a, b in [("period 2", 2, 8), ("period 3", 10, 16)]:
    print(f"{name}: radius {r[a]} -> {r[b]} pm ({100 * (r[b] - r[a]) / r[a]:+.0f}%),"
          f" IE {ie[a]:.2f} -> {ie[b]:.2f} eV (x{ie[b] / ie[a]:.1f})")
```

The last lines report that across period 2 the covalent radius shrinks by 55% (128 to 57 pm) while the ionisation energy grows 3.2-fold; across period 3 the radius shrinks by 39% and the ionisation energy grows 2.5-fold. The sawtooth shape is the periodic law itself: every alkali metal is a minimum, every noble gas a maximum.

The program also finds every place where the ionisation energy *falls* from one element to the next. Three kinds of fall appear:

- **Noble gas → alkali metal** (He→Li, Ne→Na, Ar→K): a new shell begins.
- **Group 2 → group 13** (Be→B, Mg→Al): boron's fifth electron is the first in 2p, which is higher in energy than 2s and partly shielded by it, so it is easier to remove than beryllium's 2s electron.
- **Group 15 → group 16** (N→O, P→S): nitrogen's three 2p electrons are in separate orbitals; oxygen's fourth 2p electron has to share an orbital with another electron, and their mutual repulsion makes it easier to remove.

Both dips are evidence for subshells and for Hund's rule, read straight from the data. The noble-gas radii are the odd ones out (neon at 58 pm, slightly larger than fluorine): since neon forms no bonds, its "covalent radius" is an estimate on a different footing, a reminder to check how a number in a table was obtained before reading too much into it.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Slater's $Z_\text{eff}$ predicts more than the trend. Treating lithium's 2s electron as hydrogen-like gives an ionisation energy of $13.6\ \text{eV} \times Z_\text{eff}^2 / n^2 = 13.6 \times 1.30^2 / 4 \approx 5.7$ eV, against a measured 5.39 eV. Try the same for sodium (with $n = 3$) and the estimate is off by 40%: a good reminder that rules of thumb have a range of validity.

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view">

**Where the numbers come from.** Ionisation energies and electron configurations are measured by atomic spectroscopy and evaluated by groups such as the [NIST Atomic Spectra Database](https://www.nist.gov/pml/atomic-spectra-database), which lists ground-state configurations and ionisation energies for every element with uncertainties. Standard atomic weights are evaluated by IUPAC's [Commission on Isotopic Abundances and Atomic Weights](https://www.ciaaw.org/), which since its 2009 evaluation has given some elements, such as hydrogen, carbon and chlorine, as *intervals* rather than single numbers, because their isotope mixture really does vary between sources on Earth. Professionals cite the database and version they used, as the code above does in its comments, because tables in different books disagree in the last digit.

**Relativity in the heavy elements.** In heavy atoms the inner electrons move at a substantial fraction of the speed of light, and relativistic effects contract the s orbitals and change their energies. These effects explain why gold is yellow rather than silvery and contribute to mercury being a liquid at room temperature. Quantum chemists include them routinely in calculations on heavy elements, a topic for <LessonLink slug="computational-chemistry">Computational chemistry</LessonLink>.

**The end of the table.** The seventh row was completed in 2016, when IUPAC approved the names nihonium (113), moscovium (115), tennessine (117) and oganesson (118) after the discoveries were assessed by a joint IUPAC–IUPAP working group. New elements are made a few atoms at a time by firing beams of nuclei at heavy targets, at laboratories such as RIKEN (Japan), the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (Dubna), GSI (Germany) and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (United States). As of 2026, searches for elements 119 and 120 are under way; for such short-lived atoms, even measuring chemistry one atom at a time is a frontier, and whether they behave like their lighter group partners is an open question.

**Using the table in practice.** Chemists use the trends daily without computing anything: to predict that a group 1 metal and a group 17 element will form an ionic salt, that a C–F bond is strongly polar, or that potassium gives up an electron more readily than lithium. Materials scientists use the same trends to choose dopants and alloying elements; see [bonding in solids](/en/materials/bonding-in-solids/) in Lumenary Materials.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

- **1869–1871**: Dmitri Mendeleev and, independently, Lothar Meyer publish periodic tables. Mendeleev's predictions of undiscovered elements make his version famous.
- **1894–1898**: William Ramsay and colleagues discover argon, helium, neon, krypton and xenon, and a whole new group is added.
- **1913**: Niels Bohr's model of the hydrogen atom explains its line spectrum with quantised energy levels; Henry Moseley establishes atomic number as the ordering principle. Moseley was killed in the First World War two years later, aged 27.
- **1925**: Wolfgang Pauli states the exclusion principle; Samuel Goudsmit and George Uhlenbeck propose electron spin. With Erwin Schrödinger's wave equation (1926), the shell structure acquires its modern explanation.
- **1944–1945**: Glenn Seaborg proposes that the elements after actinium form a separate actinoid series, giving the table its modern layout.
- **1962**: Neil Bartlett makes the first compound of a noble gas, a xenon compound, ending the belief that group 18 is completely inert.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Electrons orbit the nucleus like planets."** Bohr's 1913 model did picture circular orbits, and it explains hydrogen's spectrum, but it fails for every other atom. Electrons in atoms are described by orbitals, which give probabilities of position, not paths.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"4s is always lower in energy than 3d."** For the neutral atoms of potassium and calcium it is. For transition-metal ions it is not, which is why Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶ and not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s². The order of filling and the order of removal are different questions.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Atoms want a full outer shell."** Atoms want nothing; a full shell is simply a low-energy arrangement. Sodium does not "want" to lose an electron: removing it costs 5.14 eV. Ionic compounds form because the energy released when ions attract each other in a crystal more than pays for that cost, as the next lesson shows.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Noble gases never form compounds."** Xenon forms fluorides and oxides, krypton a fluoride. They are unreactive, not inert.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="try-it" kind="human" title="Something safe to try at home">

You can see that atoms emit light only at certain colours with a cheap **diffraction grating** (sold as "spectroscope glasses") or an old CD or DVD held at a slant. With an adult, look at the reflection of a compact fluorescent lamp or a street light in the grating, from a comfortable distance, and you will see separate bright lines rather than a smooth rainbow: they come from mercury atoms inside the lamp, or sodium atoms in an orange street lamp. An ordinary incandescent bulb gives a continuous rainbow instead, because its light comes from a hot solid. Never point a grating or anything else towards the Sun, and do not open or break lamps: fluorescent tubes contain mercury.

<Reflection title="Order that can be trusted">

Mendeleev left gaps in his table because he expected nature to be consistent: if the pattern held for 63 elements, it should hold for the next three. Many scientists, Robert Boyle and Michael Faraday among them, saw confidence in the regularity of creation as a reason to do science at all. Whatever one makes of it, the periodic table is one of the strongest cases in the history of science of a pattern that was trusted before it was understood, and then understood.

</Reflection>
