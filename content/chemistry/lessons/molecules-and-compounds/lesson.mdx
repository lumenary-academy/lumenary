<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Three puzzles">

Here are three puzzles.

**Puzzle 1.** Hydrogen is a gas that burns. Oxygen is the gas that things need in order to burn. Yet when hydrogen and oxygen join together, they make water, which we use to put fires out.

**Puzzle 2.** Sodium is a soft, silvery metal so reactive that it is stored under oil: dropped into water, it fizzes violently. Chlorine is a yellow-green, poisonous gas. Yet joined together, they make sodium chloride, the white crystals of table salt that you sprinkle on your food.

**Puzzle 3.** The air you breathe contains carbon dioxide, a gas. The sugar in your kitchen is a sweet, white solid. Both are made from carbon and oxygen (sugar has hydrogen too), yet nothing could look less alike.

In <LessonLink slug="atoms-and-elements">Atoms and elements</LessonLink> you met the hundred or so kinds of atom. This lesson is about what happens when atoms **join together**, and why the result can be so completely different from the atoms you started with.

Hello, I'm Chloe! Here's the big idea to look out for: a substance's properties depend not only on *which* atoms it contains, but on *how they are joined*. Change the joining, and you change everything about the substance.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Molecules, compounds and formulas">

<Definition term="molecule">

A **molecule** is a group of two or more atoms held tightly together by chemical bonds, acting as a single particle. A water molecule is one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms.

</Definition>

<Definition term="compound">

A **compound** is a pure substance made of two or more **different** elements chemically joined together, always in the same fixed proportions. Water, carbon dioxide and table salt are compounds.

</Definition>

The two words overlap but are not the same. The oxygen in the air is made of molecules of two oxygen atoms, O₂, but it is not a compound, because it contains only one element. Table salt, on the other hand, is a compound but is not made of separate molecules, as you will see below.

### Reading a chemical formula

A **chemical formula** says which elements are in a substance and in what proportion, using the element symbols from the periodic table and small numbers written below the line (**subscripts**).

- **H₂O**: two hydrogen atoms (H₂) and one oxygen atom (O, with no number, meaning 1).
- **CO₂**: one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.
- **C₆H₁₂O₆** (glucose): six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and six oxygen atoms, 24 atoms in all.
- **NaCl**: sodium and chlorine in the ratio one to one.

A subscript belongs only to the symbol just before it. A large number written **in front** of a formula counts whole molecules: **3CO₂** means three molecules of carbon dioxide, which is 3 carbon atoms and 6 oxygen atoms.

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon. Watch the difference between 2H₂O and H₂O₂. The first is two water molecules. The second is one molecule of hydrogen peroxide, a different compound altogether: a bleach and disinfectant, harmful to swallow. Moving one little number changes the substance, so chemists write formulas very carefully.

</Narrator>

### Compounds are not mixtures

In <LessonLink slug="what-is-matter">What is matter?</LessonLink> you met mixtures, in which substances are mingled but not joined. A compound is different in three ways:

| | Mixture | Compound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proportions | any amounts: a little salt or a lot in water | always fixed: every sample of water is H₂O |
| Properties | each ingredient keeps its own | new properties, unlike the elements' |
| Separating | by physical methods: filtering, evaporating, a magnet | only by a chemical reaction |

A classic example: powdered iron and yellow sulfur stirred together make a mixture, and a magnet pulls the iron straight out. When the two react (in a laboratory, with a fume cupboard), they form a new, dark compound, iron sulfide, from which no magnet can pull any iron.

</Section>

<Section id="bonds" kind="intuition" title="What holds atoms together: a first, honest picture">

Why do atoms join at all? The short answer is **electric attraction**. Every atom has a positive nucleus and negative electrons. When two atoms come close, the electrons of each are attracted to the nucleus of the other too. In the right arrangement, the atoms together have less energy than they did apart, a bit like a ball that has rolled down into a dip. That joining force is a **chemical bond**. A bond is not a little stick, as model kits suggest: it is electrons spread out between two nuclei, attracted to both.

Chemists describe three main kinds of bond. Real bonds are often somewhere in between, but these three are the right place to start.

**Sharing: covalent bonds.** Between non-metal atoms, such as hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen, atoms **share** pairs of electrons. Each shared pair, held between two nuclei, is a covalent bond. In water, the oxygen atom shares one pair of electrons with each hydrogen atom. In carbon dioxide and in oxygen gas, the atoms share two pairs, a **double bond**. Covalent bonds make molecules: water, carbon dioxide, sugar, caffeine and the molecules of life.

**Giving and taking: ionic bonds.** When a metal meets a non-metal, the metal atom often **gives** one or more electrons to the non-metal atom. Each becomes an **ion**, an atom with an electric charge. A sodium atom gives one electron to a chlorine atom: the sodium becomes Na⁺ and the chlorine becomes a chloride ion, Cl⁻. Opposite charges attract strongly, so the ions pack together in a huge, regular, three-dimensional pattern: a **crystal**.

**A sea of electrons: metallic bonds.** In a metal, such as copper or iron, every atom lets go of one or more of its outer electrons, which then wander freely through the whole piece of metal, holding the positive ions together. Those wandering electrons are why metals conduct electricity, the reason copper is used for wires.

Why do atoms give, take or share electrons in just these ways? A useful rule of thumb is that atoms tend to end up with the same number of electrons as the nearest **noble gas** in the periodic table, the family that hardly reacts at all. Sodium (11 electrons) loses one to match neon (10); chlorine (17) gains one to match argon (18). In water, sharing gives oxygen a share in 10 electrons, like neon. The deeper reasons, and the many exceptions, are explained in <LessonLink slug="chemical-bonding">Chemical bonding</LessonLink>.

<Analogy>

Covalent bonding is like two children holding the same skipping rope, each pulling on it, which keeps them together. Ionic bonding is more like one child handing a ball to another: afterwards, one has a ball too many and one a ball too few, and here the "extra" and "missing" charges pull them strongly together. Like all analogies, this one breaks down if you push it: electrons are not balls or ropes.

</Analogy>

<Section id="salt" kind="examples" title="Why salt is not like sodium or chlorine">

Now Puzzle 2 has an answer. The properties of a substance come mostly from its **electrons**: how they are arranged, and how easily they are lost, gained or shared.

<Example title="Sodium, chlorine and sodium chloride">

| | Sodium, Na | Chlorine, Cl₂ | Sodium chloride, NaCl |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| At room temperature | soft, silvery metal | yellow-green gas | white, hard crystals |
| What it is made of | sodium atoms, sharing a sea of electrons | molecules of two chlorine atoms | Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a crystal |
| Behaviour | reacts violently with water; stored under oil | poisonous; attacks the lungs | dissolves quietly in water; melts at 801 °C |
| In the body | not found as the metal | not found as the gas | needed every day, in small amounts |

A sodium *atom* has one loosely held outer electron, which it loses very easily, so sodium metal reacts fiercely with water and air. A sodium *ion* has already lost that electron; its remaining electrons are tightly held, so it is calm and stable. In the same way a chlorine *molecule* grabs electrons from almost anything, which is what makes it harmful, while a chloride *ion* already has the extra electron it "wanted". The ions are completely different particles from the atoms, so salt is a completely different substance.

</Example>

The same goes for Puzzle 1. In a water molecule, the hydrogen and oxygen atoms have already joined in a stable arrangement and have no tendency to burn. Puzzle 3 is about *how* atoms join: carbon dioxide molecules are small and barely attract each other, so it is a gas, while sugar molecules are large and cling together, so sugar is a solid.

Even an element can change its properties with the way its atoms are joined. Diamond and graphite (the "lead" in pencils) are both pure carbon. In diamond each carbon atom is bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional network, making the hardest natural material; in graphite the atoms are bonded in flat sheets that slide over each other, which is why it is soft and leaves a mark on paper.

</Section>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Counting atoms with Python">

A computer can read formulas for us. This program reads each formula letter by letter: a capital letter starts a new element symbol, a small letter can follow it (as in Na or Cl), and a number after the symbol says how many atoms. Then it adds up the atoms, and, using the average mass of each kind of atom from the periodic table, the mass of each molecule in atomic mass units (u).

```python run
import re

# Average masses of atoms, in atomic mass units (u), rounded
MASS = {"H": 1.008, "C": 12.011, "N": 14.007, "O": 15.999, "Na": 22.990, "Cl": 35.45, "Ca": 40.078}

def count_atoms(formula):
    """Read a formula like 'C6H12O6' into {'C': 6, 'H': 12, 'O': 6}."""
    counts = {}
    # a capital letter, maybe a small letter, then maybe a number
    for symbol, number in re.findall(r"([A-Z][a-z]?)(\d*)", formula):
        counts[symbol] = counts.get(symbol, 0) + (int(number) if number else 1)
    return counts

compounds = [
    ("water", "H2O"),
    ("carbon dioxide", "CO2"),
    ("table salt", "NaCl"),
    ("baking soda", "NaHCO3"),
    ("chalk", "CaCO3"),
    ("glucose", "C6H12O6"),
    ("sucrose (sugar)", "C12H22O11"),
    ("caffeine", "C8H10N4O2"),
]

print(f"{'compound':16} {'formula':10} {'atoms':>5}   {'mass (u)':>8}   atoms of each element")
for name, formula in compounds:
    counts = count_atoms(formula)
    total = sum(counts.values())
    mass = sum(MASS[el] * n for el, n in counts.items())
    print(f"{name:16} {formula:10} {total:5d}   {mass:8.2f}   {counts}")
```

<Section id="around-us" kind="human" title="Compounds at home and in your body">

Here are some compounds you meet every day. Chemists give each one an exact name, agreed internationally through IUPAC, as well as the everyday name.

| Everyday name | Chemical name | Formula | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| water | water (oxidane) | H₂O | about 60 % of an adult's body mass |
| table salt | sodium chloride | NaCl | food; sea water; your blood and sweat |
| sugar | sucrose | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ | sugar cane, sugar beet, sweets |
| blood sugar | glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | the fuel your cells burn for energy |
| baking soda | sodium hydrogencarbonate | NaHCO₃ | baking; some antacid tablets |
| vinegar's acid | ethanoic acid (acetic acid) | CH₃COOH | about 5 % of vinegar |
| chalk, limestone, eggshell | calcium carbonate | CaCO₃ | eggshells, seashells, marble, chalk |
| carbon dioxide | carbon dioxide | CO₂ | the bubbles in fizzy drinks; the gas you breathe out |
| caffeine | caffeine | C₈H₁₀N₄O₂ | tea, coffee, cocoa, kola nuts |

The molecules of life are much bigger. A protein molecule, such as the haemoglobin that carries oxygen in your blood, contains thousands of atoms, and a single DNA molecule in one of your cells contains thousands of millions. Your bones and teeth get their hardness from a compound of calcium, phosphorus and oxygen (with a little hydrogen), arranged in tiny crystals.

### How chemists see molecules

How do we know that water is bent and carbon dioxide straight? One way is to shine X-rays through a crystal: the pattern they make reveals where the atoms are. The British chemist **Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin** used this method to find the structures of penicillin (1945), vitamin B₁₂ and insulin, and won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1964. Today chemists use X-rays, magnetic resonance and computer calculations together, and the free database PubChem lists more than a hundred million compounds, each with its formula and shape.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A compound is just a mixture of its elements."** In a mixture, each substance keeps its properties and can be separated physically. In a compound, the atoms are chemically bonded into a new substance with new properties, and only a chemical reaction can split them apart.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Table salt is made of NaCl molecules."** Salt is a crystal of ions, with no separate pairs. The formula NaCl tells you the **ratio** of ions, one sodium ion for every chloride ion, not the contents of a molecule. Chemists call one Na⁺ with one Cl⁻ a **formula unit**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every molecule is a compound."** O₂, N₂ and ozone, O₃, are molecules of a single element, so they are elements, not compounds. Diamond, made only of carbon atoms, is an element too.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Chemical bonds are little sticks between the atoms."** Sticks are a handy way to draw bonds. Real bonds are electrons spread out between nuclei and attracted to both, and the atoms in a molecule are always vibrating, so bond lengths are averages.

</Misconception>

</Section>
