<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Salt on the road, salt in the sea">

On a frosty morning, lorries spread salt on the roads, and the ice turns to slush though the air is still below 0 °C. In the Gulf states, Spain, Israel, Singapore and Australia, plants turn seawater into drinking water by forcing it through membranes at sixty times atmospheric pressure. The drip in a hospital patient's arm is 0.9 % salt solution, because pure water would make red blood cells swell and burst. And in hard-water towns, white scale builds up in kettles.

All four are about **solutions**: one substance spread evenly, molecule by molecule or ion by ion, through another. Most of chemistry happens in solution, because dissolved particles move freely and meet often. About 97 % of Earth's water is in the oceans, a solution of about 3.5 % salts by mass.

This lesson asks how to say *how much* is dissolved, why some things dissolve and others do not, and why dissolving anything shifts freezing points, boiling points and the flow of water through membranes by amounts that depend only on *how many* particles are dissolved.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Dissolving, one particle at a time">

Water molecules are polar (see <LessonLink slug="chemical-bonding">Chemical bonding</LessonLink>), with a partially negative oxygen end and partially positive hydrogen ends. Each ion leaves wrapped in a **hydration shell** of oriented water molecules that shields its charge.

Whether a substance dissolves is a contest. Dissolving breaks attractions within the solute and between solvent molecules, and makes new ones between solute and solvent; it also mixes, and mixing increases entropy, which always favours dissolving a little. Sodium chloride dissolves with almost no net energy change (it absorbs a slight 3.9 kJ/mol): the attraction of the ions for water nearly pays for the lattice, and mixing tips the balance.

You can watch the contest in the kitchen, with an adult. Stir teaspoons of salt into a glass of cold water, one at a time, until no more will dissolve; do the same with sugar in a second glass; then repeat with warm water from the tap. Far more sugar than salt dissolves, and much more in warm water, while salt hardly changes. A third glass with a little cooking oil and water shows two layers that refuse to mix until a drop of washing-up liquid lets them form a cloudy mixture. Everything used is ordinary food or kitchen supplies, and nothing needs a stove.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Saying how much is dissolved">

<Definition term="solution, solvent and solute">

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture: uniform throughout, down to the scale of molecules. The component present in the largest amount, usually a liquid, is the **solvent**; the others are **solutes**. A solution with water as the solvent is **aqueous**, written (aq).

</Definition>

<Definition term="measures of concentration">

For a solute B in a solvent A:

| Quantity | Definition | Usual unit | Depends on temperature? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mass fraction $w_\text{B}$ | mass of B / mass of solution | %, ppm, ppb | no |
| mass concentration $\rho_\text{B}$ | mass of B / volume of solution | g/L, mg/L | yes |
| amount concentration $c_\text{B}$ (molarity) | amount of B / volume of solution | mol/L (mol dm⁻³), written M | yes |
| molality $b_\text{B}$ | amount of B / **mass of solvent** | mol/kg | no |
| mole fraction $x_\text{B}$ | amount of B / total amount | none | no |

The SI unit of amount concentration is mol/m³, but chemists almost always use mol/L. Volume-based measures change slightly with temperature, because liquids expand; molality and mole fraction do not.

</Definition>

<Definition term="dilution">

Adding solvent changes the volume but not the amount of solute, so the concentrations before and after are related by $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="solubility and saturation">

The **solubility** of a substance is the most that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature, often quoted in grams per 100 g of water or in mol/L. A solution holding that much is **saturated**: undissolved solid in contact with it dissolves and crystallises at equal rates, a dynamic equilibrium.

</Definition>

<Definition term="electrolyte">

An **electrolyte** is a solute whose solution conducts electricity because it contains ions. A **strong electrolyte** is essentially fully ionised in dilute solution (sodium chloride, hydrochloric acid); a **weak electrolyte** is only partly ionised (ethanoic acid, ammonia); a **non-electrolyte** dissolves as neutral molecules (sugar, ethanol, urea).

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="concentration" kind="computation" title="Concentration and dilution in practice">

Converting between units needs the molar masses and the density of the solution. The block works through a model seawater, 35.0 g of sodium chloride per kilogram of solution (density 1.023 g/mL at 20 °C), then plans a dilution and a serial dilution.

```python run
# One solution, five ways of stating its concentration:
# a model seawater of 35.0 g of sodium chloride in every 1000 g of solution.
M_NaCl, M_H2O = 58.44, 18.015          # g/mol
m_salt, m_solution = 35.0, 1000.0      # g
density = 1.023                        # g/mL at 20 °C (measured for 3.5 % NaCl)

n_salt = m_salt / M_NaCl               # mol of NaCl
m_water = m_solution - m_salt          # g of solvent
V_L = m_solution / density / 1000      # litres of solution

print(f"mass fraction      w = {100 * m_salt / m_solution:.2f} %  ({1e6 * m_salt / m_solution:,.0f} ppm by mass)")
print(f"mass concentration ρ = {m_salt / V_L:.1f} g/L")
print(f"amount conc.       c = {n_salt / V_L:.4f} mol/L")
print(f"molality           b = {n_salt / (m_water / 1000):.4f} mol/kg")
print(f"mole fraction      x = {n_salt / (n_salt + m_water / M_H2O):.5f}")

# Dilution: the amount of solute is conserved, so c1 V1 = c2 V2.
c_stock, c_want, V_want = 2.00, 0.154, 250.0      # mol/L, mol/L, mL
print(f"\n{c_want:.3f} mol/L saline: take {c_want * V_want / c_stock:.2f} mL of "
      f"{c_stock:.2f} mol/L stock and make it up to {V_want:.0f} mL")

# Serial dilution: each step takes 1.00 mL and makes it up to 10.00 mL.
N_A = 6.02214076e23
c = 0.100
print("\nstep   c / (mol/L)   formula units in 1 mL")
for step in range(0, 23):
    if step % 4 == 0 or step in (19, 20):
        print(f"{step:4d}   {c:10.1e}   {c * 1e-3 * N_A:12.3g}")
    c /= 10
```

The model seawater is 0.6127 mol/L but 0.6206 mol/kg, about 1 % apart, because a litre of it contains only 0.987 kg of water; in dilute aqueous solutions the two are nearly equal, in concentrated ones very different. The mole fraction of salt is only 0.011: even in the sea, 99 of every 100 particles are water molecules.

<Example title="Making up a solution">

To prepare 250.0 mL of 0.154 mol/L sodium chloride (medical saline, 9.0 g/L) from a 2.00 mol/L stock, use $V_1 = c_2V_2/c_1 = 0.154 \times 250.0/2.00 = 19.25$ mL. Measure it with a pipette into a 250 mL **volumetric flask**, add water, mix, and make it up to the mark: you fill *up to* the final volume rather than adding a calculated volume of water, because volumes of mixed liquids are not exactly additive. From the solid it would be $0.154 \times 0.2500 \times 58.44 = 2.25$ g of salt, dissolved and made up to 250.0 mL.

</Example>

<Narrator who="margaret">

Look at the last rows of the serial dilution. Twenty tenfold steps from 0.1 mol/L leave 0.6 formula units per millilitre, so most millilitres contain no solute at all. Analytical chemists dilute in series all the time to make calibration standards, but Avogadro's number sets a hard floor on how far a dilution can go and still contain anything.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="like-dissolves-like" kind="human" title="Like dissolves like">

The working rule of thumb is **like dissolves like**: polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents such as water, and non-polar substances in non-polar solvents such as hexane. Sugar, with its many O–H groups, hydrogen-bonds to water and dissolves superbly (about 200 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C); ethanol mixes with water in all proportions; iodine barely dissolves in water but readily in hexane. Soap works because its molecules have an ionic head that likes water and a hydrocarbon tail that likes grease.

Why doesn't oil dissolve in water? Not because they repel: there are attractive dispersion forces between them (see <LessonLink slug="gases-liquids-and-solids">Gases, liquids and solids</LessonLink>). Water molecules next to a non-polar molecule cannot hydrogen-bond to it, so they order themselves into a cage around it to keep their hydrogen bonds with each other. That ordering *lowers* the entropy of the water, and the oil is pushed out instead. This **hydrophobic effect** also folds proteins and assembles cell membranes (<LessonLink slug="biochemistry">Biochemistry</LessonLink>).

**Temperature.** Most solids dissolve more in hot water than in cold (sugar's solubility more than doubles between 20 and 100 °C), but sodium chloride barely changes, and calcium carbonate becomes *less* soluble when hot, one reason scale forms in kettles. Gases always become less soluble as temperature rises: water at 0 °C holds about 14.6 mg of oxygen per litre from the air, at 25 °C only about 8.3 mg/L, which is why a warm, still river in summer can suffocate its fish.

**Pressure** hardly matters for solids and liquids, but for a gas, **Henry's law** says that its solubility is proportional to its partial pressure, $c = k_\text{H}\,p$. A fizzy drink is bottled under a few bar of carbon dioxide; open it and the excess gas comes out as bubbles. Divers breathing compressed air dissolve extra nitrogen in their blood, which is why they ascend slowly, following decompression tables.

</Section>

<Section id="ksp" kind="computation" title="Sparingly soluble salts and the solubility product">

"Insoluble" salts are never perfectly insoluble. A tiny amount of silver chloride dissolves in water, as ions, until the solid and its ions reach a dynamic equilibrium: AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).

<Definition term="solubility product">

For a salt $\mathrm{M}_a\mathrm{X}_b$ in equilibrium with its saturated solution, $\mathrm{M}_a\mathrm{X}_b(\text{s}) \rightleftharpoons a\,\mathrm{M}^{z+}(\text{aq}) + b\,\mathrm{X}^{y-}(\text{aq})$, the **solubility product** is

$$K_\text{sp} = [\mathrm{M}^{z+}]^a\,[\mathrm{X}^{y-}]^b,$$

with each concentration divided by 1 mol/L so that $K_\text{sp}$ is a pure number. The solid does not appear: however much of it is present, the concentrations in the saturated solution are the same. $K_\text{sp}$ depends on temperature. If no other source supplies the ions, the **molar solubility** $s$ satisfies $K_\text{sp} = (as)^a(bs)^b$.

</Definition>

This is the first equilibrium constant of this course; where such constants come from, and why the solid drops out, is the subject of <LessonLink slug="chemical-equilibrium">Chemical equilibrium</LessonLink>. Two consequences matter now. A solution that already contains one of the ions dissolves less of the salt, the **common-ion effect**, because the product must still equal $K_\text{sp}$. And when two solutions are mixed, a precipitate forms only if the **ion product** $Q$, computed just after mixing, exceeds $K_\text{sp}$.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Typical tabulated solubility products at 25 °C (sources differ, sometimes by a factor of 2 or more).
# Each salt M_a X_b dissolves as  M_a X_b(s) <=> a M + b X,  so  Ksp = (a s)^a (b s)^b.
salts = {                  # Ksp,      a, b, molar mass / (g/mol)
    "AgCl":     (1.77e-10, 1, 1, 143.32),
    "BaSO4":    (1.08e-10, 1, 1, 233.39),
    "CaCO3":    (3.36e-9,  1, 1, 100.09),
    "CaF2":     (3.45e-11, 1, 2,  78.07),
    "Mg(OH)2":  (5.61e-12, 1, 2,  58.32),
}

def molar_solubility(Ksp, a, b):
    """Solubility s in mol/L of M_a X_b in pure water (ideal; ignores hydrolysis and ion pairs)."""
    return (Ksp / (a**a * b**b)) ** (1 / (a + b))

print("salt       Ksp        s / (mol/L)   s / (mg/L)")
for name, (Ksp, a, b, M) in salts.items():
    s = molar_solubility(Ksp, a, b)
    print(f"{name:8s} {Ksp:9.2e}   {s:11.2e}   {1000 * s * M:9.2f}")

# The common-ion effect: AgCl in sodium chloride solution.  s (s + c) = Ksp, solved exactly.
Ksp = salts["AgCl"][0]
s0 = molar_solubility(Ksp, 1, 1)
print("\n[Cl-] already present   s(AgCl) / (mol/L)   fraction of pure-water value")
for c in (0.0, 1e-5, 1e-3, 0.1):
    s = max(np.roots([1.0, c, -Ksp]))           # the positive root of s^2 + c s - Ksp = 0
    print(f"{c:20.0e}   {s:16.2e}   {s / s0:12.2e}")

# Will a precipitate form?  Mix equal volumes of AgNO3 and NaCl solutions; compare Q with Ksp.
for c_each in (1e-4, 1e-5):
    Q = (c_each / 2) * (c_each / 2)             # both are halved by mixing
    verdict = "> Ksp: a precipitate forms" if Q > Ksp else "< Ksp: the mixture stays clear"
    print(f"\n{c_each:.0e} mol/L each: after mixing Q = {Q:.1e} {verdict}", end="")
print()
```

Silver chloride dissolves to only $1.33 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L, about 1.9 mg per litre, and in 0.1 mol/L sodium chloride ten thousand times less. Notice also that calcium fluoride has a smaller $K_\text{sp}$ than calcium carbonate but dissolves *more*, 16 mg/L against 5.8: a 1 : 2 salt's $K_\text{sp}$ contains $s^3$, not $s^2$, so solubility products can be compared directly only between salts of the same formula type.

<Example title="A barium meal">

Barium ions are toxic, yet radiographers give patients a suspension of barium sulfate to drink before X-raying the gut, because barium's heavy nucleus absorbs X-rays and outlines the stomach and intestines. It is safe because $K_\text{sp} = 1.08 \times 10^{-10}$ keeps the dissolved barium to about $1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L, around 1.4 mg of Ba²⁺ per litre, and the sulfate pushes it lower still by the common-ion effect. A soluble barium salt would be dangerous: the difference between a medicine and a poison here is a solubility product.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

These estimates assume the dissolved ions do nothing but float about. Real ions screen each other, which raises solubilities (activities, not concentrations, enter the true $K_\text{sp}$); carbonate reacts with water as a base, so calcium carbonate dissolves about twice as much as $\sqrt{K_\text{sp}}$ suggests, and four or five times more again with CO₂ from the air, which is how rain carves limestone caves. Trust simple $K_\text{sp}$ arithmetic for very dilute solutions only.

</Narrator>

Tooth enamel is a calcium phosphate mineral, hydroxyapatite; fluoride from toothpaste swaps into it to form fluorapatite, which is less soluble in the acids that bacteria make. Precipitation is also how analytical chemists separate and weigh many ions (<LessonLink slug="analytical-chemistry">Analytical chemistry</LessonLink>).

</Section>

<Section id="electrolytes" kind="human" title="Electrolytes">

Pure water barely conducts electricity; salt water conducts well. In 1884 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius proposed in his doctoral thesis that salts, acids and bases split into ions in solution even with no current flowing. Many senior chemists doubted it, but the idea explained conductivity and the colligative properties below, and earned him the 1903 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The **van 't Hoff factor** $i$ counts the dissolved particles per formula unit: ideally 1 for sugar, 2 for NaCl (Na⁺ + Cl⁻), 3 for CaCl₂ (Ca²⁺ + 2 Cl⁻). Measured values are a little lower except in very dilute solutions, about 1.9 for sodium chloride at everyday concentrations, because oppositely charged ions cluster loosely and do not act fully independently. A weak electrolyte has $i$ only slightly above 1; how much it ionises is a question for <LessonLink slug="acids-bases-and-buffers">Acids, bases and buffers</LessonLink>.

Electrolytes keep you alive. Nerve impulses are pulses of sodium and potassium ions crossing cell membranes, and blood plasma holds about 290 milliosmoles of dissolved particles per kilogram of water. Medical saline, 0.154 mol/L sodium chloride, has a similar particle concentration ($0.154 \times 1.9 \approx 0.29$), so it neither swells nor shrinks red blood cells: it is **isotonic**. Oral rehydration solution, a balanced mixture of glucose and salts in clean water developed in the 1960s and 1970s by researchers in Dhaka and Kolkata, uses the same ideas: glucose helps the gut absorb sodium, water follows by osmosis, and it has saved millions of children from death by dehydration in cholera and other diarrhoeal diseases.

</Section>

<Section id="colligative" kind="computation" title="Colligative properties">

Dissolving anything in a liquid lowers its vapour pressure and freezing point, raises its boiling point and creates an osmotic pressure. For dilute solutions all four depend only on the *number* of dissolved particles, not on what they are: they are **colligative** ("bound together") properties, measured systematically by François-Marie Raoult in the 1880s.

**Raoult's law** says that the vapour pressure of the solvent above an ideal solution is its pure vapour pressure times its mole fraction, $p_\text{A} = x_\text{A}\,p_\text{A}^*$: fewer solvent molecules at the surface escape per second. Everything else follows.

<Theorem name="Colligative properties of dilute solutions">

For a dilute solution with solute molality $b$ and van 't Hoff factor $i$, in a solvent with molar mass $M_\text{A}$:

$$\Delta T_\text{f} = iK_\text{f}b, \qquad \Delta T_\text{b} = iK_\text{b}b, \qquad \Pi = icRT,$$

where the freezing point falls by $\Delta T_\text{f}$, the boiling point rises by $\Delta T_\text{b}$, $c$ is the amount concentration (in mol/m³ with $R = 8.314$ J/(mol K), giving $\Pi$ in Pa), and

$$K_\text{f} = \frac{RT_\text{f}^{*2}M_\text{A}}{\Delta_\text{fus}H}, \qquad K_\text{b} = \frac{RT_\text{b}^{*2}M_\text{A}}{\Delta_\text{vap}H}.$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The solution boils when its vapour pressure $x_\text{A}p_\text{A}^*(T)$ reaches the outside pressure, which the pure solvent did at $T_\text{b}^*$; so $p_\text{A}^*(T)/p_\text{A}^*(T_\text{b}^*) = 1/x_\text{A}$. By the Clausius–Clapeyron equation (<LessonLink slug="gases-liquids-and-solids">Gases, liquids and solids</LessonLink>), the left side is $\exp[(\Delta_\text{vap}H/R)(1/T_\text{b}^* - 1/T)] \approx \exp[\Delta_\text{vap}H\,\Delta T/(RT_\text{b}^{*2})]$ for a small rise $\Delta T$, and for a dilute solution $\ln(1/x_\text{A}) \approx x_\text{B} \approx M_\text{A}\,ib$. Equating logarithms gives $\Delta T = (RT_\text{b}^{*2}M_\text{A}/\Delta_\text{vap}H)\,ib$. The freezing point is where the solution's vapour pressure meets that of the pure solid, whose curve is steeper by $\Delta_\text{fus}H$, so $\Delta_\text{fus}H$ replaces $\Delta_\text{vap}H$. The osmotic pressure needs the Gibbs energy of <LessonLink slug="entropy-and-free-energy">Entropy and free energy</LessonLink>.

</Proof>

Why should the solvent care only about *how many* particles are dissolved, and not what they are? Because the effect is about dilution, not forces: every dissolved particle lowers the solvent's mole fraction, and with it the solvent's tendency to escape, to freeze or to stay on its side of a membrane. A sodium ion and a sugar molecule dilute water equally well.

</Narrator>

The block derives water's constants from its own thermodynamic data, then applies the formulas to seawater, road salt and a protein.

```python run
R = 8.314462618                      # J/(mol K)
M_w = 0.018015                       # kg/mol, water

# Cryoscopic and ebullioscopic constants of water from its own thermodynamic data
T_f, dH_fus = 273.15, 6010.0         # K, J/mol
T_b, dH_vap = 373.12, 40660.0        # K, J/mol

K_b = R * T_b**2 * M_w / dH_vap
print(f"K_f = {K_f:.3f} K kg/mol (tables: 1.86)    K_b = {K_b:.3f} K kg/mol (tables: 0.51)")

# Model seawater: 35.0 g NaCl per kg of solution, i = 2 if every ion acts independently
b = (35.0 / 58.44) / 0.965           # mol/kg
c = (35.0 / 58.44) / (1000 / 1.023 / 1000)   # mol/L

# Road salt: how much salt per kilogram of water keeps it liquid at -5 °C and at -10 °C?  (ideal)
```

From the enthalpy of fusion and the melting point alone, the program reproduces the tabulated $K_\text{f}$ of 1.86 K kg/mol. The ideal model seawater freezes at −2.31 °C; real seawater freezes at about −1.9 °C, because its salts are not all sodium chloride and its ions do not act fully independently. Its boiling point rises by only 0.64 K, and pasta water, with perhaps 10 g of salt per litre, by under 0.2 K: cooks salt it for flavour, not for temperature.

### Why salt melts ice

Ice in contact with water at 0 °C is at equilibrium: molecules leave the ice and rejoin it at equal rates. Dissolve salt in the film of water on the ice and fewer water molecules are free to rejoin the crystal, while the ice keeps losing them as before, so it melts until the brine is dilute enough, or cold enough, to be at equilibrium again. Melting absorbs heat, so the mixture gets colder. Try it with an adult: put crushed ice in two bowls, stir a few spoonfuls of salt into one, and compare them with a kitchen thermometer that reads below zero. The salted bowl usually falls well below −5 °C, often near −10 °C, cold enough to hurt skin, so hold it with a cloth.

The block's table gives the ideal dose: about 79 g of sodium chloride per kilogram of water to stay liquid at −5 °C. Below −21 °C, the **eutectic** temperature, no sodium chloride solution can stay liquid, and in practice rock salt works slowly below about −10 °C, so road services switch to calcium chloride or to grit. Calcium chloride is chosen for its eutectic near −50 °C and the heat it releases on dissolving, not for its particles: per gram it gives fewer than sodium chloride, as the table shows. Salt has costs too: it corrodes cars and bridges (<LessonLink slug="electrochemistry">Electrochemistry</LessonLink>) and harms roadside plants and streams.

### Osmosis

A **semipermeable membrane** lets solvent molecules through but not solute particles. With pure water on one side and a solution on the other, water flows into the solution, **osmosis**, until the extra pressure on the solution side stops it; that pressure is the **osmotic pressure** $\Pi$. Jean-Antoine Nollet saw the effect in 1748, Wilhelm Pfeffer measured it in 1877, and in 1886 van 't Hoff showed that $\Pi = icRT$, the same form as the ideal gas law, work that helped earn him the first Nobel Prize in Chemistry, in 1901.

Osmotic pressures are large: the ideal model seawater has $\Pi = 30.4$ bar at 25 °C, and real seawater about 27 bar, enough to hold up a column of fresh water about 276 m tall. A red blood cell in pure water swells and bursts, and in strong brine it shrinks; plants stay upright because osmosis keeps their cells pressurised, and wilt when they lose water.

<Section id="desalination" kind="examples" title="Making seawater drinkable">

Push on seawater with more than its osmotic pressure and water flows *out* through a membrane, leaving the salt behind: **reverse osmosis**. Modern membranes are thin polyamide films on a porous support, wound into spiral modules. Distillation, which boils the water off, dominated in the Gulf states for decades, but it needs more energy, and reverse osmosis has overtaken it worldwide.

Thermodynamics sets a floor on the energy. As fresh water is removed, the remaining seawater gets saltier and its osmotic pressure rises, so the work grows with the **recovery** $r$, the fraction of the feed turned into fresh water. If $\Pi$ is proportional to concentration, the least work per cubic metre of fresh water is the average of $\Pi$ over the water removed, $W_\text{min} = (\Pi_0/r)\ln[1/(1 - r)]$. The block averages numerically with the midpoint rule, checks the formula, and then estimates a real single-stage plant.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Reverse osmosis: the least work needed to take fresh water out of seawater.
# Take the osmotic pressure of seawater as about 27 bar at 25 °C, and assume it is proportional
# to the salt concentration.  Recovering a fraction r of the feed as fresh water concentrates
# the brine left behind by 1/(1 - r), so the pressure needed keeps rising as water is removed.
Pi0 = 27e5                                        # Pa, which is also J/m^3

def least_work(r, n=100_000):
    """Reversible work per m^3 of fresh water: the average of Π over the water removed."""
    removed = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n * r        # midpoints of n slices, per m^3 of feed
    return np.mean(Pi0 / (1 - removed))           # J/m^3

print(" recovery  brine Π/bar   least work/(kWh/m^3)   formula (Π0/r) ln(1/(1-r))")
for r in (0.01, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75):
    exact = Pi0 / r * np.log(1 / (1 - r))
    print(f"   {r:4.2f}   {Pi0 / (1 - r) / 1e5:9.0f}   {least_work(r) / 3.6e6:14.3f}   {exact / 3.6e6:22.3f}")

# A real single-stage plant pushes all the feed at one pressure, above the outlet brine's Π.
p, r = 60e5, 0.5                                  # Pa, recovery
pump = p / r                                      # J per m^3 of fresh water (2 m^3 of feed pumped)
recovered = 0.95 * p * (1 - r) / r                # a pressure exchanger returns 95 % of the brine's energy
print(f"\nat {p / 1e5:.0f} bar and r = {r}: pump work {pump / 3.6e6:.2f} kWh/m^3 without energy recovery,"
      f" {(pump - recovered) / 3.6e6:.2f} with it")
```

At 50 % recovery the outgoing brine has twice the salt and an osmotic pressure of 54 bar, so plants run their pumps at roughly 55 to 70 bar. The least possible work is 1.04 kWh per cubic metre, close to the 1.06 kWh/m³ that Elimelech and Phillip give for real seawater. Throwing away the high-pressure brine would cost 3.33 kWh/m³ in pumping; **energy-recovery devices** that hand the brine's pressure back to the incoming seawater bring our idealised figure to 1.75. Whole modern plants use about 3 to 4 kWh/m³, a small multiple of the thermodynamic limit, so future savings will be modest.

There is also the brine. A plant at 50 % recovery returns a cubic metre of double-strength brine to the sea for every cubic metre of fresh water, and a 2019 global survey by Jones and colleagues estimated that the world's plants discharge more brine than the fresh water they make. Diffusers that dilute it quickly, and recovering minerals from it, are active areas of engineering.

**Solubility in industry.** Many promising drug molecules dissolve too poorly in water to be absorbed, and formulation scientists work hard on salts, co-crystals and particle size to get them into solution (<LessonLink slug="medicinal-chemistry">Medicinal chemistry</LessonLink>). Peter Debye and Erich Hückel explained in 1923 how the ionic atmosphere around each ion lowers its activity; for brines, geochemists use Kenneth Pitzer's equations, built into programs such as [PHREEQC](https://www.usgs.gov/software/phreeqc-version-3), and oceanographers describe seawater with [TEOS-10](https://www.teos-10.org/). <Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"When salt dissolves it disappears, or turns into something else."** The ions are still there, spread through the water: evaporate the water and the salt comes back, and the solution's mass is the mass of salt plus water. Dissolving a salt is a physical change for the purpose of everyday chemistry, though at the molecular level the ions have swapped their neighbours in the crystal for hydration shells.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Salt melts ice because it reacts with it and gives out heat."** Sodium chloride absorbs a little heat as it dissolves, and the salted ice gets *colder*, not warmer. It melts because the dissolved ions lower the freezing point of the water in contact with the ice.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A smaller solubility product always means a less soluble salt."** Only for salts of the same formula type. Calcium fluoride has a $K_\text{sp}$ a hundred times smaller than calcium carbonate's, yet dissolves about three and a half times more, in moles per litre.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"In osmosis, the salt moves through the membrane."** The membrane stops the solute; it is the *water* that moves, from the side where it is more concentrated (purer) to the side where it is less.

</Misconception>
