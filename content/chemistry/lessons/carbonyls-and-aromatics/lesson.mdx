<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Two families that built modern chemistry">

In 1856 William Henry Perkin, an eighteen-year-old student in London, was trying to make the antimalarial quinine from coal-tar chemicals. He failed, and found instead a purple dye, mauveine, which he went on to manufacture. Within a few decades synthetic dyes had grown into a large industry in Britain, Germany and Switzerland, and the companies built on them later turned to medicines. Mauveine, like nearly every dye and drug that followed, is made of two kinds of building block: **aromatic rings** and groups containing the **carbonyl** group, C=O.

The pattern holds today. Benzene is the most common ring in marketed medicines ([Taylor and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1021/jm4017625)); making an amide, a carbonyl compound, is the reaction medicinal chemists run most often ([Brown and Boström, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01409)); paracetamol, aspirin and ibuprofen are each an aromatic ring carrying carbonyl groups. Proteins are chains of amides, sugars are carbonyl compounds in disguise, and the colour of indigo, alizarin and food dyes comes from carbonyls and rings working together.

This lesson explains why. With the curly arrows of <LessonLink slug="reaction-mechanisms">Reaction mechanisms</LessonLink> and the orbitals of <LessonLink slug="molecular-orbitals">Molecular orbitals</LessonLink>, the chemistry of both families follows from a few ideas: a polarised π bond with a low-lying empty orbital, and a ring of six π electrons that would rather be substituted than broken.

</Section>

<Section id="carbonyl" kind="intuition" title="The carbonyl group: a polarised double bond">

Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the C=O bond is polarised, C<sup>δ+</sup>=O<sup>δ−</sup>. Two consequences follow. Nucleophiles attack the **carbon**; acids and electrophiles bind to the **oxygen**, which makes the carbon even more electron-poor. And because the π bond is weaker than the σ bond, a nucleophile can add to carbon while the π electrons retreat onto oxygen, giving a tetrahedral **alkoxide**.

Hückel theory with a heteroatom makes the picture quantitative. Oxygen's orbital is given a lower energy, $\alpha_\mathrm{O} = \alpha + h\beta$ with $h \approx 1$, and the 2 × 2 matrix of <LessonLink slug="molecular-orbitals">Molecular orbitals</LessonLink> does the rest. The same code handles propenal, CH₂=CH–CH=O, where a C=C bond is conjugated with the carbonyl.

```python run
import numpy as np

def huckel(n_atoms, bonds, h=None):
    """Hueckel pi orbitals with heteroatoms. The matrix has h_i on the diagonal (alpha_i = alpha + h_i beta)
    and k_ij for each bond (beta_ij = k_ij beta). Returns x, with E = alpha + x beta, most bonding first,
    and the coefficients (one MO per column)."""
    A = np.zeros((n_atoms, n_atoms))
    for i, j, k in bonds:
        A[i, j] = A[j, i] = k
    for i, hi in (h or {}).items():
        A[i, i] = hi
    x, C = np.linalg.eigh(A)
    return x[::-1], C[:, ::-1]

def pi_charges(C, n_electrons):
    occ = np.zeros(C.shape[1])
    occ[: n_electrons // 2] = 2
    return 1 - (C**2 * occ).sum(axis=1)     # each atom brings one pi electron, minus what it holds

# Methanal, H2C=O: atom 0 = C, atom 1 = O, with h_O = 1 (a common textbook value for carbonyl oxygen).
x, C = huckel(2, [(0, 1, 1.0)], {1: 1.0})
q = pi_charges(C, 2)
print("methanal        C      O")
print(f"pi  (x = {x[0]:+.2f}) {abs(C[0, 0]):5.2f}  {abs(C[1, 0]):5.2f}")
print(f"pi* (x = {x[1]:+.2f}) {abs(C[0, 1]):5.2f}  {abs(C[1, 1]):5.2f}   <- the LUMO")
print(f"pi charge      {q[0]:+5.2f}  {q[1]:+5.2f}")

# Propenal, CH2=CH-CH=O: atoms O, C1 (the carbonyl carbon), C2, C3 (the far end of the C=C)
x, C = huckel(4, [(0, 1, 1.0), (1, 2, 1.0), (2, 3, 1.0)], {0: 1.0})
q = pi_charges(C, 4)
print("\npropenal          O     C1     C2     C3")
print("pi charge   " + "".join(f"{v:+7.2f}" for v in q))
print("LUMO coeff  " + "".join(f"{abs(v):7.2f}" for v in C[:, 2]))
print(f"LUMO at x = {x[2]:+.3f} (ethene's LUMO is at x = -1)")
```

In methanal the filled π orbital has 0.85 of its amplitude on oxygen, but the empty π* has 0.85 on **carbon**. A nucleophile's electrons go into the LUMO, so they flow to carbon. The LUMO also lies lower than ethene's ($\alpha + 0.62|\beta|$ against $\alpha + |\beta|$), which is why a carbonyl group accepts nucleophiles that ignore an ordinary C=C bond.

Propenal adds a subtlety. The largest positive charge is on the carbonyl carbon C1 (+0.33), but the largest LUMO coefficient is on C3 (0.66), the far end of the C=C bond. Small, highly charged nucleophiles such as hydride donors and organolithium reagents, whose reactions are governed mainly by electrostatics, tend to add to C1 (**1,2-addition**). Larger, more polarisable nucleophiles such as thiols, amines and enolates, whose reactions are governed by orbital overlap, add to C3 (**conjugate** or **1,4-addition**, the Michael reaction). Several modern cancer medicines, ibrutinib and osimertinib among them, carry a small C=C–C=O unit that makes a covalent bond to one particular cysteine thiol in their target protein by exactly this conjugate addition.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Notice what decided the site of attack: not where the negative charge sits, but where the empty orbital is largest. The carbonyl oxygen is the most negative atom in the molecule, yet a nucleophile ignores it, because the electrons it brings must go *into* π*, and π* lives mostly on carbon. That one fact organises the whole of carbonyl chemistry.

</Narrator>

<Section id="addition" kind="definitions" title="Addition, substitution and condensation">

What happens after the first attack depends on what is attached to the carbonyl carbon.

<Definition term="nucleophilic addition">

In aldehydes (RCHO) and ketones (R₂CO) the carbon carries only hydrogen and carbon, neither of which can leave. The tetrahedral alkoxide picks up a proton, and the overall result is **addition** across the C=O bond.

</Definition>

Water adds to give a **hydrate**, R₂C(OH)₂, reversibly. The equilibrium constant shows how electrophilic the carbonyl is: roughly $2 \times 10^3$ for methanal (in water it is almost all hydrate), about 1 for ethanal, and about $10^{-3}$ for propanone, whose two methyl groups both feed electron density to the carbon and crowd the tetrahedral product. Alcohols add in the same way to give **hemiacetals**; glucose spends nearly all its time in water as a six-membered ring formed when one of its own OH groups adds to its aldehyde (<LessonLink slug="biochemistry">Biochemistry</LessonLink>). Hydride donors such as sodium borohydride add H⁻ to carbon and reduce aldehydes and ketones to alcohols, and carbon nucleophiles such as Grignard reagents (Victor Grignard, Nobel Prize 1912) add a new C–C bond, one of the main ways chemists build carbon skeletons.

<Definition term="nucleophilic acyl substitution">

In carboxylic acid derivatives (RCOX) the carbon carries a group X that can leave. The tetrahedral intermediate collapses, the C=O bond re-forms and X⁻ is expelled: overall, X is **replaced** by the nucleophile, by addition followed by elimination.

</Definition>

The better the leaving group, and the less the group X donates its lone pair into the C=O bond, the more reactive the derivative:

| Derivative | Example | X | Reactivity towards water |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| acyl chloride | ethanoyl chloride | Cl | violent, fast at room temperature |
| anhydride | ethanoic anhydride | OCOCH₃ | fast |
| ester | ethyl ethanoate | OCH₂CH₃ | slow without acid or base catalysis |
| amide | ethanamide | NH₂ | very slow; days of heating with strong acid or base |

The amide sits at the bottom because nitrogen's lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl: the C–N bond has partial double-bond character, the amide is planar, and rotating about the C–N bond costs about 80 kJ/mol. That delocalisation is what makes proteins durable, and it can be switched off on purpose. In **penicillin** the amide is bent into a strained four-membered ring that cannot be planar; the lone pair can no longer delocalise, and the ring amide acylates a serine in a bacterial cell-wall enzyme far more readily than an ordinary amide could, which is how the drug kills bacteria. **Aspirin** works the same way: it is an ester that transfers its acetyl group to a serine of the enzyme cyclooxygenase.

<Definition term="condensation">

A **condensation** joins two molecules with the loss of a small one, usually water. Carbonyl chemistry has three classic examples: an aldehyde or ketone with a primary amine gives an **imine** (C=N); a carboxylic acid with an alcohol gives an **ester**; and a carboxylic acid with an amine gives an **amide**.

</Definition>

Imine formation is everywhere in biochemistry. In the eye, the aldehyde retinal is held to the protein opsin by an imine with a lysine side chain; absorbing a photon twists one of retinal's C=C bonds, and that is the first chemical step of vision. Repeat a condensation at both ends of two molecules and you get a **condensation polymer**: polyesters such as PET from a diacid and a diol, and polyamides such as nylon-6,6 from a diacid and a diamine (see [polymers](/en/materials/polymers/) in Lumenary Materials).

</Section>

<Section id="enolates" kind="examples" title="Enols and enolates">

The carbonyl group makes its neighbours reactive too. A C–H bond next to C=O (on the **α-carbon**) is unusually acidic: propanone has p$K_\mathrm{a}$ about 19, against about 50 for an alkane, because the anion left behind, the **enolate**, spreads its negative charge onto oxygen.

<Example title="Keto and enol forms">

Propanone, CH₃COCH₃, and its enol, CH₂=C(OH)CH₃, are **tautomers**: isomers that interconvert by moving a proton and a double bond. They are different compounds, not two drawings of one, and the equilibrium lies far towards the keto form, which has the stronger C=O bond: only a few molecules in every billion are enol. In pentane-2,4-dione, where the enol's C=C is conjugated with the second carbonyl and its OH can hydrogen-bond to it, the enol is the major form in the pure liquid.

</Example>

An enolate is a **carbon nucleophile**, and that makes it one of the most valuable species in synthesis. Its negative charge is shared between oxygen and the α-carbon, and with most electrophiles it reacts at carbon, making a new C–C bond.

- In the **aldol reaction**, an enolate adds to the carbonyl of a second aldehyde or ketone, giving a β-hydroxy carbonyl compound (an "aldehyde-alcohol", hence the name); losing water then gives a C=C conjugated with the C=O (the aldol condensation). The enzyme aldolase runs the reverse reaction in glycolysis, splitting a six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon pieces.
- In the **Claisen condensation**, an ester enolate attacks a second ester, which then loses its alkoxide by acyl substitution. Cells build fatty acids two carbons at a time with Claisen-type condensations of thioesters.

When a ketone has two different α-carbons, which enolate forms is a case of kinetic or thermodynamic control, exactly as in <LessonLink slug="reaction-mechanisms">Reaction mechanisms</LessonLink>. For 2-methylcyclohexanone, a strong, bulky base at −78 °C removes the more accessible proton fastest and the enolate is trapped before it can equilibrate (the kinetic enolate, with the less substituted C=C); a weaker base at room temperature lets the enolates interconvert, and the more substituted, more stable one wins.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Before calling two drawings resonance structures, check one condition: are all the nuclei in the same places? Keto and enol forms fail it, because a *proton* has moved: they are two molecules, each with its own spectrum and energy, and their ratio is an equilibrium constant that depends on solvent and temperature. An enolate's "carbanion" and "alkoxide" drawings pass it: only *electrons* are drawn differently, so they are two pictures of one ion.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="aromatic" kind="intuition" title="Aromatic rings: substitution, not addition">

<LessonLink slug="molecular-orbitals">Molecular orbitals</LessonLink> showed where benzene's special stability comes from: six π electrons exactly fill the three bonding orbitals of a six-membered ring, the first case of Hückel's $4n + 2$ rule, worth about 150 kJ/mol of extra stability. Chemically this means benzene does *not* behave like three alkenes. Cyclohexene decolourises bromine in seconds by adding Br₂ across its C=C bond; benzene ignores bromine unless a catalyst is present, and then it gives bromobenzene, C₆H₅Br, by **substitution**. Addition would destroy the aromatic sextet; substitution gives it back.

<Definition term="electrophilic aromatic substitution">

The aromatic π system acts as a nucleophile towards a strong electrophile E⁺. In a slow first step the ring donates a pair of π electrons to E, giving a carbocation called the **arenium ion** (σ-complex, or Wheland intermediate), in which one carbon is sp³ and the positive charge is shared by the other five. In a fast second step a base removes the proton from the sp³ carbon and its C–H electrons restore the aromatic ring. Overall, H is replaced by E.

</Definition>

The art lies in making a strong enough electrophile, usually with a Lewis acid. For **halogenation**, iron(III) bromide polarises Br₂ so that one bromine becomes electrophilic. For **Friedel–Crafts acylation** (Charles Friedel and James Crafts, Paris, 1877), aluminium chloride pulls chloride from an acyl chloride to give an acylium ion, R–C≡O⁺, which attaches a ketone group to the ring. **Sulfonation** uses sulfur trioxide, and **nitration** the nitronium ion, NO₂⁺. These four reactions, and the coupling of aromatic rings with diazonium ions that makes azo dyes, are how most aromatic compounds of industry have been made since the nineteenth century.

<Section id="directing" kind="computation" title="Directing effects">

A substituent already on the ring changes both **how fast** the next substitution goes and **where** it goes. The arenium ion explains both. Draw the three resonance structures for attack *ortho* (next to the substituent) or *para* (opposite it): one of them always puts the positive charge on the carbon carrying the substituent. Attack *meta* never does.

- A substituent with a **lone pair** (–OH, –OR, –NH₂, –NHCOR) can donate it to that positive carbon, giving a fourth resonance structure in which every atom has an octet. Ortho and para attack are strongly favoured, and the ring reacts faster than benzene: these groups are **activating, ortho/para-directing**. Alkyl groups do the same, more weakly, by hyperconjugation.
- An **electron-withdrawing** group (–NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR, –COOR, –CF₃) has a δ+ atom next to the ring. Ortho or para attack would put a positive charge right beside it, so those positions are hit hardest, and what remains is meta: these groups are **deactivating, meta-directing**.
- **Halogens** are the exception that proves the rule: they withdraw electrons through the σ bond (deactivating), but donate a lone pair to the arenium ion (ortho/para-directing).

Wheland turned this argument into a number in 1942. The **localisation energy** of a position is the π energy lost when that carbon is pulled out of the π system, with two electrons, to bond to the electrophile: in Hückel terms, the π energy of the arene minus that of the arenium ion. By the Hammond postulate the arenium ion resembles the transition state of the slow step, so a lower localisation energy means a faster reaction. The program computes it for each position of four substituted rings, with standard textbook heteroatom parameters.

```python run
import numpy as np

def pi_energy(n_atoms, bonds, h, n_electrons):
    """Total Hueckel pi energy, as the coefficient of beta (E = n alpha + X beta, beta < 0)."""
    A = np.zeros((n_atoms, n_atoms))
    for i, j, k in bonds:
        A[i, j] = A[j, i] = k
    for i, hi in h.items():
        A[i, i] = hi
    x = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(A))[::-1]
    occ = np.zeros(n_atoms)
    occ[: n_electrons // 2] = 2
    return float(occ @ x)

def localisation(n_atoms, bonds, h, n_electrons, r):
    """Wheland's localisation energy for attack at atom r, in units of |beta|: the pi energy lost
    when atom r and two pi electrons are taken out of the pi system to bond to the electrophile."""
    keep = [i for i in range(n_atoms) if i != r]
    new = {old: k for k, old in enumerate(keep)}
    bonds2 = [(new[i], new[j], k) for i, j, k in bonds if r not in (i, j)]
    h2 = {new[i]: v for i, v in h.items() if i != r}
    return pi_energy(n_atoms, bonds, h, n_electrons) - pi_energy(n_atoms - 1, bonds2, h2, n_electrons - 2)

ring = [(i, (i + 1) % 6, 1.0) for i in range(6)]      # atom 0 carries the substituent
# Substituent models (standard textbook Hueckel parameters h and k):
molecules = {
    "benzene":        (6, ring, {}, 6),
    "methylbenzene":  (6, ring, {0: -0.5}, 6),                           # CH3 makes C0 electron-richer
    "methoxybenzene": (7, ring + [(0, 6, 0.8)], {6: 2.0}, 8),            # -O-: lone pair, 2 pi electrons
    "benzaldehyde":   (8, ring + [(0, 6, 1.0), (6, 7, 1.0)], {7: 1.0}, 8),  # -CH=O conjugated
    "pyridine":       (6, ring, {0: 0.5}, 6),                            # N in place of C0
}
print(f"{'':15s} {'ortho':>7s} {'meta':>7s} {'para':>7s}   localisation energy / |beta|")
for name, (n, bonds, h, ne) in molecules.items():
    L = [localisation(n, bonds, h, ne, r) for r in (1, 2, 3)]
    best = "all equal" if max(L) - min(L) < 1e-9 else ["ortho", "meta", "para"][int(np.argmin(L))]
    print(f"{name:15s} {L[0]:7.3f} {L[1]:7.3f} {L[2]:7.3f}   lowest: {best}")
print("(for pyridine, ortho/meta/para mean the 2-, 3- and 4-positions)")
```

Benzene's localisation energy is $2.536|\beta|$ at every position. The methyl and methoxy groups lower it at ortho and para (2.35–2.41) and leave meta essentially unchanged: activating, ortho/para-directing. The aldehyde group raises it everywhere, least at meta (2.549), most at para: deactivating, meta-directing, with ortho close behind. Pyridine, where a nitrogen replaces one CH, behaves like benzene with a built-in acceptor, and is attacked at the 3-position, as observed. A model with two parameters per substituent and no electron repulsion gets every direction right.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The model ranks ortho and para close together, but it has no idea that ortho is next door to the substituent. A bulky group shields its ortho positions, so real reactions of methylbenzene or methoxybenzene usually give more para product than the two ortho positions would suggest. And pyridine is far less reactive than the model's 2.538 implies, because under the acidic conditions of most substitutions its nitrogen is protonated, and a positively charged ring is a very poor nucleophile.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="hammett" kind="computation" title="Measuring substituent effects: the Hammett equation">

In 1937 Louis Hammett at Columbia University found a way to put numbers on "electron-donating" and "electron-withdrawing". He took the ionisation of substituted benzoic acids in water as a standard and defined, for each substituent X in the meta or para position, the **substituent constant**

$$\sigma_\mathrm{X} = \log_{10}\frac{K_\mathrm{X}}{K_\mathrm{H}} = \mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}(\mathrm{H}) - \mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}(\mathrm{X}).$$

Positive σ means electron-withdrawing (the acid is stronger); negative means electron-donating. He then found that for many other reactions of the same substituted rings, rate or equilibrium constants obey

$$\log_{10}\frac{k_\mathrm{X}}{k_\mathrm{H}} = \rho\,\sigma_\mathrm{X},$$

a straight line whose slope, the **reaction constant** ρ, measures how sensitive the reaction is to electrons in the ring. Positive ρ means negative charge builds up in the transition state (or product); negative ρ means positive charge does. This is the **linear free-energy relationship** mentioned in <LessonLink slug="reaction-mechanisms">Reaction mechanisms</LessonLink>: logarithms of $K$ and $k$ are free energies, and the equation says substituent effects on two different reactions are proportional.

Here it is applied to the acidity of phenols, using measured p$K_\mathrm{a}$ values and the σ values of the [Hansch–Leo–Taft compilation](https://doi.org/10.1021/cr00002a004).

```python run
import numpy as np

# Substituted phenols in water at 25 C: Hammett sigma (from benzoic acids), sigma-minus where it
# differs, and the measured pKa (rounded; compilations differ by a few hundredths).
phenols = {            # sigma, sigma_minus, pKa
    "H":      (0.00, None, 9.99),
    "4-CH3":  (-0.17, None, 10.26),
    "3-CH3":  (-0.07, None, 10.09),
    "4-OCH3": (-0.27, None, 10.21),
    "3-OCH3": (0.12, None, 9.65),
    "4-Cl":   (0.23, None, 9.41),
    "3-Cl":   (0.37, None, 9.12),
    "3-CN":   (0.56, None, 8.61),
    "3-NO2":  (0.71, None, 8.36),
    "4-CN":   (0.66, 1.00, 7.95),
    "4-NO2":  (0.78, 1.27, 7.15),
}
pKa_H = phenols["H"][2]
# Fit log(K/K_H) = pKa_H - pKa = rho * sigma, using every phenol except the two para acceptors.
fit = [n for n, (s, s_minus, p) in phenols.items() if s_minus is None]
sigma = np.array([phenols[n][0] for n in fit])
logK = np.array([pKa_H - phenols[n][2] for n in fit])
rho, c = np.polyfit(sigma, logK, 1)
r = np.corrcoef(sigma, logK)[0, 1]
print(f"rho = {rho:.2f}, intercept = {c:+.2f}, r = {r:.3f}  ({len(fit)} phenols)\n")
print("phenol   measured  predicted from sigma  from sigma-minus")
for n, (s, s_minus, p) in phenols.items():
    pred = pKa_H - (c + rho * s)
    extra = f"{pKa_H - (c + rho * s_minus):10.2f}" if s_minus is not None else ""
    print(f"{n:7s} {p:8.2f} {pred:12.2f}            {extra}")
```

Nine phenols fall on a line with ρ = 2.09 and correlation coefficient 0.990: phenol ionisation is about twice as sensitive to substituents as benzoic acid ionisation, because the negative charge of a phenoxide sits on an oxygen attached directly to the ring rather than one atom further away. Two compounds miss badly. 4-Nitrophenol is predicted to have p$K_\mathrm{a}$ 8.24 and actually has 7.15, more than ten times more acidic than the line allows; 4-cyanophenol misses by half a unit. Both have a para acceptor that can take the phenoxide's negative charge directly by resonance, which a benzoic acid cannot do. Hammett's successors handled this with a second scale, **σ⁻**, measured on reactions where such direct conjugation happens; with σ⁻ both phenols return to within 0.2 of the line. The same idea with positive charge gives **σ⁺**, the right scale for electrophilic aromatic substitution, where reaction constants are large and negative.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Put the slope into everyday terms. With ρ = 2.09, a substituent with σ = 0.5 makes the phenol $10^{2.09 \times 0.5} \approx 11$ times more acidic. Since $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$, each unit of $\log_{10} K$ is 5.7 kJ/mol at 25 °C, so that same substituent changes the free energy of ionisation by about 6 kJ/mol. Hammett plots are free-energy bookkeeping at the level of a few kilojoules.

</Narrator>

<Section id="medicines-and-dyes" kind="human" title="Why medicines and dyes are built from these pieces">

**Dyes need conjugation.** A molecule is coloured when its HOMO–LUMO gap falls in the visible range (<LessonLink slug="spectroscopy">Spectroscopy</LessonLink>), and the gap shrinks as a π system grows. Aromatic rings and carbonyl groups are the easiest way to build long, rigid, stable π systems, and pairing an electron donor (an amino or hydroxy group on a ring) with an acceptor (a carbonyl or an azo group) shrinks the gap further. **Alizarin**, the red of madder root, is an anthraquinone: three fused rings with two carbonyls. In 1868 Carl Graebe and Carl Liebermann in Berlin made it from coal-tar anthracene, the first natural dye made synthetically. **Indigo**, the blue of denim, has two carbonyls conjugated through a C=C bond with two aromatic rings; Adolf von Baeyer worked out its structure and a synthesis, and BASF began selling synthetic indigo in 1897. **Azo dyes**, Ar–N=N–Ar′, are made by an electrophilic aromatic substitution in which the electrophile is an aryldiazonium ion; methyl orange, the acid–base indicator, and food colours such as Sunset Yellow FCF and Allura Red AC are azo dyes.

**Medicines need shape, contacts and stability.** A drug must fit a protein pocket, stick to it and survive the body for hours. Aromatic rings are flat and rigid, fill hydrophobic pockets, and stack against the rings of phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan side chains. Amides are stable in water and provide a hydrogen-bond donor (N–H) and acceptor (C=O) in a fixed geometry, the same contacts that hold proteins together. Both are also easy to *make*: electrophilic substitution and the cross-coupling reactions of <LessonLink slug="synthesis-and-retrosynthesis">Synthesis and retrosynthesis</LessonLink> decorate rings at will, and amide formation is reliable enough to be run by robots. The ease has a cost. Medicinal chemists noticed that molecules with many flat aromatic rings tend to be poorly soluble and more often fail in development, and since [Lovering and colleagues' 2009 analysis](https://doi.org/10.1021/jm901241e) the field has deliberately made drug candidates more three-dimensional, with more sp³ carbon (<LessonLink slug="medicinal-chemistry">Medicinal chemistry</LessonLink>).

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="reading" title="The professional view">

In industry, carbonyl and aromatic chemistry is mostly a question of **selectivity and conditions**. Classical electrophilic substitutions use strong acids and stoichiometric Lewis acids that end up as waste, so process chemists replace them where they can with solid acid catalysts, with cross-couplings, or with **C–H functionalisation**, in which a metal catalyst converts a specific ring C–H bond directly. A well-known example is the BHC route to ibuprofen, which shortened the original six-step synthesis to three catalytic steps, the first of them a Friedel–Crafts acylation (<LessonLink slug="catalysis-and-green-chemistry">Catalysis and green chemistry</LessonLink>). Amide formation, the most frequent reaction in drug discovery, is being moved from wasteful activating reagents towards catalytic and enzymatic methods for the same reason.

Hammett's σ values survive as descriptors in modern structure–activity models, and substituent effects are now also computed directly. Programs predict p$K_\mathrm{a}$ values and the most reactive site of a ring for electrophilic substitution from quantum-chemical calculations of proton affinities and arenium-ion energies, the modern version of Wheland's localisation energy (<LessonLink slug="computational-chemistry">Computational chemistry</LessonLink>), and machine-learned models trained on those calculations make the same predictions in milliseconds (<LessonLink slug="ai-for-chemistry">AI for chemistry</LessonLink>).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Nucleophiles attack the carbonyl oxygen because it has the lone pairs."** Lone pairs make oxygen a *nucleophile* and a base, the site where protons and Lewis acids bind. Nucleophiles attack the carbon, where the C=O π* orbital is largest.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Benzene is cyclohexatriene, so it reacts like an alkene."** Its six π electrons are delocalised over six equal bonds, and the aromatic stabilisation would be lost on addition. It reacts with electrophiles by substitution, and only with strong electrophiles.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Halogens direct ortho and para, so they must be activating."** Direction and rate are separate questions. Halogens slow the reaction down by withdrawing electrons through the σ bond, yet their lone pairs still make ortho and para the least unfavourable positions.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A meta-director makes the meta position more reactive."** It makes every position less reactive than in benzene; meta is simply hit least. Compare the program's benzaldehyde row: all three localisation energies are above benzene's.

</Misconception>

</Section>
