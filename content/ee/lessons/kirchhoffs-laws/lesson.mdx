<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="When a circuit has more than one loop">

In <LessonLink slug="circuits-and-ohms-law">Circuits and Ohm's law</LessonLink> every circuit was a single loop: one battery, one or two resistors, one current. Ohm's law, $V = IR$, was enough. But open almost any electronic device and the circuit is a *network*. A phone's charging port feeds the battery and the processor at the same time. A car's lighting has the alternator and the battery both connected to the same wires. Even a humble sensor board has a reference voltage shared by several resistors, each carrying a different current.

Ohm's law describes one component. It says nothing about how components *connect*. For that we need two more laws, published in 1845 by Gustav Kirchhoff, then a 21-year-old student in Königsberg:

- **Kirchhoff's current law (KCL):** the currents flowing into any junction add up to zero. What flows in must flow out.
- **Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL):** the voltages around any closed loop add up to zero. Go round a loop and you come back to the potential you started from.

These two sentences, together with one law per component such as Ohm's law, are enough to work out every current and every voltage in any circuit of resistors and sources, however tangled. Every method in the rest of this level, and every circuit simulator, is a way of organising them efficiently. This lesson is about the laws themselves: what they say, why they are true, and how to use them without sign mistakes.

</Section>

<Section id="anatomy" kind="definitions" title="Nodes, branches and loops">

To apply the laws we need words for the parts of a circuit's shape.

<Definition term="Branch, node, loop, mesh">

A **branch** is a single two-terminal element: a resistor, a source, a wire with a component in it. A **node** is a point where two or more branches meet; all the wire joining those terminals is part of the same node, because an ideal wire has no resistance and so no voltage across it. A **loop** is any closed path through the circuit that passes through no node twice. A **mesh** is a loop that has no other branch inside it, like a window pane in a drawing of a flat circuit.

</Definition>

Count the figure's parts. There are $n = 4$ nodes (a, b, c and ground) and $b = 5$ branches. There are three loops: the left window (V1, R1, R3), the right window (R3, R2, V2) and the outer rim (V1, R1, R2, V2). The two windows are the meshes.

How many *independent* equations do the laws give? For a connected circuit:

- KCL can be written at every node, but only $n - 1$ of those equations are independent. The last one is the sum of the others (every branch current enters one node and leaves another, so adding all $n$ equations gives $0 = 0$).
- KVL can be written round every loop, but only $b - n + 1$ are independent. For a flat circuit the meshes are a convenient independent set; the outer loop of the figure is the sum of the two meshes.

Here that is $3 + 2 = 5$ equations, one for each of the five branches. That is no coincidence: once Ohm's law ties each resistor's voltage to its current, there are exactly as many independent Kirchhoff equations as unknown branch currents.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The number $b - n + 1$ depends only on the circuit's *graph*, not on what the components are: it is the number of independent cycles, what graph theorists call the cycle rank. Kirchhoff proved it himself in 1847 by picking a tree that touches every node; each branch left out of the tree closes exactly one loop. Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.discrete.graph-theory">Graph theory</TopicLink> has the full story.

</Narrator>

Many nodes in the figure are not very interesting: node a only joins V1 to R1, so whatever flows through one flows through the other. Engineers often count **essential nodes** (where three or more branches meet: b and ground) and **essential branches** (paths between essential nodes: left, middle, right). The counting works with those too: three essential branches and two essential nodes give $3 - 2 + 1 = 2$ independent loops and $2 - 1 = 1$ KCL equation (at b), three equations for three unknown branch currents.

</Section>

<Section id="signs" kind="definitions" title="Signs: reference directions and the passive sign convention">

Most errors in circuit analysis are sign errors, so it pays to be fussy from the start.

Before solving, you *choose* a direction for each unknown current (an arrow) and a polarity for each unknown voltage (a $+$ and a $-$). These are **reference directions**. They are bookkeeping, not predictions. If the true current flows the other way, the algebra simply returns a negative number. A negative answer is never a mistake in itself; it is information.

<Definition term="Passive sign convention">

For a two-terminal element with voltage $v$ (measured from its $+$ terminal to its $-$ terminal) and current $i$, the **passive sign convention** chooses the current's reference arrow to *enter* the $+$ terminal. Then

$$
p = v\,i
$$

is the power **absorbed** by the element. If $p > 0$ the element takes energy from the circuit (a resistor heating, a battery being charged); if $p < 0$ it delivers energy to the circuit (a battery discharging).

</Definition>

A resistor obeys Ohm's law in the form $v = Ri$ *only* when its labels follow the passive convention; with the arrow entering the $-$ terminal you must write $v = -Ri$. So the habit is: label each resistor's voltage with $+$ where its current arrow enters. A source is different: its voltage is given, and its current arrow is usually drawn leaving its $+$ terminal, the way a source normally pushes current. That arrow enters the $-$ terminal, so the source *absorbs* $p = -v\,i$, which is negative when it delivers power.

<Example title="Is it delivering or absorbing?">

A 9 V battery has a current of 50 mA entering its $+$ terminal. With the passive convention, $p = 9 \times 0.05 = 0.45$ W absorbed: the battery is being charged. If instead the measured current entering the $+$ terminal is $-50$ mA (that is, 50 mA leaving it), then $p = -0.45$ W: the battery delivers 0.45 W to the circuit. Same element, same arithmetic, and the sign tells you which way the energy flows.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="kcl" kind="intuition" title="Kirchhoff's current law: conservation of charge">

<Definition term="Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)">

At any node, at every instant, the algebraic sum of the branch currents is zero:

$$
\sum_{k} i_k = 0,
$$

counting currents whose arrows point into the node as positive and those pointing out as negative (or the other way round, consistently). Equivalently, the total current in equals the total current out.

</Definition>

**Why it is true.** Charge is conserved: it is never created or destroyed, only moved. If more current flowed into a node than out, charge would pile up there, at a rate equal to the difference. In a circuit drawing, a node is just a bit of ideal wire, and we assume it can hold no net charge. So whatever arrives must leave at once.

<Analogy>

Think of a junction of water pipes, completely full and with rigid walls. Water cannot be squeezed, so the litres per second flowing in through some pipes must equal the litres per second flowing out through the others. If two pipes bring in 3 and 2 litres per second and a third carries 4 away, the fourth must carry away 1 litre per second. The analogy has limits (water pressure is not exactly voltage), but for junctions it is a good one.

</Analogy>

At node b of the figure, the arrows of $I_1$ and $I_2$ point in and the arrow of $I_3$ points out, so KCL says

$$
I_1 + I_2 - I_3 = 0.
$$

KCL also holds for a whole region. Draw a closed curve round any part of a circuit, a **supernode**, and the net current crossing it is zero, for the same reason. A whole circuit board connected to the rest of the world by two wires carries the same current in one wire as out of the other, whatever is happening inside.

<Narrator who="gideon">

"A node holds no charge" is an assumption, the **lumped-circuit approximation**. It holds when the circuit is much smaller than the wavelength of its signals, so that every part of a wire sees the same current at the same moment. At 50 Hz the wavelength is 6000 km and any circuit you will build is tiny; at 3 GHz it is 10 cm, a circuit board is no longer small, and engineers switch to <LessonLink slug="transmission-lines">transmission lines</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="electromagnetic-waves">Maxwell's equations</LessonLink>.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="kvl" kind="intuition" title="Kirchhoff's voltage law: conservation of energy">

<Definition term="Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)">

Around any closed loop, at every instant, the algebraic sum of the voltages across the branches is zero:

$$
\sum_{k} v_k = 0.
$$

Walk round the loop in one direction; add a branch voltage when you enter at its $+$ terminal (a drop) and subtract it when you enter at its $-$ terminal (a rise).

</Definition>

**Why it is true.** A voltage is a difference in *electric potential*, the energy per coulomb that a charge has at a point. Every node has one potential. Walk round a loop, adding up the differences between successive nodes, and you end where you started, at the same potential, so the differences must sum to zero. In energy terms: a coulomb carried once round the loop gains energy in the sources and gives it up in the resistors, and it returns with exactly the energy it started with. If it came back with more, you could circulate charge forever and extract free energy.

Choose the clockwise direction round the left window of the figure, starting at the bottom of V1. We enter V1 at its $-$ terminal (a rise of 12 V), then R1 at the end where $I_1$ enters (a drop of $R_1 I_1$), then R3 at the top where $I_3$ enters (a drop of $R_3 I_3$):

$$
-12 + R_1 I_1 + R_3 I_3 = 0.
$$

Round the right window, anticlockwise from the bottom of V2: a rise of 5 V through V2, then drops across R2 and R3:

$$
-5 + R_2 I_2 + R_3 I_3 = 0.
$$

Any direction of travel works, as long as you keep it all the way round.

<Note>

Voltages are always *between two points*. "The voltage at node b" is shorthand for the voltage of b relative to a chosen **reference node**, usually called **ground** and given the potential 0 V. The reference is a choice too: change it and every node voltage shifts by the same constant, while every branch voltage, every current and every power stays the same.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="consequences" kind="examples" title="Series, parallel and dividers, derived">

The rules for combining resistors that you may have memorised are not separate laws. They are consequences of KCL, KVL and Ohm's law, and deriving them shows exactly when they apply.

<Theorem name="Series and parallel resistors">

(a) Resistors $R_1, \dots, R_k$ in **series** (connected end to end, so that every node between them joins exactly two of them and nothing else) behave, as seen from the two ends, like one resistor $R_s = R_1 + \cdots + R_k$.

(b) Resistors in **parallel** (all connected between the same two nodes) behave like one resistor $R_p$ with $\dfrac{1}{R_p} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \cdots + \dfrac{1}{R_k}$. Equivalently, conductances $G = 1/R$ add: $G_p = G_1 + \cdots + G_k$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

(a) KCL at each node between two neighbouring resistors, where only those two branches meet, says the same current $i$ flows through every resistor. By Ohm's law, resistor $j$ has voltage $R_j i$. KVL round the loop formed by the chain and the two end nodes gives the total voltage $v = R_1 i + \cdots + R_k i = (R_1 + \cdots + R_k)\,i$, which is Ohm's law for a single resistance $R_s$.

(b) All the resistors connect the same two nodes, so KVL (round the loop through any two of them) says they share one voltage $v$. Resistor $j$ carries $i_j = v/R_j$. KCL at either node gives the total current $i = i_1 + \cdots + i_k = v\,(1/R_1 + \cdots + 1/R_k)$, which is Ohm's law for $1/R_p = \sum_j 1/R_j$.

</Proof>

Two consequences are used every day.

**The voltage divider.** Two resistors in series across a voltage $V_s$ share it in proportion to their resistances. The current is $V_s/(R_a + R_b)$, so the voltage across $R_b$ is

$$
V_\text{out} = V_s \, \frac{R_b}{R_a + R_b}.
$$

**The current divider.** Two resistors in parallel share a current $I$ in *inverse* proportion to their resistances, because they share a voltage $v = I\,(R_1 \parallel R_2)$:

$$
I_1 = I\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}, \qquad I_2 = I\,\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}.
$$

The smaller resistor takes the larger share. A current of 10 mA entering 1 kΩ in parallel with 4 kΩ splits as 8 mA through the 1 kΩ and 2 mA through the 4 kΩ; both have 8 V across them, and KCL checks: $8 + 2 = 10$.

<Example title="A divider under load">

Two 10 kΩ resistors across a 9 V battery give $V_\text{out} = 4.5$ V. Now connect a 10 kΩ load from $V_\text{out}$ to ground. The divider rule assumed that the *same* current flows through $R_a$ and $R_b$; the load breaks that assumption, because some current now leaves the middle node through $R_L$. Combine first: $R_b \parallel R_L = 5$ kΩ. Then the divider rule applies to $R_a$ in series with 5 kΩ:

$$
V_\text{out} = 9 \times \frac{5}{10 + 5} = 3 \text{ V}.
$$

A third low. The lesson: a divider's formula is only as good as the claim that nothing else draws current from its middle. The computation section shows how big the load must be before you can ignore it.

</Example>

<Section id="worked" kind="examples" title="Worked example: two sources, three resistors">

Now the network of the first figure, which no series-parallel trick can simplify, because the two sources sit in different branches. We solve it with the **branch-current method**: one unknown current per essential branch, KCL at the essential nodes, KVL round the meshes.

<Example title="Branch currents of the two-source network">

**Unknowns.** $I_1$ (right through R1), $I_2$ (left through R2), $I_3$ (down through R3), with the arrows of the figure. Working in kΩ and mA keeps the numbers small: a kΩ times a mA is a volt.

**Equations.** From the previous sections:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_1 + I_2 - I_3 &= 0 &&\text{(KCL at b)}\\
2 I_1 + 3 I_3 &= 12 &&\text{(KVL, left mesh)}\\
1 I_2 + 3 I_3 &= 5 &&\text{(KVL, right mesh)}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Solve.** Substitute $I_3 = I_1 + I_2$ into the two loop equations: $5 I_1 + 3 I_2 = 12$ and $3 I_1 + 4 I_2 = 5$. Multiply the first by 4 and the second by 3 and subtract: $11 I_1 = 33$, so $I_1 = 3$ mA. Then $3 I_2 = 12 - 15$, so $I_2 = -1$ mA, and $I_3 = 3 + (-1) = 2$ mA.

**Interpret.** $I_2$ is negative: 1 mA flows from b through R2 *into* the $+$ terminal of V2. The 12 V source is pushing current backwards through the 5 V source, as a charger pushes current into a battery. Node b sits at $R_3 I_3 = 3 \times 2 = 6$ V.

**Check with a power balance.** With the passive convention, each source absorbs $-V I$ for a current arrow leaving its $+$ terminal:

| Element | Absorbed power |
| --- | --- |
| V1 | $-12 \times 3 = -36$ mW (delivers 36 mW) |
| V2 | $-5 \times (-1) = +5$ mW (absorbs 5 mW) |
| R1 | $2 \times 3^2 = 18$ mW |
| R2 | $1 \times (-1)^2 = 1$ mW |
| R3 | $3 \times 2^2 = 12$ mW |
| **Total** | $-36 + 5 + 18 + 1 + 12 = 0$ |

The total is zero, as it must be: every milliwatt V1 delivers is absorbed somewhere. A non-zero total would mean an error, almost always a sign.

</Example>

The power balance is not a separate law. It follows from KCL and KVL alone: for any circuit, whatever its components, $\sum_k v_k i_k = 0$ over all branches (a result called **Tellegen's theorem**). That is what makes it such a good check: it catches mistakes in either law, and it costs one line.

</Section>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Solving the equations with NumPy">

The branch equations are linear, so they form a matrix equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, and the computer can solve them however large the circuit. Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">Linear algebra</TopicLink> explains why a unique solution exists when the equations are independent. Here we write the three equations of the worked example row by row, in SI units, and let `numpy.linalg.solve` do the elimination.

```python run
import numpy as np

# The two-source network: V1 = 12 V, V2 = 5 V, R1 = 2 kΩ, R2 = 1 kΩ, R3 = 3 kΩ.
V1, V2 = 12.0, 5.0
R1, R2, R3 = 2e3, 1e3, 3e3

# Unknowns: branch currents [I1, I2, I3], with the reference directions of the figure.
A = np.array([
    [1.0, 1.0, -1.0],   # KCL at node b:           I1 + I2 - I3 = 0
    [R1,  0.0,  R3],    # KVL, left mesh:    R1 I1 + R3 I3 = V1
    [0.0, R2,   R3],    # KVL, right mesh:   R2 I2 + R3 I3 = V2
])
b = np.array([0.0, V1, V2])
I1, I2, I3 = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
print(f"I1 = {I1*1e3:+.3f} mA   I2 = {I2*1e3:+.3f} mA   I3 = {I3*1e3:+.3f} mA")
print(f"node b sits at {R3*I3:.3f} V")

# Power absorbed by each element (passive sign convention). Each source's current
# arrow leaves its + terminal, so the source absorbs -V*I.
absorbed = {"V1": -V1 * I1, "V2": -V2 * I2, "R1": R1 * I1**2, "R2": R2 * I2**2, "R3": R3 * I3**2}
for name, p in absorbed.items():
    role = "absorbs " if p > 0 else "delivers"
    print(f"{name}: {role} {abs(p)*1e3:6.3f} mW")
print(f"|sum of absorbed powers| = {abs(sum(absorbed.values()))*1e3:.9f} mW")

# An independent check: KVL round the outer loop, V1 -> R1 -> R2 -> V2.
outer = -V1 + R1*I1 - R2*I2 + V2
print(f"outer loop: |-V1 + R1 I1 - R2 I2 + V2| = {abs(outer):.9f} V")
```

The outer loop was not one of the equations we gave the solver, so its coming out zero is a genuine check: it is the sum of the two mesh equations, exactly as the counting argument predicted.

Next, the loaded divider. The script sweeps the load from 1 kΩ to 10 MΩ, combining $R_b$ with the load in parallel and applying the divider rule, and draws a bar for each output voltage.

```python run
# A 9 V battery and two 10 kΩ resistors make a "4.5 V" divider. What happens when we load it?
Vs, Ra, Rb = 9.0, 10e3, 10e3

def parallel(*rs):
    return 1 / sum(1 / r for r in rs)

print(f"unloaded: Vout = {Vs * Rb / (Ra + Rb):.3f} V")
print(f"{'load':>8} {'Vout':>8} {'error':>8}")
for RL in [1e3, 3.3e3, 10e3, 47e3, 100e3, 470e3, 1e6, 10e6]:
    Rlow = parallel(Rb, RL)                  # Rb and the load share the same two nodes
    vout = Vs * Rlow / (Ra + Rlow)           # divider rule with the combined lower resistance
    err = (vout - 4.5) / 4.5 * 100
    bar = "#" * round(vout / 4.5 * 30)
    label = f"{RL/1e3:g} k" if RL < 1e6 else f"{RL/1e6:g} M"
    print(f"{label:>7}Ω {vout:7.3f} V {err:7.2f} %  {bar}")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

Read the table as a rule of thumb: the error is roughly the divider's own resistance (here $R_a \parallel R_b = 5$ kΩ) divided by the load, so a load ten times larger costs about 10 %, and a hundred times larger about 1 %. A typical digital multimeter has an input resistance of 10 MΩ, which is why measuring this divider with one changes its output by only about 0.05 %; measure a divider made of megohm resistors and the meter itself becomes a serious load.

</Narrator>

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="Kirchhoff at the bench and in industry">

**Measuring.** A voltmeter measures the voltage between two nodes, so it is connected *across* an element, in parallel; an ideal voltmeter draws no current, so KCL is undisturbed. An ammeter measures the current in a branch, so the branch must be broken and the meter inserted *in series*; an ideal ammeter has no voltage across it, so KVL is undisturbed. Real meters approach these ideals but do not reach them, as the divider table showed. On a real board engineers rarely break a branch to measure current. They design in a small **current-sense resistor** (often a few milliohms to an ohm) and measure the voltage across it.

**Checking.** KCL and KVL are the first tools of debugging. If the currents you measure into a node do not sum to roughly zero, one of your measurements is wrong or the circuit is not wired as drawn. If a supply's current is larger than the sum of what its loads should draw, some current is going somewhere it should not: a short circuit, a damaged part, a leaky capacitor.

**Budgeting.** Battery-powered products, from hearing aids to soil-moisture sensors in a field, are designed around a **power budget**: a table of every load's current in every mode, summed by KCL into the battery current. A sensor that wakes once a minute may draw tens of milliamps for a few milliseconds and a few microamps the rest of the time; KCL plus a time average tells you whether the battery lasts a week or five years. <LessonLink slug="real-time-and-low-power">Real-time and low-power design</LessonLink> returns to this.

**Simulating.** Every circuit simulator, from SPICE to the tools that check the power wiring of a chip with billions of nodes, is at heart a machine for writing KCL at every node and solving the result. <LessonLink slug="nodal-and-mesh-analysis">Nodal and mesh analysis</LessonLink>, the next lesson, shows how to write those equations systematically, and how SPICE does it.

<Note>

**Trying it safely.** Everything in this lesson can be built with a 9 V battery or a pack of AA cells, a breadboard and resistors of 1 kΩ and up, which keep currents to a few milliamps; a current-limited bench supply set to 20 mA or less is better still. Never connect a battery's terminals directly together: even a small cell can drive amps through a short circuit and get hot. And never experiment with mains electricity; the same laws hold there, but the currents can kill.

</Note>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Current gets used up as it goes round the circuit."** Current is a flow of charge, and charge is conserved: the same current that leaves a battery's $+$ terminal returns to its $-$ terminal (KCL round the whole circuit). What gets used is *energy*: each coulomb arrives at the resistor carrying more energy than it leaves with, and the difference becomes heat.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I got a negative current, so I made a mistake."** A negative result means the current flows opposite to the arrow you drew, nothing more. The arrows were your choice. Change a sign in your *equations* to make the answer positive and you really will have made a mistake.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A source always delivers power."** Our 5 V source absorbed 5 mW, because the 12 V source drove current into its $+$ terminal. Batteries being charged, and motors driven backwards by their load, absorb power. Only the passive sign convention and the arithmetic tell you which way the energy flows.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Resistors in parallel share the current equally."** Only if they are equal. They share the *voltage* equally; the current divides in inverse proportion to resistance, so most of it goes through the smallest resistor.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Two resistors that are next to each other in the drawing are in series."** Series means that the *same current* must flow through both, which needs a node joining just those two branches. In the first figure, R1 and R3 touch at node b but are not in series, because R2 joins b too.

</Misconception>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

Georg Simon Ohm published his law in 1827, from careful measurements with thermocouples as steady sources. Gustav Kirchhoff, studying in Königsberg under Franz Neumann, stated the two laws in 1845 in a paper on how current spreads through a flat conducting plate, and in 1847 he showed how to choose an independent set of equations for any network and solve them. That second paper, written decades before anyone spoke of graph theory, counted independent loops using trees, and contains the first form of what is now called the matrix-tree theorem. Kirchhoff went on to develop spectral analysis of the elements with Robert Bunsen and to state the law of thermal radiation that also bears his name. His circuit laws were later understood as low-frequency consequences of Maxwell's equations of the 1860s, which is why they hold so well in a lumped circuit and break down in an antenna.
