<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="One circuit, every frequency">

A loudspeaker box usually contains two drivers: a large woofer that can move plenty of air but cannot vibrate quickly, and a small tweeter that vibrates quickly but would be destroyed by the large, slow swings of a bass note. Something must send the low frequencies of the music to one and the high frequencies to the other. A sensor connected to an analog-to-digital converter must not let through frequencies above half the sampling rate, or they will reappear in the data disguised as slow signals. A medical amplifier may need to remove the 50 or 60 Hz hum picked up from nearby mains wiring while keeping the heartbeat. A radio must pass one station and reject its neighbours.

Each of these is a **filter**, a circuit designed to treat different frequencies differently. To design one you need to see a circuit's behaviour at *all* frequencies at once. The previous lesson, <LessonLink slug="ac-and-phasors">AC circuits, phasors and impedance</LessonLink>, solved a circuit at one frequency. Do it at every frequency and plot the result the way engineers do, in decibels on logarithmic axes, and you have the **frequency response**: the subject of this lesson.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Transfer functions and decibels">

<Definition term="Transfer function, frequency response">

Drive a linear circuit at its input with a sinusoid of angular frequency $\omega$, and let $V_\text{in}$ and $V_\text{out}$ be the phasors of the input and output. Their ratio

$$
H(j\omega) = \frac{V_\text{out}}{V_\text{in}}
$$

is the **transfer function** evaluated at $j\omega$. As $\omega$ varies, $|H(j\omega)|$ (the **magnitude response**, or gain) says how much each frequency is amplified or attenuated, and $\angle H(j\omega)$ (the **phase response**) how far it is shifted. Together they are the circuit's **frequency response**.

</Definition>

Because $H$ is a ratio of phasors, it is found with impedances alone, often as a voltage divider. A sinusoid at frequency $\omega$ goes in, the same sinusoid comes out multiplied by $|H|$ and shifted by $\angle H$, and by superposition any mixture of frequencies is filtered frequency by frequency.

<Definition term="Decibel">

The **decibel** (dB) measures a ratio on a logarithmic scale. A power ratio $P_2/P_1$ is $10\log_{10}(P_2/P_1)$ dB. Since power is proportional to the square of voltage (across equal resistances), a voltage ratio is $20\log_{10}|V_2/V_1|$ dB, and the **gain in decibels** of a transfer function is $20\log_{10}|H(j\omega)|$.

| Voltage ratio $\lvert H\rvert$ | 10 | 2 | $\sqrt2$ | 1 | $1/\sqrt2$ | $1/2$ | $1/10$ | $1/100$ | $1/1000$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gain (dB) | +20 | +6.02 | +3.01 | 0 | −3.01 | −6.02 | −20 | −40 | −60 |

</Definition>

Decibels turn multiplication into addition: two stages with gains of −6 dB and −20 dB in cascade give −26 dB (provided neither loads the other, a condition we return to below). (Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.analysis.exponential-logarithmic-functions">Exponential and logarithmic functions</TopicLink> reviews the rules of logarithms used here.) A logarithmic frequency axis gives each **decade** (a factor of 10 in frequency) and each **octave** (a factor of 2) the same width, so that 20 Hz to 20 kHz, the range of human hearing, spans three equal decades. A **Bode plot** is the pair of graphs, gain in decibels and phase in degrees, against frequency on a logarithmic axis.

A filter's **passband** is the range of frequencies it passes with little loss; its **stopband** is the range it attenuates. The **cutoff frequency** $f_c$ is conventionally where the gain has fallen 3.01 dB below its passband value. There $|H|$ is $1/\sqrt2$ of its passband value, and the power delivered to a resistive load has halved: so $f_c$ is also called the **half-power** or **−3 dB frequency**. The **roll-off** is how steeply the gain falls in the stopband, in dB per decade or per octave.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why logarithms twice? Because filter responses are built from factors like $1 + j\omega/\omega_c$, and the logarithm turns a product of factors into a sum of terms. On log–log axes each factor is nearly a pair of straight lines, so a complicated response can be sketched by adding straight lines. That is the whole trick of the Bode plot.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="first-order" kind="examples" title="First-order filters">

The simplest low-pass filter is a resistor followed by a capacitor to ground, with the output taken across the capacitor (circuit (a) in the figure below). As a voltage divider of impedances,

$$
H_\text{LP}(j\omega) = \frac{1/(j\omega C)}{R + 1/(j\omega C)} = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC} = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega/\omega_c},
\qquad \omega_c = \frac{1}{RC},\quad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}.
$$

At low frequencies the capacitor is nearly an open circuit and the input passes unchanged; at high frequencies it is nearly a short circuit and the output collapses. Swap the two parts (circuit (b)) and the output is taken across the resistor:

$$
H_\text{HP}(j\omega) = \frac{R}{R + 1/(j\omega C)} = \frac{j\omega/\omega_c}{1 + j\omega/\omega_c},
$$

a **high-pass** filter with the same cutoff. Both are **first-order** filters: each has one energy store, and its transfer function has $\omega$ to the first power in the denominator.

<Theorem name="Bode asymptotes of a first-order filter">

Let $H = 1/(1 + jx)$ with $x = \omega/\omega_c$. Then:

1. For $x \ll 1$ the gain is close to 0 dB; for $x \gg 1$ it is close to $-20\log_{10} x$ dB, a straight line falling **20 dB per decade** (6.02 dB per octave) that meets the 0 dB line at $x = 1$.
2. The gain differs from these two asymptotes by at most 3.01 dB, at $x = 1$, and by 0.97 dB an octave either side ($x = \tfrac12$ or $2$).
3. The phase is $-\arctan x$: 0° at low frequency, −45° at the cutoff, −90° at high frequency. The straight line that falls from 0° at $x = 0.1$ to −90° at $x = 10$ is never more than 5.7° in error.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The gain is $20\log_{10}|H| = -10\log_{10}(1 + x^2)$. For $x \le 1$ the asymptote is 0 dB, so the error is $-10\log_{10}(1 + x^2)$, largest in size at $x = 1$, where it is $-10\log_{10}2 = -3.01$ dB. For $x \ge 1$ the asymptote is $-20\log_{10}x = -10\log_{10}x^2$, and the error is $-10\log_{10}(1 + x^2) + 10\log_{10}x^2 = -10\log_{10}(1 + 1/x^2)$, again largest at $x = 1$. At $x = 2$ or $x = \tfrac12$ the error is $-10\log_{10}1.25 = -0.97$ dB. For the phase, $\arctan x$ is 45° at $x = 1$, like the straight line; at the breakpoints $x = 0.1$ and $x = 10$ the line reads 0° and 90° while $\arctan 0.1 = 5.71°$ and $\arctan 10 = 84.29°$, and between them the error, which a short calculation shows has its other extremes at about $5.3°$, stays below 5.71°; outside them it shrinks towards zero.

</Proof>

The program prints both filters' responses with $f_c = 1$ kHz, compares the low-pass with its asymptotes, and draws a small Bode magnitude plot.

```python run
import numpy as np

fc = 1000.0                                   # corner frequency: R C = 1 / (2 pi fc)

def lowpass(f):  return 1 / (1 + 1j * f / fc)
def highpass(f): return (1j * f / fc) / (1 + 1j * f / fc)
def db(h):       return 20 * np.log10(np.abs(h))
def deg(h):      return np.degrees(np.angle(h))
def clean(x):    return round(float(x), 2) + 0.0         # so that -0.0004 prints as 0.00

print("    f (Hz) | low-pass  dB  asymptote  error   phase | high-pass dB   phase")
for f in (10, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000, 100000):
    lp, hp = lowpass(f), highpass(f)
    asym = 0.0 if f <= fc else -20 * np.log10(f / fc)   # the straight-line approximation
    print(f"{f:10.0f} | {clean(db(lp)):11.2f} {clean(asym):9.2f} {clean(db(lp) - asym):7.2f} {deg(lp):7.1f} |"
          f" {clean(db(hp)):11.2f} {deg(hp):7.1f}")

# ASCII Bode magnitude plot of the low-pass: '*' is the true curve, '.' the asymptotes.
cols_per_decade, decades = 12, 4
f_axis = 10 ** np.linspace(1, 1 + decades, cols_per_decade * decades + 1)   # 10 Hz to 100 kHz
true_db = db(lowpass(f_axis))
asym_db = np.where(f_axis <= fc, 0.0, -20 * np.log10(f_axis / fc))
print()
for level in range(0, -45, -5):
    row = ""
    for t, a in zip(true_db, asym_db):
        on_true, on_asym = abs(t - level) <= 2.5, abs(a - level) <= 2.5
        row += "*" if on_true else ("." if on_asym else " ")
    print(f"{level:4d} dB |{row}")
print("        +" + "-" * len(f_axis))
axis = [" "] * (len(f_axis) + 20)
for k, name in enumerate(["10 Hz", "100 Hz", "1 kHz", "10 kHz", "100 kHz"]):
    pos = 9 + k * cols_per_decade                      # under the first column of each decade
    axis[pos:pos + len(name) + 1] = "^" + name
print("".join(axis).rstrip())
```

Read the table against the theorem: −3.01 dB at 1 kHz, −0.97 dB an octave either side of it, and from 10 kHz on the true curve lies on the −20 dB per decade asymptote to within 0.04 dB. The high-pass column is the low-pass reflected about 1 kHz: −40 dB at 10 Hz, −3.01 dB at the corner, and a phase that *leads* by 90° at low frequencies, falling to 0°.

<Section id="second-order" kind="examples" title="Second-order filters: band-pass and notch">

The series RLC circuit of <LessonLink slug="rlc-circuits">RLC circuits and resonance</LessonLink> gives four filters from one loop, depending on where you take the output. With $u = \omega/\omega_0$ and $Q = \frac1R\sqrt{L/C}$, the shared denominator is $1 - u^2 + ju/Q$, and

$$
H_\text{across C} = \frac{1}{1 - u^2 + ju/Q}, \qquad
H_\text{across R} = \frac{ju/Q}{1 - u^2 + ju/Q}, \qquad
H_\text{across L and C} = \frac{1 - u^2}{1 - u^2 + ju/Q}.
$$

- Across the **capacitor** the circuit is a second-order **low-pass**: flat below $\omega_0$, falling **40 dB per decade** above it, twice as steep as a first-order filter. Near $\omega_0$ the gain peaks at about $Q$ when $Q > 1/\sqrt2$, which is the resonance of the previous lesson. With $Q = 1/\sqrt2$ the response is as flat as possible without a peak, the **Butterworth** response.
- Across the **resistor** it is a **band-pass** filter (circuit (c) below): the gain is exactly 1 (0 dB) at $\omega_0$, where the inductor and capacitor cancel, and falls 20 dB per decade on either side. Its −3 dB bandwidth is $\omega_0/Q$, so Q sets how narrow the band is.
- Across the **inductor and capacitor together** it is a **notch** or band-stop filter (circuit (d) is the same idea with the parts rearranged): at $\omega_0$ the pair has zero impedance and the output is zero, while frequencies well away from $\omega_0$ pass almost untouched.

Since the resistor voltage and the L-plus-C voltage add up to the input, $H_\text{band-pass} + H_\text{notch} = 1$ at every frequency: what one keeps, the other removes.

The program evaluates all three outputs of a series circuit with $L = 10$ mH, $C = 22$ nF and $R = 68\ \Omega$, then finds the band-pass's −3 dB points by bisection.

```python run
import numpy as np

L, C, R = 10e-3, 22e-9, 68.0          # a series RLC driven by v_in
f0 = 1 / (2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(L * C))
Q = np.sqrt(L / C) / R

def parts(f):
    """Phasor voltages across R (band-pass), across L+C (notch) and across C (low-pass)."""
    w = 2 * np.pi * f
    ZR, ZL, ZC = R, 1j * w * L, 1 / (1j * w * C)
    total = ZR + ZL + ZC
    return ZR / total, (ZL + ZC) / total, ZC / total

db = lambda h: 20 * np.log10(np.abs(h))
clean = lambda x: round(float(x), 2) + 0.0      # so that -0.001 prints as 0.00
print(f"f0 = {f0:.1f} Hz, Q = {Q:.2f}, so the bandwidth should be f0/Q = {f0 / Q:.1f} Hz\n")
print(" f / f0   band-pass dB  phase   notch dB   low-pass dB")
for r in (0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0, 1.05, 1.1, 2.0, 10.0):
    bp, notch, lp = parts(r * f0)
    notch_db = f"{clean(db(notch)):10.2f}" if db(notch) > -100 else "    < -100"
    phase = round(float(np.degrees(np.angle(bp))), 1) + 0.0
    print(f"{r:6.2f} {clean(db(bp)):13.2f} {phase:7.1f} {notch_db} {clean(db(lp)):12.2f}")

def crossing(f_lo, f_hi, target=-3.0103):
    """Bisection on a log-frequency axis for where the band-pass magnitude crosses target dB."""
    rising = db(parts(f_lo)[0]) < target
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = np.sqrt(f_lo * f_hi)
        if (db(parts(mid)[0]) < target) == rising:
            f_lo = mid
        else:
            f_hi = mid
    return np.sqrt(f_lo * f_hi)

f1, f2 = crossing(f0 / 10, f0), crossing(f0, f0 * 10)
print(f"\n-3 dB points: {f1:.1f} Hz and {f2:.1f} Hz; bandwidth {f2 - f1:.1f} Hz")
f = np.logspace(2, 6, 1001)
bp, notch, lp = parts(f)
print("band-pass + notch = 1 at every frequency checked:", np.allclose(bp + notch, 1))
```

The band-pass centre is 10 730.2 Hz with Q = 9.91, and the −3 dB points at 10 202.7 Hz and 11 285.0 Hz give a bandwidth of 1082.3 Hz, exactly $f_0/Q$. A decade either side the band-pass is down by 39.84 dB. The notch column mirrors it: deep at $f_0$, −2.94 and −3.15 dB at 5% either side, and essentially 0 dB an octave away. The low-pass column shows the resonant peak of the previous lesson, +19.93 dB at $f_0$ (that is, $Q$), and −39.91 dB a decade above: the 40 dB-per-decade roll-off.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The ideal notch is infinitely deep; a real one is not. The inductor's winding has resistance, and at $f_0$ the notch output is the voltage across that resistance. A 10 mH inductor with 5 Ω of winding resistance in this circuit leaves the notch at $20\log_{10}(5/73) = -23.3$ dB, and 5% component tolerances would move the notch away from the frequency you meant to remove.

</Narrator>

<Section id="design" kind="computation" title="Designing a filter to a specification">

Real design starts from a specification. Suppose a sensor is sampled 10 000 times a second, and we want a first-order low-pass filter in front of the converter with a cutoff of 1.00 kHz, within 5%, at least 12 dB of attenuation at 5 kHz (half the sampling rate), and a resistor between 1 kΩ and 100 kΩ, so that the filter neither draws too much current from the sensor nor is too easily disturbed by what follows it.

The attenuation requirement is easy to check by hand: at 5 kHz, five times the cutoff, a first-order filter gives $10\log_{10}(1 + 5^2) = 14.15$ dB. The hard part is that resistors and capacitors come only in **preferred values**. The international E-series (standardised in IEC 60063) space values evenly on a logarithmic scale: E6 has 6 values per decade (1.0, 1.5, 2.2, 3.3, 4.7, 6.8) and suits capacitors, E24 has 24 and suits 5% and better resistors, and E96 serves 1% parts. The program searches every combination of an E6 capacitor and an E24 resistor.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Specification: first-order RC low-pass, cutoff 1.00 kHz within 5%, at least 12 dB of
# attenuation at 5 kHz (half the 10 kHz sampling rate), R between 1 kOhm and 100 kOhm.
E24 = [1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.5, 1.6, 1.8, 2.0, 2.2, 2.4, 2.7, 3.0,
       3.3, 3.6, 3.9, 4.3, 4.7, 5.1, 5.6, 6.2, 6.8, 7.5, 8.2, 9.1]   # resistors
E6 = [1.0, 1.5, 2.2, 3.3, 4.7, 6.8]                                   # capacitors

def values(series, lo, hi):
    out = [m * 10.0**k for k in range(-12, 7) for m in series]
    return [v for v in out if lo <= v * (1 + 1e-9) and v <= hi * (1 + 1e-9)]

target = 1000.0
designs = []
for C in values(E6, 1e-9, 1e-6):
    for R in values(E24, 1e3, 100e3):
        fc = 1 / (2 * np.pi * R * C)
        designs.append((abs(fc / target - 1), R, C, fc))
designs.sort(key=lambda d: (round(d[0], 9), d[1]))   # smallest error; ties go to the smaller R

print("   R          C          fc (Hz)   error   loss at 5 kHz")
for err, R, C, fc in designs[:6]:
    loss = 10 * np.log10(1 + (5000 / fc) ** 2)
    print(f"{R / 1e3:5.1f} kOhm  {C * 1e9:6.1f} nF  {fc:9.1f}  {100 * err:5.2f} %  {loss:6.2f} dB")

# Tolerances move the cutoff: check the worst corners for two grades of capacitor.
err, R, C, fc = designs[0]
print(f"\nchosen: {R / 1e3:.1f} kOhm with {C * 1e9:.0f} nF -> {fc:.1f} Hz")
for r_tol, c_tol in ((0.01, 0.05), (0.01, 0.02)):
    corners = [1 / (2 * np.pi * R * (1 + dr) * C * (1 + dc))
               for dr in (-r_tol, r_tol) for dc in (-c_tol, c_tol)]
    ok = 950 <= min(corners) and max(corners) <= 1050
    print(f"R {r_tol:.0%}, C {c_tol:.0%}: cutoff from {min(corners):.0f} Hz to {max(corners):.0f} Hz"
          f" -> {'meets' if ok else 'fails'} the 5% specification")
```

Two designs tie for best, 1.6 kΩ with 100 nF and 16 kΩ with 10 nF, both giving 994.7 Hz (0.53% low) and 14.19 dB at 5 kHz. They differ in **impedance level**: the first draws ten times more current from the source; the second is ten times more easily loaded by what follows. In front of many converters the larger capacitor is preferred, because it also acts as a small charge reservoir for the converter's sampling capacitor.

The tolerance check is the real lesson. With a 1% resistor and an ordinary 5% capacitor, the cutoff could lie anywhere from 938 Hz to 1058 Hz, which fails a ±5% specification even though the nominal design is only 0.53% off. A 2% capacitor (938 becomes 966, 1058 becomes 1025) passes.

<Narrator who="margaret">

A quick estimate gets the same answer without a search. Since $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$, a 1% error in $R$ or in $C$ moves $f_c$ by about 1% the other way, so small tolerances simply add: 1% plus 5% is about 6%, and $994.7 \times (1 - 0.06) \approx 935$ Hz is close to the exact 938 Hz. With a 2% capacitor the sum is 3%, comfortably inside the 5% budget.

</Narrator>

<Narrator who="gideon">

The printed tolerance is not the whole story for a capacitor. Ceramic capacitors with the stable C0G (also called NP0) dielectric drift by only tens of parts per million per degree, but the smaller X7R and similar types can lose a large fraction of their capacitance with temperature, age and DC bias, well beyond their printed tolerance. For a filter whose cutoff matters, use C0G or film capacitors, and read the manufacturer's curves, not just the label.

</Narrator>

```python run
import numpy as np

R, C = 1.6e3, 100e-9                      # the low-pass designed above: fc = 994.7 Hz

def par(a, b):                            # two impedances in parallel
    return a * b / (a + b)

def rc_loaded(f, R, C, R_load=np.inf):
    Zc = 1 / (2j * np.pi * f * C)
    Zp = Zc if np.isinf(R_load) else par(Zc, R_load)
    return Zp / (R + Zp)

def two_sections(f, R1, C1, R2, C2, buffered):
    """Two RC low-passes in cascade, with or without an ideal buffer between them."""
    if buffered:
        return rc_loaded(f, R1, C1) * rc_loaded(f, R2, C2)
    Zc1, Zc2 = 1 / (2j * np.pi * f * C1), 1 / (2j * np.pi * f * C2)
    Zp = par(Zc1, R2 + Zc2)               # the second section loads the first
    return Zp / (R1 + Zp) * Zc2 / (R2 + Zc2)

def cutoff(H, lo=1.0, hi=1e6):
    """Where |H| is 3.01 dB below its low-frequency value (bisection on a log axis)."""
    ref = abs(H(0.01))
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = np.sqrt(lo * hi)
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if abs(H(mid)) > ref / np.sqrt(2) else (lo, mid)
    return lo

print("load on the filter     DC gain    cutoff")
for R_load in (np.inf, 100e3, 10e3, 1.6e3):
    H = lambda f: rc_loaded(f, R, C, R_load)
    name = "none" if np.isinf(R_load) else f"{R_load / 1e3:g} kOhm"
    gain_db = round(20 * np.log10(abs(H(0.01))), 2) + 0.0   # + 0.0 turns -0.0 into 0.0
    print(f"{name:18} {gain_db:8.2f} dB {cutoff(H):8.1f} Hz")

print("\ntwo sections in cascade                 cutoff   at 5 kHz")
cases = [("buffered, both 1.6 kOhm / 100 nF", (R, C, R, C, True)),
         ("unbuffered, both 1.6 kOhm / 100 nF", (R, C, R, C, False)),
         ("unbuffered, second 16 kOhm / 10 nF", (R, C, 10 * R, C / 10, False))]
for name, args in cases:
    H = lambda f: two_sections(f, *args)
    print(f"{name:38} {cutoff(H):6.1f} Hz {20 * np.log10(abs(H(5000))):7.2f} dB")
```

A 100 kΩ load barely matters (−0.14 dB, cutoff moved 1.6%). A 10 kΩ load costs 1.29 dB of passband gain and pushes the cutoff from 994.7 Hz to 1153.9 Hz. A load equal to $R$ halves the gain and doubles the cutoff. Cascading is worse: two identical sections, wired directly, have a cutoff of 372.3 Hz, not the 640.2 Hz that two independent sections would give, because the second section's input impedance is part of the first section's load.

There are two remedies. The first is **impedance scaling**: make each stage's impedance much higher than the one before (here, 16 kΩ and 10 nF for the second section), so that the loading is small; the cutoff comes back to 597.6 Hz. The second, better one is a **buffer** between stages, an amplifier with a very high input impedance and a very low output impedance, so that each stage sees exactly the load it was designed for. A voltage-follower op-amp does this, and once an amplifier is in the circuit it can do more: **active filters** such as the Sallen–Key and multiple-feedback circuits produce second-order responses, with any Q you choose, from resistors and capacitors alone, avoiding inductors, which are bulky, lossy and pick up magnetic interference at audio frequencies. (A passive 50 Hz notch with a 10 µF capacitor would need an inductor of about 1 H.) <LessonLink slug="operational-amplifiers">Operational amplifiers</LessonLink> builds them.

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="Filters in practice">

**Loudspeaker crossovers.** A two-way speaker sends the output of a low-pass filter to the woofer and of a high-pass filter to the tweeter, with the same crossover frequency, typically somewhere between 1.5 and 3 kHz. The first-order pair has a lovely property: $\frac{1}{1 + jx} + \frac{jx}{1 + jx} = 1$ exactly, so the two acoustic outputs add back to the original signal (if the drivers were ideal and in the same place). But first-order slopes of 20 dB per decade send a lot of energy to each driver outside its range, so most designs use steeper filters. A widely used choice is the fourth-order **Linkwitz–Riley** crossover, described by Siegfried Linkwitz in 1976, built from two cascaded second-order Butterworth sections per output, whose outputs are −6 dB each at the crossover and sum to a flat magnitude. Passive crossovers between an amplifier and the drivers must also cope with the fact that a loudspeaker's impedance is far from a constant resistance, which is why many modern designs filter at line level, actively or digitally, with one amplifier per driver.

**Anti-aliasing.** A converter that samples at rate $f_s$ cannot tell a frequency $f$ from $f_s - f$ (<LessonLink slug="sampling-and-the-fft">Sampling and the FFT</LessonLink> explains why), so anything above $f_s/2$ must be removed *before* sampling. A first-order filter is rarely enough: our design leaves a 5 kHz interferer only 14 dB weaker, while a 12-bit converter resolves about 72 dB. Early CD players, sampling at 44.1 kHz for audio up to 20 kHz, needed steep, high-order analog filters squeezed into that narrow gap. Most modern audio and many measurement converters avoid the problem by **oversampling**: a sigma-delta converter samples far above the Nyquist rate, so a simple RC filter suffices in front of it, and a sharp digital filter (<LessonLink slug="digital-filters">Digital filters</LessonLink>) does the rest after the conversion.

**Tools and families.** Engineers rarely derive high-order filters by hand. Standard families trade one property for another: **Butterworth** (maximally flat passband), **Chebyshev** (steeper roll-off at the price of ripple), **Bessel** (nearly constant time delay, so pulses keep their shape) and **elliptic** or Cauer (the steepest, with ripple in both bands). Design tables and free online tools from converter and amplifier manufacturers produce component values; SPICE checks the design with real component models; and on the bench, a **network analyser** or an audio analyser measures the actual frequency response. The first LC filters were invented around 1915, independently by George Campbell at AT&T and Karl Willy Wagner in Germany, for carrying several telephone conversations on one line; Stephen Butterworth described his maximally flat response in 1930; and Hendrik Bode, at Bell Labs, made the straight-line plots that bear his name standard in the 1930s and 1940s, while designing the feedback amplifiers that <LessonLink slug="feedback-control">Feedback control</LessonLink> returns to.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A low-pass filter blocks everything above its cutoff."** At the cutoff the gain is only 3 dB down, and a first-order filter is just 20 dB down a whole decade higher. A frequency twice the cutoff still passes at 45% of its amplitude (−7 dB). Steep rejection needs higher-order filters.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Decibels of a voltage ratio are $10\log_{10}$."** That is the formula for power ratios. For voltage or current ratios use $20\log_{10}$, so that a voltage ratio and the corresponding power ratio give the same number of decibels.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Two identical RC filters in a row give the same cutoff with twice the slope."** Even with an ideal buffer between them, the cascade is already 6.02 dB down at the original cutoff, and its −3 dB point falls to 0.644 of it. Without a buffer, the second stage loads the first and the cutoff falls further, to 0.374 of the single-stage value in our example.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A passive filter can never make a voltage bigger."** It cannot deliver more *power* than it receives, but a resonant RLC filter can raise a voltage: the second-order low-pass above has a gain of +19.93 dB (about 10 times) at its resonance. Q times the input voltage across a capacitor is a real voltage, as the RLC lesson warned.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The Bode asymptotes are the response."** They are a sketch. For a first-order filter they are wrong by 3 dB at the corner. For a second-order filter with high Q they miss the resonant peak entirely: the asymptotes meet at 0 dB at $\omega_0$, while the true gain there is $20\log_{10}Q$.

</Misconception>
