<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Clean rails from a messy source">

Every circuit in the previous lessons quietly assumed a perfect supply: a fixed voltage that never sags, never wobbles and never carries interference. Real sources are nothing like that. A lithium-ion cell falls from 4.2 V to about 3.0 V as it discharges. A USB-C charger negotiates 5, 9, 15 or 20 V, and up to 48 V under the extended-power rules of USB Power Delivery. A car's 12 V system swings from below 9 V while the engine cranks to well above 14 V while charging. And the circuits want something else entirely: a processor core at well under 1 V, accurate to a few per cent while its current jumps by amperes in nanoseconds; memory at 1.1 V; input and output pins at 1.8 or 3.3 V; a quiet 3.3 V for an audio converter. A phone has dozens of such **rails**.

Turning messy sources into clean rails is the job of **power supplies** and **regulators**. Then, even with perfect rails, every signal carries **noise**, random fluctuations that come from the physics of charge itself and set the smallest signal you can ever measure. This lesson covers both, because both decide whether a design works when it leaves the simulator.

<Note>

Safe experiments use batteries, USB power banks and current-limited bench supplies at low voltage. Converting mains electricity is done inside enclosed adapters that are designed, tested and certified to safety standards by their manufacturers. Never open or modify a mains adapter or charger: its capacitors can stay dangerously charged after it is unplugged.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="linear" kind="definitions" title="Linear regulators and LDOs">

In <LessonLink slug="semiconductors-and-diodes">Semiconductors and diodes</LessonLink> a Zener diode held a rail steady by wasting current in parallel with the load. A **series** regulator does better: it puts a transistor, the **pass element**, between the input and the output, and controls it with a feedback loop so that it drops exactly the excess voltage.

The circuit is the non-inverting amplifier of <LessonLink slug="operational-amplifiers">Operational amplifiers</LessonLink> with a power transistor added at its output: the loop forces the divided output to equal the reference, so $V_{out} = V_{ref}(1 + R_1/R_2)$. The reference is usually a **bandgap reference**, about 1.2 V, which combines a junction voltage that falls with temperature (about −2 mV/K) with a thermal-voltage term that rises with it, so that the sum is nearly constant; Bob Widlar and Paul Brokaw devised the classic versions in the early 1970s.

<Definition term="Dropout, LDO, efficiency">

The **dropout voltage** is the smallest $V_{in} - V_{out}$ at which the regulator still regulates. Classic regulators with an npn pass transistor need about 1.5–2.5 V. A **low-dropout regulator** (LDO), with a p-channel MOSFET (or pnp) pass element that can be driven fully on, needs only about 0.1–0.3 V. Because the same current flows in and out (plus a small **quiescent current** $I_q$), a linear regulator's efficiency can never exceed $V_{out}/V_{in}$:
$$\eta = \frac{V_{out} I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out} + I_q)}, \qquad P_{\text{heat}} = (V_{in} - V_{out})I_{out} + V_{in}I_q.$$

</Definition>

The heat has to go somewhere. A package's **thermal resistance** from junction to ambient, $\theta_{JA}$ in °C/W, gives the junction temperature $T_J = T_A + P\,\theta_{JA}$, which must stay below the rated maximum, often 125 °C or 150 °C.

```python run
# Linear regulators: where the power goes, and how hot the regulator gets.
T_AMBIENT = 40.0        # degrees C inside an enclosure on a warm day
T_MAX = 125.0           # a typical maximum junction temperature

cases = [  # (description, V_in, V_out, I_out in A, quiescent current in A, theta_JA in C/W)
    ("12 V -> 5 V, 0.5 A, TO-220, no heatsink", 12.0, 5.0, 0.5, 5e-3, 50.0),
    ("12 V -> 5 V, 0.5 A, TO-220 on a heatsink", 12.0, 5.0, 0.5, 5e-3, 12.0),
    ("5 V -> 3.3 V, 0.3 A, SOT-223 LDO", 5.0, 3.3, 0.3, 1e-3, 60.0),
    ("3.7 V cell -> 3.3 V, 0.2 A, small LDO", 3.7, 3.3, 0.2, 50e-6, 150.0),
]
print(f"{'case':42} {'efficiency':>10} {'heat (W)':>9} {'T_junction':>11}")
for name, vin, vout, iout, iq, theta in cases:
    p_in = vin * (iout + iq)
    p_out = vout * iout
    heat = p_in - p_out
    tj = T_AMBIENT + heat * theta
    flag = "  too hot!" if tj > T_MAX else ""
    print(f"{name:42} {p_out / p_in:10.1%} {heat:9.3f} {tj:9.0f} C{flag}")
```

Dropping 7 V at half an ampere turns 3.56 W into heat, and a bare TO-220 package would reach 218 °C: it would shut itself down, if it has thermal protection, or fail. A heatsink brings it to 83 °C, but at 41.3% efficiency. The same physics favours linear regulators where the voltage drop is small: from a lithium cell to 3.3 V, an LDO is 89.2% efficient and barely warm. Linear regulators are prized because they are simple, cheap, small and *quiet*: their feedback loop actively rejects ripple and noise on the input, a property called **power-supply rejection ratio** (PSRR), typically 60–80 dB at low frequencies, falling at high frequencies as the loop gain falls.

<Narrator who="gideon">

An LDO is a feedback loop driving a capacitive load, the very situation the <LessonLink slug="feedback-and-oscillators">previous lesson</LessonLink> warned about. Many LDOs are stable only with an output capacitor in a specified range of capacitance *and* equivalent series resistance (ESR), and some older designs oscillate with a ceramic capacitor whose ESR is too low. Read that section of the datasheet before choosing the capacitor.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="switching" kind="computation" title="Switch-mode supplies: the buck converter">

A linear regulator wastes the voltage difference as heat because its pass transistor works as a variable resistor. A **switch-mode** supply instead uses transistors as switches, fully on or fully off, which in the <LessonLink slug="transistors">Transistors</LessonLink> lesson was the low-loss way to use them, and smooths the result with an inductor and a capacitor, which store energy but ideally dissipate none.

The **buck converter** steps voltage down. The switch node spends a fraction $D$, the **duty cycle**, of each period connected to $V_{in}$ and the rest connected to ground. In steady state the inductor's average voltage must be zero (otherwise its current would grow without limit), so the output equals the switch node's average:
$$V_{out} = D\,V_{in}.$$
Power, not current, is conserved: an ideal buck from 12 V to 5 V at 1 A draws only about 0.42 A from its input. While the high-side switch is on, the inductor has $V_{in} - V_{out}$ across it and its current rises; while it is off, it has $-V_{out}$ and the current falls. The peak-to-peak **inductor ripple** and the resulting **output ripple** (for an ideal capacitor) are
$$\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{in} - V_{out})\,D}{L f_s}, \qquad \Delta V_{out} \approx \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 f_s C}.$$
Raising the switching frequency $f_s$ shrinks the inductor and capacitor needed, which is why modern converters switch at hundreds of kilohertz to several megahertz. The program simulates an ideal buck, cycle by cycle, from rest.

```python run
# An ideal synchronous buck converter, simulated switching cycle by switching cycle.
VIN, FS = 12.0, 500e3          # input voltage and switching frequency
L, C, R_LOAD = 10e-6, 22e-6, 5.0
D = 5.0 / 12.0                 # duty cycle chosen for 5 V out
STEPS = 240                    # time steps per switching period (D x STEPS is a whole number)
dt = 1 / (FS * STEPS)

i_l, v_c = 0.0, 0.0            # inductor current and capacitor voltage, starting from rest
history = []
for n in range(int(3e-3 * FS) * STEPS):          # 3 ms of operation
    on = (n % STEPS) < D * STEPS                 # high-side switch on for the first D of each period
    v_sw = VIN if on else 0.0                    # the switch node: input voltage, or ground
    i_l += (v_sw - v_c) / L * dt                 # inductor: L di/dt = v_sw - v_out
    v_c += (i_l - v_c / R_LOAD) / C * dt         # capacitor: C dv/dt = i_L - i_load
    history.append((i_l, v_c))

last = history[-20 * STEPS:]                     # the last 20 periods, in steady state
i_vals, v_vals = [h[0] for h in last], [h[1] for h in last]
v_avg = sum(v_vals) / len(v_vals)
print(f"output {v_avg:.3f} V (D x VIN = {D * VIN:.3f} V), load current {v_avg / R_LOAD:.3f} A")
print(f"inductor ripple {max(i_vals) - min(i_vals):.3f} A peak to peak, "
      f"formula (VIN - VOUT) D / (L fs) = {(VIN - 5.0) * D / (L * FS):.3f} A")
print(f"output ripple {(max(v_vals) - min(v_vals)) * 1e3:.2f} mV peak to peak, "
      f"formula dI / (8 fs C) = {(VIN - 5.0) * D / (L * FS) / (8 * FS * C) * 1e3:.2f} mV")
peak = max(h[1] for h in history)
print(f"start-up: the output overshoots to {peak:.2f} V before settling (no control loop)")
```

The simulation settles at 5.000 V with 0.584 A of inductor ripple and 6.64 mV of output ripple, matching the formulas (0.583 A and 6.63 mV). Real converters lose a few per cent in the switches' on-resistance, in charging and discharging their gate and node capacitances every cycle, and in the inductor, so practical efficiencies are about 85–95%, against 41.7% for a linear regulator doing the same job. Higher-voltage outputs come from the **boost** converter (the same parts rearranged), and isolated supplies such as phone chargers use a transformer in a **flyback** or similar topology; <LessonLink slug="power-electronics">Power electronics</LessonLink> goes deeper.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Look at the last line: started with no control, the output overshoots to 9.05 V, because the LC filter is a lightly damped resonant circuit. Real converters close a feedback loop around the duty cycle, with all the stability questions of the previous lesson, and ramp up gently with a **soft-start** circuit. Without them, the first power-up would damage the 5 V circuitry it was meant to feed.

</Narrator>

The price of switching is noise. The switch node slews by the whole input voltage in nanoseconds, and the input current arrives in sharp-edged pulses, so a switcher radiates and conducts interference at its switching frequency and harmonics up to hundreds of megahertz. Products must meet electromagnetic-compatibility limits (in the international CISPR standards, the European CE regime and the United States FCC rules). A common arrangement uses both kinds: a switcher for efficiency, followed by an LDO for a quiet rail where an analog circuit needs it.

</Section>

<Section id="decoupling" kind="computation" title="Ripple and decoupling">

A regulator can hold its own output steady, but it cannot hold steady the supply pin of a chip a few centimetres away. When a processor's current jumps by an ampere in a nanosecond, even 1 nH of wiring between them develops $L\,di/dt = 10^{-9} \times 10^9 = 1$ V. So every chip has **decoupling** (or **bypass**) capacitors right beside its supply pins, a local reservoir that supplies fast current changes until the regulator catches up. Engineers state the requirement as a **target impedance**: if a 0.8 V core rail may move by 3% (24 mV) when its current steps by 20 A, the supply network must look like at most $24\ \text{mV}/20\ \text{A} = 1.2\ \text{m}\Omega$ at every frequency the step contains.

A real capacitor is not just a capacitance. It has an **equivalent series resistance** (ESR) and an **equivalent series inductance** (ESL) from its electrodes, terminations and mounting. Above its **self-resonant frequency** $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ it behaves as an inductor. The traditional habit is to place a large capacitor and a small one together. The netlist computes the impedance of a 10 µF capacitor alone and with a 100 nF capacitor beside it, using plausible ESR and ESL values for small ceramic capacitors with their mounting; a 1 A AC current source makes the node voltage equal to the impedance.

The 10 µF capacitor reaches its ESR, 5.0 mΩ, at its self-resonance near 1.6 MHz, and above that its impedance rises like an inductor's. The 100 nF capacitor adds a second dip, 19.4 mΩ at 19.3 MHz, and helps above about 20 MHz, but between the two dips the first capacitor's inductance resonates with the second's capacitance in **parallel**, an **anti-resonance** that peaks at 0.240 Ω at 12.2 MHz. At 10 MHz the pair is *worse* than the single capacitor. Folk rules such as "always add a 100 nF" can backfire; power-integrity engineers simulate the whole network, including the board's planes and the chip's package, and choose values, counts and placements to keep the impedance under the target everywhere. What helps reliably is reducing inductance: small packages, capacitors right at the pins, and short, wide connections with several vias into solid planes (<LessonLink slug="printed-circuit-boards">Printed circuit boards</LessonLink>).

<Section id="noise" kind="definitions" title="Noise: the floor under every measurement">

Remove every interference source and a signal is still not perfectly clean, because current is carried by discrete charges in random thermal motion. Three kinds of **intrinsic noise** matter everywhere.

<Definition term="Thermal, shot and flicker noise">

- **Thermal (Johnson–Nyquist) noise.** Every resistance $R$ at temperature $T$ produces a random open-circuit voltage with root-mean-square value
$$v_n = \sqrt{4kTRB}$$
over a bandwidth $B$, independent of any current flowing. Its **spectral density**, $\sqrt{4kTR}$ in volts per root hertz, is flat (white) up to frequencies far beyond electronics.
- **Shot noise.** A current $I$ that crosses a barrier, such as a p–n junction, as independent charges $q$ fluctuates with rms value $i_n = \sqrt{2qIB}$.
- **Flicker (1/f) noise.** Below a **corner frequency**, from hertz to tens of kilohertz depending on the device, many components add noise whose power density rises as $1/f$. MOSFETs, whose channel carriers are trapped and released at the oxide surface, have much more of it than BJTs.

</Definition>

John B. Johnson measured thermal noise at Bell Labs in 1927–28, and his colleague Harry Nyquist derived the formula from thermodynamics in a paper printed right after Johnson's; Walter Schottky had described shot noise in 1918.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why must a resistor be noisy? A resistor absorbs energy from any circuit connected to it, turning it into heat. In thermal equilibrium, energy must flow back as fast as it flows in, or the resistor would cool its surroundings for free; the noise voltage is that return flow. This **fluctuation–dissipation** link is why anything that dissipates also fluctuates, and why the formula contains only $k$, $T$ and $R$.

</Narrator>

Noise sources that are **uncorrelated** add as powers, so their rms values add as a root-sum-square, not linearly. Averaging $N$ independent readings of a steady signal reduces the noise by $\sqrt N$. The quality of a signal is measured by the **signal-to-noise ratio**, usually in decibels: $\text{SNR} = 20\log_{10}(V_{signal}/V_{noise})$ for rms voltages.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

k, q, T = 1.380649e-23, 1.602176634e-19, 300.0

print("Thermal (Johnson-Nyquist) noise, v = sqrt(4 k T R B)")
for r in (50, 1e3, 10e3, 1e6):
    density = math.sqrt(4 * k * T * r)                     # V per root hertz
    print(f"  {r:>9,.0f} ohm: {density * 1e9:7.2f} nV/rtHz, "
          f"{density * math.sqrt(20e3) * 1e6:7.3f} uV rms over 20 kHz")

i_dc = 1e-3
print(f"\nShot noise of 1 mA, i = sqrt(2 q I B): {math.sqrt(2 * q * i_dc) * 1e12:.1f} pA/rtHz")
print(f"kT/C noise on a 1 pF sampling capacitor: {math.sqrt(k * T / 1e-12) * 1e6:.1f} uV rms")

# Uncorrelated noise adds as power (root-sum-square), and averaging N readings
# cuts it by sqrt(N). Check both with seeded random numbers.
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
a = rng.normal(0.0, 3.0, 1_000_000)        # 3 uV rms
b = rng.normal(0.0, 4.0, 1_000_000)        # 4 uV rms, independent of a
print(f"\nrms of a + b: {np.std(a + b):.3f} uV (sqrt(3^2 + 4^2) = 5, not 3 + 4 = 7)")
signal = 1.0                                # a 1 uV DC level buried in the noise of a
for n in (1, 100, 10_000):
    means = signal + a[: (len(a) // n) * n].reshape(-1, n).mean(axis=1)
    snr_db = 20 * math.log10(signal / np.std(means))
    print(f"average of {n:>6} readings: noise {np.std(means):.4f} uV, SNR {snr_db:+.1f} dB")
```

A 1 kΩ resistor at 300 K produces 4.07 nV/√Hz, or 0.576 µV rms across the 20 kHz audio band; a 1 MΩ resistor produces 128.7 nV/√Hz. That is why low-noise amplifiers use low resistances at their inputs, and why a microphone preamplifier's noise is quoted against the equivalent resistor. Sampling a voltage onto a capacitor freezes a noise of $\sqrt{kT/C}$, 64.4 µV for 1 pF whatever resistance charged it, a floor that sets the size of capacitors in analog-to-digital converters (<LessonLink slug="sensors-and-data-conversion">Sensors and data conversion</LessonLink>). And the simulation confirms the two rules: 3 µV and 4 µV of independent noise make 4.99 µV, not 7, and a 1 µV signal 9.5 dB below a single reading's noise rises to +30.5 dB after averaging 10 000 readings, 40 dB of improvement for a factor of $10^4$ in readings.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Put numbers on a real chain. A microphone delivers 10 mV rms; the first amplifier's input noise is 5 nV/√Hz, and its 1 kΩ source adds 4.07 nV/√Hz, together $\sqrt{5^2 + 4.07^2} = 6.45$ nV/√Hz, or 0.91 µV over 20 kHz. That gives an SNR of $20\log_{10}(10\ \text{mV}/0.91\ \mu\text{V}) \approx 81$ dB, decided almost entirely in the first stage. A 16-bit converter's ideal SNR is 98 dB, so here the microphone's own electronics, not the converter, set the limit.

</Narrator>

<Section id="grounding" kind="human" title="Grounding and layout habits">

"Ground" is a conductor, with resistance and inductance like any other, and current returning through it develops voltage. The most common source of mysterious errors is **common-impedance coupling**: two circuits sharing a stretch of return path. If a motor's 2 A returns through the same 10 mΩ of copper as a temperature sensor's signal, the sensor's ground moves by 20 mV every time the motor starts; at 10 mV per °C, that is a 2 °C error that appears and disappears with the motor. Habits that prevent such problems:

- **Give high currents their own return paths**, joined to the sensitive circuits' ground at a single point (a **star** ground) near the supply, so that large currents never flow through the reference of a small signal.
- **Use a solid ground plane** on a circuit board. At high frequencies return current flows in the plane directly under its signal trace, the path of least inductance, so an unbroken plane keeps loops small; a slot cut under a fast trace forces the return current around it and turns the loop into an antenna.
- **Keep fast, high-current loops tiny.** In a buck converter the "hot loop" formed by the input capacitor and the two switches carries current that switches on and off in nanoseconds; its area sets how much the converter radiates. Layout guides from every converter manufacturer begin with it.
- **Place decoupling capacitors at the pins**, with the shortest possible connection to the plane, as the impedance simulation showed.
- **Separate noisy from quiet**: switchers, clocks and digital buses on one side of the board, analog inputs and references on the other, and never route a sensitive trace across a switching node.
- **Avoid ground loops** between instruments. Two pieces of equipment each connected to earth and joined by a signal cable form a loop that picks up mains-frequency hum; balanced (differential) connections, which the instrumentation amplifier was built for, reject it.

These habits continue in <LessonLink slug="printed-circuit-boards">Printed circuit boards</LessonLink>, where layout becomes a discipline of its own.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Power in professional practice">

A phone or laptop board carries a **power-management IC** (PMIC) that integrates many buck converters, LDOs, battery charging and sequencing, so that rails come up in the order the processor requires. Servers and AI accelerators take this furthest: in many data centres 48 V is distributed to each board and converted down to below 1 V at hundreds of amperes right beside the processor, a problem <LessonLink slug="powering-data-centres">Powering data centres</LessonLink> describes. Designers start from manufacturers' online design tools and reference designs, simulate the control loop and the power distribution network, and measure ripple, transient response, efficiency and emissions on the bench. Anything connected to the mains is designed to international safety standards (from the International Electrotechnical Commission and its national counterparts) and tested by certified laboratories before it may be sold, which is why mains supplies are bought as finished, certified products rather than built at a workbench.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A linear regulator is efficient if it uses a good LDO."** No regulator that passes the same current in and out can beat $V_{out}/V_{in}$. An LDO only allows a smaller difference; from 12 V to 3.3 V it is at most 27.5% efficient, however good it is.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A switching converter is just a more efficient linear regulator."** It is a different machine, with its own costs: output ripple at the switching frequency, electromagnetic interference, a control loop to stabilise, and poorer behaviour at very light loads unless it changes mode. Quiet analog rails often still need an LDO after it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Noise voltages add up."** Independent noise sources add in power: 3 µV and 4 µV of uncorrelated noise make 5 µV. Only correlated signals, such as interference from a common source, add linearly.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Ground is zero volts everywhere."** Ground is a conductor. Any current flowing in it creates voltage differences between its points, which is why where currents return matters as much as where they go.

</Misconception>

</Section>
