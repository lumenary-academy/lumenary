<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why switch?">

A laptop charger takes 230 V or 120 V AC from the wall and delivers, say, 20 V DC over a USB-C cable. Inside the laptop, other converters turn 20 V into the 1 V or so that the processor's cores need, at tens of amperes. An electric car turns 400 V or 800 V DC from its battery into three-phase AC of variable frequency for its motor, and a solar farm turns the DC of its panels into AC synchronised to the grid. Every one of these conversions is done by **power electronics**, and almost all of them at efficiencies of 90 to 99%.

The obvious way to drop a voltage is a **linear regulator**: a transistor in series with the load, partly on, acting as a variable resistor. It is simple and quiet, but it burns the difference. Dropping 12 V to 1 V at 50 A this way would deliver 50 W to the processor and waste $(12 - 1) \times 50 = 550$ W in the transistor: an efficiency of 8%.

The trick of power electronics is to use transistors only as **switches**, fully on or fully off. An ideal switch dissipates nothing in either state: when it is on the voltage across it is zero, and when it is off the current through it is zero, so $vi = 0$ always. The switches chop the input into pulses, and inductors and capacitors, which store energy without dissipating it, smooth the pulses into the output we want. All the losses come from the ways real switches, inductors and capacitors fall short of ideal, and engineering them down is most of the craft.

<Note>

**Safety.** Mains-powered converters, chargers and power supplies contain capacitors charged to around 400 V that can stay charged long after the plug is pulled, and EV and solar converters work at hundreds of volts DC, which is especially dangerous. Never open a charger, a power supply or anything plugged into the mains. To experiment, use simulation, as in this lesson, or a low-voltage converter kit powered from a current-limited bench supply or a small battery pack at a few volts.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="switches" kind="definitions" title="The switches">

Power converters are built from a handful of semiconductor switches. You met their small-signal cousins in <LessonLink slug="semiconductors-and-diodes">Semiconductors and diodes</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="transistors">Transistors</LessonLink>; here what matters is how much voltage they block when off, how much they dissipate when on, and how fast they switch.

<Definition term="Power semiconductor switches">

- A **power diode** conducts in one direction only, with a forward drop of 0.3 to 1 V or more, and turns on and off by itself as the circuit's voltages change. **Schottky** diodes have low drops and switch fast.
- A **power MOSFET** is a voltage-controlled switch that behaves, when on, like a small resistance $R_{\text{DS(on)}}$, from about a milliohm in low-voltage parts upward. It switches in nanoseconds. It also contains an intrinsic **body diode** from source to drain.
- An **IGBT** (insulated-gate bipolar transistor) is driven like a MOSFET but conducts like a bipolar transistor, with a roughly constant on-state drop of 1 to 3 V. It suits high voltages (from 600 V to 6.5 kV) and high currents, but switches more slowly and so at lower frequencies, typically a few to tens of kilohertz.
- **Wide-bandgap** devices are made of semiconductors whose bandgap is about three times silicon's (1.12 eV): **silicon carbide** (SiC, 3.26 eV for the 4H polytype) and **gallium nitride** (GaN, 3.4 eV). A wider bandgap supports a critical electric field roughly ten times higher, so a device can block the same voltage with a much thinner, more heavily doped drift layer, with lower resistance and less stored charge. SiC MOSFETs (from 650 V to 3.3 kV) switch faster than silicon IGBTs at similar voltages and tolerate high temperatures; GaN transistors (mostly up to 650 V) switch fastest of all.

</Definition>

The choice follows the voltage and the frequency. A 12 V-to-1 V converter for a processor uses silicon MOSFETs at hundreds of kilohertz to a few megahertz. A compact 65 W USB-C charger increasingly uses GaN, because faster switching allows a smaller transformer. An 800 V electric-car inverter uses SiC MOSFETs, and the main inverters of many cars on 400 V batteries now do too. A wind turbine's converter or a locomotive's drive uses silicon IGBT modules, and the largest HVDC converters use IGBTs or thyristors by the thousand. These devices are made by many companies in Europe, the United States, Japan and China, and wide-bandgap manufacturing has expanded quickly since the late 2010s.

</Section>

The **buck converter** (left) switches its input onto a node for a fraction $D$ of each switching period $T = 1/f_{\text{sw}}$, the **duty cycle**. The switch-node voltage is a train of pulses, $V_{\text{in}}$ for time $DT$ and about 0 V (through the diode) for $(1-D)T$. The inductor and capacitor form a low-pass filter that passes the pulses' average, $DV_{\text{in}}$, and blocks most of the switching frequency. The way to make this precise, and to analyse any converter, rests on two facts about periodic steady state.

<Theorem name="Volt-second and charge balance">

In periodic steady state (every waveform repeats each period $T$), the average voltage across any inductor is zero, and the average current into any capacitor is zero.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For an inductor, $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$. Integrating over one period, $\int_0^T v_L\,dt = L\,[i_L(T) - i_L(0)] = 0$, because the current repeats. Dividing by $T$, the average of $v_L$ is zero. For a capacitor, $i_C = C\,dv_C/dt$ and the same argument with $v_C(T) = v_C(0)$ gives an average $i_C$ of zero.

</Proof>

**Buck.** With the switch on, the inductor sees $V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}}$ for $DT$; with it off, $-V_{\text{out}}$ for $(1-D)T$ (taking the output as nearly constant and the diode as ideal). Volt-second balance:
$$(V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}})\,DT - V_{\text{out}}(1-D)T = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_{\text{out}} = D\,V_{\text{in}}.$$
Charge balance on the output capacitor says the inductor's average current equals the load current. The inductor current ramps up by $\Delta I$ while the switch is on and down by the same amount while it is off:
$$\Delta I = \frac{(V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}})\,D}{L\,f_{\text{sw}}}.$$
This triangular ripple flows into the capacitor; the charge it delivers above the average, a triangle of area $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{T}{2} \cdot \tfrac{\Delta I}{2}$, raises the capacitor's voltage by
$$\Delta V = \frac{\Delta I}{8\,f_{\text{sw}}\,C},$$
the output ripple of an ideal capacitor. (A real capacitor's equivalent series resistance adds $\Delta I \cdot R_{\text{ESR}}$, often the larger term.)

**Boost.** In the boost converter (right) the inductor sits at the input. With the switch on, the inductor is across the input and charges: $v_L = V_{\text{in}}$ for $DT$. With it off, its current is forced through the diode into the output: $v_L = V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}}$ for $(1-D)T$. Volt-second balance:
$$V_{\text{in}}D + (V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}})(1-D) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_{\text{out}} = \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{1-D}.$$
At $D = 0.5$ the boost doubles its input; the input current is $1/(1-D)$ times the output current, so power is conserved. Combine the two ideas and you get the **buck-boost** ($V_{\text{out}} = -DV_{\text{in}}/(1-D)$), and put a transformer in place of its inductor and you get the **flyback**, the isolated converter inside most small chargers.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Notice that the duty cycle, not any component value, sets the conversion ratio. $L$ and $C$ only set the ripple and the dynamics. That is why one converter design can serve a range of loads, and why the controller's only job is to nudge $D$.

</Narrator>

<Section id="simulate" kind="computation" title="A buck converter, simulated">

Here is a buck converter of the kind on a laptop's motherboard, stepping 12 V down to 5 V at 2 A at a switching frequency of 500 kHz, with $L = 4.7\ \mu$H and $C = 22\ \mu$F. The simulation steps the inductor current and capacitor voltage through time, 480 steps per switching period, with an ideal switch and diode, starting from rest.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A buck converter: 12 V in, 5 V out at 2 A, switching at 500 kHz (ideal switch and diode).
V_in, V_target = 12.0, 5.0
f_sw = 500e3
L, C, R_load = 4.7e-6, 22e-6, 2.5
D = V_target / V_in                    # duty cycle from volt-second balance
T = 1 / f_sw
steps = 480                            # time steps per switching period
on_steps = round(D * steps)            # 200 of them with the switch on
dt = T / steps
periods = 750                          # 1.5 ms: long enough for the LC filter to settle

i_L = v_C = 0.0
v_peak = 0.0
last = []                              # (time, i_L, v_C) over the last few periods
for p in range(periods):
    for s in range(steps):
        on = s < on_steps
        v_sw = V_in if on else 0.0     # the switch node: V_in, or 0 V through the conducting diode
        i_L += dt * (v_sw - v_C) / L
        if i_L < 0:                    # the diode blocks reverse current (discontinuous mode)
            i_L = 0.0
        v_C += dt * (i_L - v_C / R_load) / C
        v_peak = max(v_peak, v_C)
        if p >= periods - 20:
            last.append((s * dt + p * T, i_L, v_C))

t, i, v = map(np.array, zip(*last))
dI_formula = (V_in - V_target) * D / (L * f_sw)
dV_formula = dI_formula / (8 * f_sw * C)
print(f"duty cycle D = {D:.4f}")
print(f"output: average {v.mean():.3f} V, ripple {1e3 * (v.max() - v.min()):.1f} mV peak to peak"
      f" (formula {1e3 * dV_formula:.1f} mV)")
print(f"inductor current: average {i.mean():.3f} A, ripple {i.max() - i.min():.3f} A peak to peak"
      f" (formula {dI_formula:.3f} A)")
print(f"start-up overshoot: output peaked at {v_peak:.2f} V")

print("\ninductor current over one switching period (each # is 0.1 A above 1.2 A):")
for k in range(0, steps, 30):
    print(f"{t[k] * 1e6 - t[0] * 1e6:5.2f} us  {'ON ' if k < on_steps else 'off'}  {i[k]:.3f} A  "
          + "#" * int(round((i[k] - 1.2) / 0.1)))
```

The simulation and the formulas agree: 5.000 V out at 2.000 A, with 1.242 A of inductor ripple (formula 1.241 A) and 14.1 mV of output ripple (formula 14.1 mV), about 0.3%. The start-up is another matter: switched on at the full duty cycle, the undamped LC filter rings, and the output overshoots to 8.75 V, enough to damage a 5 V load. Real controllers prevent this with a **soft start** that ramps the duty cycle up over a few milliseconds, and they regulate the output with a feedback loop (designed with the tools of <LessonLink slug="feedback-control">Feedback control</LessonLink>) rather than trusting a fixed $D$.

The same converter in SPICE, with a real switch resistance and a Schottky diode model, shows what ideal models leave out:

```spice check

```

ngspice reports an output of about 4.75 V instead of 5 V, with a ripple of about 14.6 mV and an inductor ripple of about 1.28 A. The diode's forward drop, about 0.4 V for the $(1-D)$ part of each period, lowers the switch node's average: volt-second balance now gives $V_{\text{out}} \approx DV_{\text{in}} - (1-D)V_F$. In a real product the feedback loop would raise $D$ to about 0.44 to restore 5 V, and a low-voltage design would replace the diode with a second MOSFET, switched in antiphase (**synchronous rectification**), whose few milliohms drop millivolts instead.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Both models assume **continuous conduction**: the inductor current never falls to zero. Here that holds while the load draws more than half the ripple, $\Delta I/2 \approx 0.62$ A. At lighter loads the current reaches zero, the diode stops conducting before the period ends, and $V_{\text{out}} = DV_{\text{in}}$ no longer holds; the output rises unless the controller cuts $D$. Light-load behaviour is where many converter designs, and many datasheet efficiency claims, are tested.

</Narrator>

<Section id="inverters" kind="examples" title="Inverters and PWM">

An **inverter** turns DC into AC. Its building block is the **half-bridge** or **leg**: two switches in series across the DC supply, with the output taken from their midpoint, which is connected alternately to the positive and the negative rail. The two switches must never be on together, or they short the supply (**shoot-through**), so the controller leaves a short **dead time**, tens to hundreds of nanoseconds, between turning one off and the other on. Two legs make a single-phase **H-bridge** (which also drives a DC motor in both directions); three legs make the **three-phase inverter** of every variable-speed drive, electric car and solar or battery inverter.

To make a sinusoid with switches that are only on or off, the controller varies each leg's duty cycle smoothly. In **sine-triangle PWM**, a sinusoidal reference at the desired output frequency is compared with a fast triangular **carrier**; the upper switch is on whenever the reference is above the carrier. The leg's average voltage over each carrier period follows the reference, so its output contains the wanted sinusoid, of peak $m_a V_{\text{dc}}/2$ for a **modulation index** $m_a \le 1$, plus harmonics clustered around multiples of the carrier frequency, which the motor's inductance or a filter removes easily.

The program below builds a three-phase inverter's three legs on a 400 V DC link, with $m_a = 0.8$, a 50 Hz output and a carrier at 99 times that, 4950 Hz, and takes the spectrum of one leg's voltage and of the line-to-line voltage between two legs.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A three-phase, two-level inverter with sine-triangle PWM.
V_dc = 400.0          # DC link voltage, V
f1 = 50.0             # output (fundamental) frequency, Hz
m_f = 99              # carrier frequency / fundamental: a multiple of 3, so f_carrier = 4950 Hz
m_a = 0.8             # modulation index: peak of the reference / peak of the carrier
N = 99 * 400          # samples in one fundamental period
t = np.arange(N) / (N * f1)

carrier = 2 * np.abs(2 * ((m_f * f1 * t) % 1.0) - 1) - 1          # triangle between -1 and +1
legs = []
for k in range(3):                                                 # legs a, b, c
    reference = m_a * np.cos(2 * np.pi * f1 * t - 2 * np.pi * k / 3)
    upper_on = reference > carrier                                 # compare: upper switch on, or lower
    legs.append(np.where(upper_on, V_dc / 2, -V_dc / 2))           # leg voltage from the DC midpoint
v_a, v_b = legs[0], legs[1]
v_ab = v_a - v_b                                                   # line-to-line output voltage

def amplitudes(x):
    """Peak amplitude of each harmonic (index n = n times 50 Hz) over one fundamental period."""
    return 2 * np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x)) / len(x)

A_a, A_ab = amplitudes(v_a), amplitudes(v_ab)
print(f"expected fundamentals: leg {m_a * V_dc / 2:.1f} V peak, line-to-line {np.sqrt(3) * m_a * V_dc / 2:.1f} V peak")
print("harmonic   frequency    leg a (V)   line a-b (V)")
for n in [1, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 95, 97, 99, 101, 103, 196, 197, 198, 199, 200]:
    print(f"{n:8d}   {n * f1:7.0f} Hz   {A_a[n]:9.1f}   {A_ab[n]:11.1f}")
low = np.sqrt(np.sum(A_ab[2:60] ** 2) / 2)
print(f"RMS of all line-to-line harmonics from 100 Hz to 3 kHz: {low:.2f} V")
```

One leg's fundamental is 159.9 V peak, matching $m_a V_{\text{dc}}/2 = 160$ V, and its largest harmonic sits at the carrier frequency, 163.8 V at 4950 Hz, with sidebands at 4850 and 5050 Hz and another cluster near 9900 Hz. The low-order harmonics a motor would feel (the 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th) are a fraction of a volt, at the level of the simulation's time resolution. In the line-to-line voltage, the fundamental is $\sqrt3$ larger, 276.9 V, and the component at exactly the carrier frequency **vanishes**: each leg has the same carrier, so the carrier harmonic is identical in all three legs and cancels when two legs are subtracted. This is why the carrier ratio is chosen as a multiple of three. Industrial drives go further: adding a third harmonic to the references, or using the equivalent **space-vector modulation**, raises the usable output voltage by about 15% (a factor $2/\sqrt3$) without distorting the line-to-line voltage.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The cost of PWM is switching loss: every edge of those pulses spends energy in the transistors. A drive switching at 5 kHz makes 10,000 edges a second per switch; a GaN charger at 500 kHz makes a million. Doubling the carrier frequency halves the size of the filter but roughly doubles the switching loss, and choosing it is the central trade-off of converter design.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="rectifiers" kind="computation" title="Rectifiers and power-factor correction">

Going the other way, from AC to DC, the simplest circuit is a **diode bridge** followed by a large smoothing capacitor. It is cheap and robust, and for decades it was the front end of nearly every electronic device. Its flaw is how it draws current. The capacitor holds the DC voltage near the peak of the AC, so the diodes conduct only for a short time near each peak, when the supply voltage rises above the capacitor's. The supply sees narrow, tall pulses of current instead of a sinusoid.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A diode-bridge rectifier with a smoothing capacitor: the front end of a simple mains adapter.
V_rms, f = 230.0, 50.0
R_s = 1.0              # resistance of the supply wiring and the bridge, ohm
C = 100e-6             # smoothing capacitor, F
R_load = 1000.0        # the DC load, ohm (about 100 W at 310 V)
dt = 1e-6
n_cycle = int(round(1 / (f * dt)))
v_supply = (np.sqrt(2) * V_rms * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * dt * np.arange(10 * n_cycle))).tolist()

v_C = 0.0
i_last = []
for n, v_s in enumerate(v_supply):                 # ten cycles of the supply; keep the last one
    i_in = max(abs(v_s) - v_C, 0.0) / R_s          # the bridge conducts only while |v_s| exceeds v_C
    v_C += dt * (i_in - v_C / R_load) / C
    if n >= 9 * n_cycle:
        i_last.append(i_in if v_s >= 0 else -i_in) # the supply current changes sign with the supply

i = np.array(i_last)
v = np.array(v_supply[9 * n_cycle:])
P = np.mean(v * i)
I_rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(i**2))
A = 2 * np.abs(np.fft.rfft(i)) / len(i)            # A[n] is the harmonic at n x 50 Hz
thd = np.sqrt(np.sum(A[2:41] ** 2)) / A[1]
print(f"real power {P:.1f} W, RMS current {I_rms:.2f} A, peak current {np.abs(i).max():.2f} A, DC {v_C:.0f} V")
print(f"power factor {P / (V_rms * I_rms):.3f}; total harmonic distortion of the current {thd:.0%}")
print("harmonic n (at n x 50 Hz), % of fundamental: " + "  ".join(f"n={n}: {100 * A[n] / A[1]:.0f}%" for n in [3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13]))
print(f"the bridge conducts for {np.mean(np.abs(i) > 0.01):.0%} of each cycle")
print(f"a PFC front end, drawing a sinusoid in phase, needs {P / V_rms:.2f} A RMS for the same power")
```

For about 98 W of output, the bridge draws pulses 3.56 A high, conducting only 15% of the time, with an RMS current of 0.91 A. The power factor is 0.469 even though the current pulses are centred on the voltage peaks, because the current is not sinusoidal: its harmonics carry no real power but add to the RMS current. The third harmonic is 95% of the fundamental. Multiply by every computer, television and LED driver on a street and the distortion becomes a problem for the grid: extra heating in cables and transformers, third harmonics adding in the neutral (as in <LessonLink slug="three-phase-power">AC power and three-phase systems</LessonLink>), and distorted supply voltages. Standards such as IEC 61000-3-2 therefore limit the harmonic currents that equipment may draw from public low-voltage supplies.

The fix is a **power-factor-correction (PFC) front end**: a boost converter placed between the bridge and the capacitor, whose controller makes the inductor current follow the shape of the rectified supply voltage, cycle by cycle, at tens or hundreds of kilohertz. The supply then sees a nearly sinusoidal current in phase with the voltage, with power factors above 0.99, and the boost output provides a regulated DC bus of around 400 V for the stage that follows. The program's last line shows the gain: the same power needs only 0.43 A RMS instead of 0.91 A. Large three-phase equipment such as EV fast chargers and data-centre rectifiers uses three-phase active front ends that do the same job, and the newest designs drop the diode bridge altogether (**bridgeless** or **totem-pole** PFC, often with GaN or SiC switches) to save its losses.

</Section>

<Section id="losses" kind="examples" title="Losses, heat and efficiency">

Every converter's efficiency is a ledger of small losses. For a MOSFET carrying current $I$ with duty cycle $D$:

- **Conduction loss**: $D\,I^2 R_{\text{DS(on)}}$ (for an IGBT or a diode, roughly $V_{\text{drop}} \times$ average current).
- **Switching loss**: during each transition the device briefly carries current and blocks voltage at once. For transition times $t_r$ and $t_f$, a rough estimate is $\tfrac12 V I (t_r + t_f) f_{\text{sw}}$.
- **Gate-drive loss**: charging the gate each cycle, $Q_g V_{\text{drive}} f_{\text{sw}}$.
- And outside the switches: the diode's or synchronous rectifier's conduction loss, the inductor's winding and core loss, and the capacitors' ESR loss.

<Example title="Where the buck converter's losses go">

Take the 12 V to 5 V, 2 A buck converter above (10 W output) with a MOSFET of 10 mΩ, transitions of 10 ns each, 10 nC of gate charge driven at 5 V, a Schottky diode dropping 0.4 V, and an inductor of 15 mΩ winding resistance:

| Loss | Calculation | Watts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MOSFET conduction | $0.417 \times 2^2 \times 0.010$ | 0.017 |
| MOSFET switching | $\tfrac12 \times 12 \times 2 \times 20\ \text{ns} \times 500\ \text{kHz}$ | 0.120 |
| Gate drive | $10\ \text{nC} \times 5\ \text{V} \times 500\ \text{kHz}$ | 0.025 |
| Diode | $0.4 \times 2 \times 0.583$ | 0.467 |
| Inductor winding | $2^2 \times 0.015$ | 0.060 |
| **Total** | | **0.688** |

The efficiency is $10/10.688 = 93.6\%$, and two-thirds of the loss is the diode. Replace it with a second 10 mΩ MOSFET and its loss falls to $0.583 \times 2^2 \times 0.010 = 0.023$ W, the total to 0.245 W, and the efficiency rises to 97.6%. That single change is why nearly every low-voltage converter today is synchronous.

</Example>

**Heat** is the other half of the story. The power a device dissipates must flow from its junction to the surrounding air through a chain of thermal resistances, in kelvin per watt: junction to case $R_{\theta\text{JC}}$, case to heatsink $R_{\theta\text{CS}}$ (the thermal pad or grease), and heatsink to air $R_{\theta\text{SA}}$. Like resistors in series:
$$T_J = T_A + P\,(R_{\theta\text{JC}} + R_{\theta\text{CS}} + R_{\theta\text{SA}}).$$
A transistor in a TO-220 package dissipating 6 W, with $R_{\theta\text{JC}} = 0.8$ K/W, a 0.5 K/W pad and a 5 K/W heatsink, in 45 °C air, runs at $45 + 6 \times 6.3 = 82.8$ °C, comfortably below a typical 150 or 175 °C limit. Without the heatsink, the package alone has around 62 K/W to air, and the junction would head for $45 + 6 \times 62 = 417$ °C: the device would fail long before. Thermal design (heatsinks, fans, liquid cooling in cars and data centres, and the circuit board's copper as a heat spreader) often sets a converter's size more than its electronics do.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Temperature also sets lifetime. In many converters the first part to wear out is not a transistor but an aluminium electrolytic capacitor, whose electrolyte slowly dries out. A widely used rule of thumb, from the Arrhenius law, is that its life roughly halves for every 10 °C hotter it runs, which is why designers place electrolytics away from hot spots, or avoid them altogether in long-life products such as solar microinverters.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="applications" kind="human" title="Where power electronics works">

**USB-C chargers.** A modern charger is a PFC stage (above about 75 W of input) followed by an isolated flyback or resonant converter that steps the rectified mains down to the voltage the device negotiates over the cable: 5 to 20 V in USB Power Delivery's standard range, and up to 48 V and 240 W with the [USB PD 3.1](https://www.usb.org/usb-charger-pd) extended range. GaN switches let these converters run at hundreds of kilohertz, shrinking the transformer, which is why chargers of the same power have become much smaller since about 2018.

**EV chargers and drivetrains.** An electric car's **on-board charger** is an AC-to-DC converter of a few kilowatts (commonly 7 to 22 kW), with PFC, used at home and at public AC points. **DC fast chargers** put the converter in the roadside cabinet instead, delivering from tens to hundreds of kilowatts directly to the battery, from a three-phase grid connection through an active front end and isolated DC-DC stages. Inside the car, the traction **inverter** drives the motor of <LessonLink slug="transformers-and-machines">Transformers and electric machines</LessonLink> by PWM and runs in reverse to recover energy when braking, and a DC-DC converter supplies the 12 V or 48 V systems. Cars with 800 V batteries, whose higher voltage halves the current for a given charging power, rely on SiC devices.

**Solar and battery inverters.** A solar inverter has a DC-DC stage that operates the panels at their maximum power point (the subject of <LessonLink slug="renewables-and-storage">Renewables, storage and electric vehicles</LessonLink>) and an inverter that injects current synchronised to the grid. They range from **microinverters** behind a single panel, through **string inverters** of a few to a hundred kilowatts, to **central inverters** of several megawatts. Battery storage uses the same hardware in both directions, and how these inverters behave during grid disturbances is now one of the central questions of grid engineering, taken up in <LessonLink slug="the-power-grid">The power grid</LessonLink>.

**And everywhere else**: variable-speed drives in industry, LED drivers, induction hobs, railway traction, HVDC converter stations, and the power delivery of AI data centres, which is the subject of <LessonLink slug="powering-data-centres">Powering data centres</LessonLink>. MIT's open course [Power Electronics](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-334-power-electronics-spring-2007/) and the [IEEE Power Electronics Society](https://www.ieee-pels.org/) are good places to go deeper.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A boost converter creates energy: 5 V in, 12 V out."** Power is conserved, less losses. A boost converter delivering 12 V at 1 A from 5 V draws at least 2.4 A from its input. The same is true of every converter and every transformer.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The duty cycle alone sets the output voltage."** Only for ideal components in continuous conduction. Diode and switch drops, winding resistance, light loads (discontinuous conduction) and input-voltage changes all shift the output, which is why every practical converter regulates with feedback.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Higher switching frequency is always better."** It shrinks the inductors, capacitors and transformers, but switching and gate losses grow in proportion to frequency, and faster edges radiate more electromagnetic interference. Every design picks a frequency that balances size, efficiency and EMI.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Wide-bandgap devices make silicon obsolete."** SiC and GaN win where their speed or voltage rating pays for their cost, but silicon MOSFETs still dominate low-voltage conversion, and silicon IGBTs remain the workhorses of high-power industrial and traction converters.

</Misconception>

</Section>
