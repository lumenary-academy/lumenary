<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Making small signals big">

A microphone produces a few millivolts. A guitar pickup, an electrocardiogram electrode, a radio antenna and a strain gauge produce signals of the same size or smaller. The converter that will turn the signal into numbers wants a volt or more, and a loudspeaker wants watts. In between sits an **amplifier**, which makes a copy of the signal that is larger but otherwise as nearly identical as possible.

The <LessonLink slug="transistors">previous lesson</LessonLink> used transistors as switches, slammed fully on or fully off. An amplifier uses them gently, in the middle of their range, where a small change at the base or gate makes a proportionally larger change in the collector or drain current. The difficulty is that the devices are *nonlinear*, exponential (BJT) or square-law (MOSFET). This lesson's central idea is that any smooth curve looks straight if you look at a small enough piece of it. Choose a quiet operating point, keep the signal small, and the transistor becomes a linear circuit element whose gain you can calculate with the tools of the Circuits level.

</Section>

<Section id="bias" kind="intuition" title="Biasing: choosing the operating point">

**Biasing** sets the DC voltages and currents around which the signal will swing: the **operating point**, or **quiescent point** (Q-point). For a BJT amplifier that means a collector current $I_C$ and a collector–emitter voltage $V_{CE}$ that keep it in the forward-active region with room for the signal to swing both ways: the collector must not fall into saturation on the negative swings, nor rise to the supply on the positive ones.

The naive way to bias a BJT is a single resistor from the supply to the base, setting $I_B$ and hence $I_C = \beta I_B$. It fails, because $\beta$ varies by a factor of two or three between parts and with temperature. The standard cure is the **four-resistor bias**: a divider $R_1, R_2$ fixes the base voltage, and an **emitter resistor** $R_E$ turns that voltage into a current:
$$I_E \approx \frac{V_B - V_{BE}}{R_E}.$$
If the transistor's current rises, the emitter voltage rises, $V_{BE}$ falls, and the current is pulled back: **negative feedback**, the subject of <LessonLink slug="feedback-and-oscillators">Feedback, stability and oscillators</LessonLink>. Replacing the divider by its Thévenin equivalent ($V_{th}$ behind $R_{th} = R_1 \parallel R_2$, from <LessonLink slug="network-theorems">Network theorems</LessonLink>) gives the exact relation
$$I_E = \frac{V_{th} - V_{BE}}{R_E + R_{th}/(\beta + 1)},$$
which depends on $\beta$ only through the small term $R_{th}/(\beta+1)$.

```python run
import math

VT, IS, VA = 0.025852, 1e-14, 100.0          # thermal voltage, saturation current, Early voltage
VCC, R1, R2, RC, RE = 12.0, 47e3, 10e3, 4.7e3, 1e3

def bias(beta):
    """Solve the four-resistor bias circuit: Thevenin base source, exponential V_BE."""
    vth, rth = VCC * R2 / (R1 + R2), R1 * R2 / (R1 + R2)
    ic = 1e-3
    for _ in range(50):                       # fixed-point iteration on V_BE (converges fast)
        vbe = VT * math.log(ic / IS)
        ie = (vth - vbe) / (RE + rth / (beta + 1))
        ic = ie * beta / (beta + 1)
    return ic, vbe, VCC - ic * RC - ie * RE

print("four-resistor bias (R1 47k, R2 10k, RC 4.7k, RE 1k, VCC 12 V)")
for beta in (100, 200, 400):
    ic, vbe, vce = bias(beta)
    print(f"  beta {beta:3d}: I_C = {ic * 1e3:.3f} mA, V_BE = {vbe:.3f} V, V_CE = {vce:.2f} V")

print("one base resistor from VCC instead, sized for 1.38 mA at beta = 200")
RB = (VCC - 0.66) / (1.378e-3 / 200)
for beta in (100, 200, 400):
    ic = beta * (VCC - 0.66) / RB
    vce = VCC - ic * RC
    if vce > 0.2:
        print(f"  beta {beta:3d}: I_C = {ic * 1e3:.3f} mA, V_CE = {vce:.2f} V")
    else:
        print(f"  beta {beta:3d}: would need V_CE = {vce:.2f} V, impossible: the transistor saturates")

ic, vbe, vce = bias(200)
gm = ic / VT
rpi = 200 / gm
ro = VA / ic
print(f"\nsmall-signal parameters at beta = 200: gm = {gm * 1e3:.1f} mS, "
      f"r_pi = {rpi / 1e3:.2f} kohm, r_o = {ro / 1e3:.1f} kohm, gm*r_o = {gm * ro:.0f}")
RL = 10e3
rload = 1 / (1 / RC + 1 / ro + 1 / RL)
print(f"midband gain with RE bypassed, 10 kohm load: -gm (RC || r_o || RL) = {-gm * rload:.1f}")
print(f"with RE left unbypassed: about -(RC || RL) / (RE + 1/gm) = {-(1 / (1 / RC + 1 / RL)) / (RE + 1 / gm):.2f}")
```

With four-resistor bias, a fourfold spread in $\beta$, from 100 to 400, moves the collector current only from 1.321 to 1.409 mA, under 7%. With a single base resistor, the same spread takes the current from 0.689 mA to a value the circuit cannot reach: the transistor saturates and the amplifier stops working. The rest of the output looks ahead to the small-signal parameters, which the next section defines.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The bias network is a design for the *worst* part, not the typical one. Check it at the lowest and highest $\beta$, at the lowest and highest temperature ($V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV per kelvin, which raises the current), and with resistor tolerances, and confirm that the collector still has room to swing at every corner. A rule of thumb that makes this easy: let about a tenth of the supply, here 1.4 V, fall across $R_E$, and make the divider current ten times the base current.

</Narrator>

<Section id="small-signal" kind="definitions" title="Small-signal models">

Write each quantity as its DC operating value plus a small signal: $v_{BE} = V_{BE} + v_{be}$, $i_C = I_C + i_c$. Expanding the exponential in a Taylor series around the operating point,
$$i_C = I_S e^{(V_{BE} + v_{be})/V_T} = I_C\, e^{v_{be}/V_T} = I_C\left(1 + \frac{v_{be}}{V_T} + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{v_{be}}{V_T}\right)^2 + \cdots\right).$$
If $|v_{be}|$ is a few millivolts, much less than $V_T \approx 26$ mV, the squared term is small and $i_c \approx (I_C/V_T)\,v_{be}$: the signal current is *proportional* to the signal voltage.

<Definition term="Transconductance and the small-signal parameters">

The **transconductance** $g_m = \partial i_C / \partial v_{BE}$ at the operating point is the transistor's gain, in amperes per volt (siemens). With it come the input resistance $r_\pi$ seen at the base and the output resistance $r_o$ seen at the collector or drain:

| | BJT | MOSFET (square law) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $g_m$ | $I_C / V_T$ | $2 I_D / V_{ov} = \sqrt{2 k I_D}$ |
| input | $r_\pi = \beta / g_m$ | infinite (insulated gate) |
| $r_o$ | $V_A / I_C$ ($V_A$ = Early voltage) | $1 / (\lambda I_D)$ |
| intrinsic gain $g_m r_o$ | $V_A / V_T$, often 1000–5000 | $2 / (\lambda V_{ov})$, often 10–100 |

</Definition>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Small-signal analysis is calculus in disguise: $g_m$ is the slope of the device's curve at the operating point, and the small-signal circuit is the tangent-line approximation of the whole circuit. That is why every DC source becomes a short (its voltage does not change), and why the gain depends on where you biased: change $I_C$ and you change the slope.

</Narrator>

The striking fact about a BJT is that $g_m$ depends only on the current: every BJT at 1 mA has $g_m \approx 38.7$ mS, whatever its size or maker. A MOSFET's $g_m$ depends on its size and overdrive too, and for the same current it is smaller: at 1 mA with a typical overdrive of 0.2 V, $g_m = 2 \times 1/0.2 = 10$ mS.

The **hybrid-π model** puts these together into a linear circuit that replaces the transistor in signal calculations, with two capacitances added for high frequencies: $C_\pi$ between base and emitter (the depletion capacitance of the junction plus the charge stored in the base), and $C_\mu$ across the reverse-biased collector–base junction. For a MOSFET, $r_\pi$ is infinite, $C_{gs}$ plays the part of $C_\pi$ and $C_{gd}$ the part of $C_\mu$.

<Section id="gain-stages" kind="examples" title="Common-emitter and common-source gain">

In the amplifier of the figure, the signal enters at the base and leaves at the collector, with the emitter grounded for signals by $C_E$: a **common-emitter** (CE) stage. Replace the transistor by its hybrid-π model and every capacitor by a short at midband frequencies. The input voltage appears directly across $r_\pi$, so the collector current is $g_m v_{in}$, and it flows through everything connected to the collector in parallel, $R_C \parallel r_o \parallel R_L$:
$$A_v = \frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}} = -g_m\,(R_C \parallel r_o \parallel R_L).$$
The minus sign means the stage **inverts**: when the base rises, more current flows through $R_C$ and the collector falls. For our circuit, $g_m = 53.3$ mS and $R_C \parallel r_o \parallel R_L = 3.06$ kΩ, so $A_v \approx -163$, as the program printed. The common-source (CS) stage of a MOSFET gives $A_v = -g_m (R_D \parallel r_o \parallel R_L)$ in exactly the same way.

<Example title="Trading gain for predictability: emitter degeneration">

Leave $R_E$ unbypassed and the signal sees it too. The input now divides between the junction and $R_E$, and the gain becomes
$$A_v \approx -\frac{R_C \parallel R_L}{R_E + 1/g_m}.$$
With $1/g_m = 18.8\ \Omega$ and $R_E = 1$ kΩ, the gain drops to about $-3.14$. That looks like a loss, but notice what has gone from the formula: almost all dependence on $g_m$, which changes with temperature, with the operating point, and over the signal's own swing, which causes distortion. The gain is now set by a ratio of resistors. A common compromise bypasses part of $R_E$ and leaves a few tens of ohms unbypassed, for a gain of perhaps 30 to 50 that is both useful and predictable. This is negative feedback again: giving up gain to buy accuracy and linearity.

</Example>

The best gain a single transistor can give is with a load much larger than its own $r_o$: the **intrinsic gain** $g_m r_o$. A BJT's is $V_A/V_T$, about 3900 in our example. A modern short-channel MOSFET may manage only 10 to 30, which is why integrated amplifiers replace load resistors by **current-source loads** (transistors whose output resistance is high) and stack transistors in **cascodes** to multiply output resistance.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Where does the "few millivolts" limit bite? Feed the bypassed stage 10 mV peak: $v_{be}/V_T = 0.39$, so the exponential's squared term is about a fifth of the linear one. The collector swings about 1.6 V, but one half of each cycle is about 1.5 times the size of the other: a badly distorted copy. Degeneration reduces the voltage across the junction itself, which is why it linearises.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="followers" kind="examples" title="Followers: gain of one, and why that is useful">

Take the output from the emitter instead of the collector, with the collector tied to the supply: a **common-collector** stage, better called an **emitter follower**. The emitter follows the base, one $V_{BE}$ lower, so the voltage gain is just under one:
$$A_v = \frac{g_m R_E'}{1 + g_m R_E'} \approx 1, \qquad R_{in} \approx (\beta + 1)\,R_E', \qquad R_{out} \approx \frac{1}{g_m} + \frac{R_{sig}}{\beta + 1},$$
where $R_E'$ is everything connected to the emitter. The follower's value is in its resistances: it presents a high resistance to the source and a low one to the load. It is a **buffer**, an impedance converter.

<Example title="Driving a 100 Ω load from a 10 kΩ source">

Connect a 100 Ω load directly to a source with 10 kΩ of internal resistance, and the load receives only $100/10\,100 \approx 1\%$ of the signal. Put an emitter follower biased at 5 mA between them, with $\beta = 200$: its output resistance is $1/g_m + R_{sig}/(\beta + 1) = 5.2 + 49.8 \approx 55\ \Omega$, so the load now receives $100/155 \approx 65\%$ of the signal. The follower has no voltage gain, yet it multiplied the signal at the load by about 65.

</Example>

The MOSFET equivalent, the **source follower**, has infinite input resistance and an output resistance of about $1/g_m$. Its gain is a little further below one, because the body effect raises the threshold as the source rises. Followers appear at the outputs of op-amps, as level shifters, and as the buffers of voltage references.

</Section>

<Section id="differential" kind="computation" title="The differential pair">

Most amplifiers inside chips do not amplify a voltage relative to ground. They amplify the *difference* between two inputs, and ignore what the two have in common. The circuit that does this is the **differential pair**: two matched transistors whose emitters (or sources) are joined and fed by a single **tail current** $I_{tail}$.

Because the tail current is fixed, whatever one transistor gains the other loses. With bipolar transistors the exponential law makes the split exact: the ratio of the collector currents is $e^{v_d/V_T}$, where $v_d$ is the difference between the base voltages, and the differential output is
$$v_{out} = I_{tail} R_C \tanh\!\left(\frac{v_d}{2V_T}\right).$$
For small $v_d$, $\tanh x \approx x$ and the gain is $g_m R_C$ with $g_m$ of one transistor at half the tail current. A voltage applied equally to both bases, a **common-mode** signal such as hum picked up on both wires, moves both emitters together and leaves the split unchanged. With an imperfect tail source of output resistance $R_{tail}$, a little common-mode signal leaks through, with gain about $R_C/(2R_{tail})$ at each output. The ratio of differential to common-mode gain is the **common-mode rejection ratio** (CMRR).

```python run
import math

VT = 0.025852
I_TAIL, RC = 1e-3, 10e3        # tail current and collector resistors
R_TAIL = 200e3                 # output resistance of a real (imperfect) tail current source

def collector_currents(vd):
    """Large-signal BJT pair: the tail current divides according to exp(V_BE / V_T)."""
    ic1 = I_TAIL / (1 + math.exp(-vd / VT))
    return ic1, I_TAIL - ic1

print(" v_d (mV)   I_C1 (mA)   I_C2 (mA)   v_out (V)   linear model (V)")
gm = I_TAIL / 2 / VT                           # each transistor carries half the tail current
for vd_mv in (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 100, 200):
    ic1, ic2 = collector_currents(vd_mv / 1e3)
    vout = (ic1 - ic2) * RC                    # differential output, (V_C2 - V_C1)
    print(f"{vd_mv:8d}   {ic1 * 1e3:9.4f}   {ic2 * 1e3:9.4f}   {vout:9.4f}   {gm * RC * vd_mv / 1e3:12.4f}")

print(f"\ndifferential gain gm*RC = {gm * RC:.1f}; exact output = I_tail*RC*tanh(v_d / 2V_T)")
acm = RC / (2 * R_TAIL)                        # each side, for a common-mode input
print(f"common-mode gain (single-ended) about RC / (2 R_tail) = {acm:.3f}")
adm = gm * RC / 2                              # single-ended differential gain
print(f"CMRR = A_dm / A_cm = {adm / acm:.0f}, or {20 * math.log10(adm / acm):.1f} dB")
```

The pair is linear only for $|v_d|$ up to about 10 mV (within about 1.2% of the straight line there); at 40 mV it delivers 6.5 V instead of the linear 7.7 V, and beyond about 100 mV it simply steers the whole tail current to one side. That gentle, symmetric saturation is very useful: a differential pair overdriven by a large input limits cleanly instead of breaking down. The CMRR of 3868, about 72 dB, comes from a tail source of only 200 kΩ; integrated designs use better current sources and reach 80 to 120 dB. Every operational amplifier begins with a differential pair, which is how the <LessonLink slug="operational-amplifiers">next lesson</LessonLink> starts.

</Section>

<Section id="frequency" kind="computation" title="Frequency response and the Miller effect">

An amplifier's gain is flat only over a band of frequencies. At **low** frequencies the coupling and bypass capacitors stop being short circuits; at **high** frequencies the transistor's own capacitances $C_\pi$ and $C_\mu$ start to conduct. Each capacitor contributes a pole, as in <LessonLink slug="frequency-response-and-filters">Frequency response and filters</LessonLink>, and the two corners where the gain falls by 3 dB bound the **bandwidth**.

The low corner here is set by the emitter bypass capacitor, even though it is the largest. It must short out $R_E$ against the resistance looking into the emitter, which is tiny, about $1/g_m + (R_S \parallel R_{th})/(\beta+1) \approx 23\ \Omega$. So 100 µF gives a corner near $1/(2\pi \times 100\ \mu\text{F} \times 23\ \Omega) \approx 70$ Hz, while the 10 µF coupling capacitors, which see kilohms, contribute corners of only a few hertz.

The high corner is dominated by the small capacitance $C_\mu$, because of the **Miller effect**, first described for vacuum tubes by John Milton Miller around 1920. $C_\mu$ connects the input to an output that swings $A_v$ times as far in the opposite direction. The voltage across it is therefore $(1 - A_v)$ times the input voltage, so it draws $(1 - A_v)$ times as much current as a capacitor to ground would: from the input it looks like
$$C_{M} = C_\mu (1 + g_m R'_L),$$
where $R'_L = R_C \parallel r_o \parallel R_L$. With $C_\mu = 4$ pF and a gain of 163, that is about 657 pF, twenty times the $C_\pi$ of 31 pF. The input pole is then at $1/(2\pi R_{sig}'(C_\pi + C_M))$, with $R_{sig}' = R_S \parallel R_{th} \parallel r_\pi$.

The program below checks that estimate against an exact AC analysis of the whole small-signal circuit: it writes the nodal equations at each frequency, with complex admittances $sC$ for every capacitor, and solves them with NumPy, the method of <LessonLink slug="nodal-and-mesh-analysis">Nodal and mesh analysis</LessonLink> and exactly what SPICE's `.ac` does.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

# Small-signal values from the bias point (beta = 200, I_C = 1.378 mA)
gm, rpi, ro = 1.378e-3 / 0.025852, 200 / (1.378e-3 / 0.025852), 100 / 1.378e-3
RS, RTH, RC, RE, RL = 1e3, 47e3 * 10e3 / 57e3, 4.7e3, 1e3, 10e3
CIN, COUT, CE = 10e-6, 10e-6, 100e-6
CPI, CMU = 10e-12 + 0.4e-9 * gm, 4e-12        # junction + diffusion capacitance; collector junction

def gain(f):
    """Solve the hybrid-pi circuit's nodal equations at frequency f; return v_out / v_source."""
    s = 2j * math.pi * f
    n = 5                                         # nodes: 0 source side of C_in, 1 base, 2 emitter, 3 collector, 4 output
    Y = np.zeros((n, n), complex)
    I = np.zeros(n, complex)
    def admit(a, b, y):                           # an admittance y between nodes a and b (None = ground)
        for p, q in ((a, b), (b, a)):
            if p is not None:
                Y[p, p] += y
                if q is not None:
                    Y[p, q] -= y
    admit(0, None, 1 / RS); I[0] = 1 / RS         # 1 V source behind R_S, as its Norton equivalent
    admit(0, 1, s * CIN)
    admit(1, None, 1 / RTH)
    admit(1, 2, 1 / rpi + s * CPI)
    admit(1, 3, s * CMU)
    admit(3, 2, 1 / ro)
    admit(3, None, 1 / RC)
    admit(2, None, 1 / RE + s * CE)
    admit(3, 4, s * COUT)
    admit(4, None, 1 / RL)
    Y[3, 1] += gm; Y[3, 2] -= gm                  # g_m v_be flows from collector to emitter
    Y[2, 1] -= gm; Y[2, 2] += gm
    return np.linalg.solve(Y, I)[4]

freqs = [10, 30, 70, 200, 1e3, 1e4, 1e5, 3e5, 1e6, 3e6]
print("   f (Hz)    |gain|   gain (dB)   phase (deg)")
for f in freqs:
    g = gain(f)
    print(f"{f:9.0f}  {abs(g):8.1f}   {20 * math.log10(abs(g)):8.1f}   {math.degrees(np.angle(g)):9.1f}")

mid = abs(gain(1e4))
def corner(lo, hi):                               # bisection on a log scale for |gain| = mid / sqrt 2
    for _ in range(60):
        m = math.sqrt(lo * hi)
        if (abs(gain(m)) > mid / math.sqrt(2)) == (abs(gain(lo)) > mid / math.sqrt(2)):
            lo = m
        else:
            hi = m
    return m
print(f"\nmidband gain {mid:.1f}; -3 dB at {corner(1, 1e4):.1f} Hz and {corner(1e4, 1e8) / 1e3:.0f} kHz")

rload = 1 / (1 / RC + 1 / ro + 1 / RL)
cmiller = CMU * (1 + gm * rload)
rsig = 1 / (1 / RS + 1 / RTH + 1 / rpi)
print(f"Miller estimate: C_mu x (1 + gm R'L) = {cmiller * 1e12:.0f} pF, plus C_pi {CPI * 1e12:.1f} pF, "
      f"seen through {rsig:.0f} ohm -> f_H = {1 / (2 * math.pi * rsig * (cmiller + CPI)) / 1e3:.0f} kHz")
```

From the source the midband gain is 117.6 (the 163 of the transistor stage, reduced because the 1 kΩ source resistance and the 2.6 kΩ input resistance form a divider). The band runs from 70.3 Hz to 314 kHz, and the one-line Miller estimate, 321 kHz, is within 3%. Notice the phase: −180° in midband (the inversion), moving towards −90° at each end as each pole adds or removes up to 90°.

The agreement is within about 1%. The small differences come from things the hand analysis ignored: SPICE includes the Early effect in the bias point (raising $I_C$ slightly) and its Gummel–Poon model makes $\beta$ itself depend a little on $V_{CE}$. This is the professional rhythm: estimate by hand, so you know what answer to expect and which component matters, then simulate to get the details right.

The Miller effect has well-known cures. A **cascode** stacks a common-base transistor on top of the CE stage, so the first transistor's collector sees only the low input resistance of the second and has almost no voltage gain to multiply $C_\mu$. Followers have no Miller multiplication at all. And the transistor's own speed limit is its **transition frequency** $f_T = g_m / (2\pi(C_\pi + C_\mu))$, the frequency at which its current gain falls to one: 240 MHz for our model transistor, and hundreds of gigahertz for the silicon–germanium transistors in radio chips.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The Miller capacitance is a midband approximation: it assumes the gain from base to collector is still $-g_m R'_L$ at the frequency of interest, which stops being true near the pole it predicts. It worked to 3% here because the input pole is far below the output one. In wideband amplifiers the poles interact, and you need the full analysis, or the simulator.

</Narrator>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Amplifiers in professional practice">

Discrete single-transistor amplifiers like this one are now rare outside teaching, audio enthusiasm and radio-frequency front ends; most products use integrated operational amplifiers and dedicated amplifier chips. But every one of those chips is built from the stages in this lesson. An analog integrated-circuit designer sizes transistors for a chosen **$g_m/I_D$** (transconductance per unit of current, a measure of how efficiently a transistor turns bias current into gain), biases them with **current mirrors**, uses differential pairs everywhere for their rejection of supply and substrate noise, adds cascodes for gain, and simulates across process, voltage and temperature **corners** with the foundry's BSIM models before committing to silicon.

You can now do this yourself with open tools: ngspice, the open-source process design kits SkyWater SKY130 (from SkyWater and Google, United States), GlobalFoundries GF180MCU, and IHP SG13G2 (a silicon–germanium process from Germany's IHP), and community programmes that fabricate small student designs. <LessonLink slug="chip-design">How chips are designed</LessonLink> describes the full flow.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"More gain is always better."** Gain beyond what the signal needs only amplifies noise and brings the stage closer to clipping, and high-gain stages are the most sensitive to temperature, part variation and the Miller effect. Engineers usually choose modest, accurate gain, and get it with feedback.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$g_m$ is a property of the transistor type, like β on the datasheet."** A BJT's $g_m$ is set by its bias current, $I_C/V_T$, and a MOSFET's by its current and overdrive. Change the bias and you change the gain.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The small-signal model works for any signal size."** It is a tangent-line approximation, accurate only while $|v_{be}|$ stays well below $V_T$ (or $|v_{gs}|$ well below the overdrive). Larger signals distort, as the differential pair's tanh curve showed.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A follower is useless because its gain is one."** Its power gain and current gain are large. It lets a weak, high-resistance source drive a heavy load, which in the example multiplied the signal at the load by about 65.

</Misconception>
