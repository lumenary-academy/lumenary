<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What are we paying for?">

Every month or so, families all over the world receive an electricity bill, or top up a prepaid meter with a token or a mobile-money payment. The bill doesn't charge for the number of devices you own, or for how many times you pressed a switch. It charges for **energy**, counted in a unit with a strange name: the **kilowatt-hour**.

In this lesson you will find out what that unit means, how much energy everyday devices really use, why replacing an old light bulb with an LED saves so much, and how a battery packs energy into a small box of chemicals. You will also see why a phone gets warm when it charges, and how to keep batteries safe.

</Section>

<Section id="power" kind="definitions" title="Energy and power">

**Energy** is what makes things happen: it lights a room, heats water, turns a fan and makes sound. It is measured in **joules** (J). Energy is never created or destroyed; it only changes from one form to another. An electric kettle turns electrical energy into heat, a fan into movement (and a little heat and sound), a lamp into light (and heat).

**Power** is about *speed*: how fast the energy is being changed from one form into another.

<Definition term="power">

**Power** is the amount of energy transferred each second. It is measured in **watts** (W): one watt is one joule per second. A 60 W lamp turns 60 joules of electrical energy into light and heat every second. Large powers are written in **kilowatts**: 1 kW = 1000 W.

</Definition>

There is a lovely way to work out electrical power. Voltage tells you how many joules each coulomb carries, and current tells you how many coulombs arrive each second. Multiply them, and you get the number of joules arriving each second:

<Definition term="electrical power, P = VI">

The power delivered to a component is its voltage multiplied by the current through it:

$$
P = V \times I,
$$

with $P$ in watts, $V$ in volts and $I$ in amperes.

</Definition>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Watch what the units do: (joules per coulomb) × (coulombs per second) = joules per second. The coulombs cancel, exactly as in a fraction. When the units of a formula work out like this, it is a good sign the formula makes sense.

</Narrator>

<Example title="Power from voltage and current">

- A red LED with 2 V across it and 20 mA through it: $P = 2 \times 0.02 = 0.04$ W.
- A phone charger giving 5 V at 2 A: $P = 5 \times 2 = 10$ W.
- An electric kettle in a country with 230 V mains, drawing 13 A: $P = 230 \times 13 = 2990$ W, or about 3 kW. That is about 75 000 times the power of the LED.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

For a resistor you can combine $P = VI$ with Ohm's law, $V = IR$, to get $P = I \times I \times R$. The current appears twice, so doubling the current through a wire makes it produce *four* times as much heat. That is why thin wires carrying big currents get dangerously hot, and why short circuits are so dangerous.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="kwh" kind="examples" title="Kilowatt-hours and the electricity bill">

Joules are tiny for household use: a kettle uses hundreds of thousands of them to boil one jug of water. So electricity companies use a much bigger unit of energy.

<Definition term="kilowatt-hour">

A **kilowatt-hour** (kWh) is the energy used by a device with a power of 1 kilowatt running for 1 hour. Since 1 kW is 1000 joules per second and an hour has 3600 seconds, 1 kWh = 3 600 000 J. To find the energy a device uses, in kWh:

$$
\text{energy (kWh)} = \text{power (kW)} \times \text{time (hours)}.
$$

</Definition>

The electricity meter in a home counts kilowatt-hours, and the bill charges a price for each one. In India, the United Kingdom and many other countries, people often call a kilowatt-hour a "unit" of electricity.

Prices vary a lot between countries, suppliers and even times of day, and they change often, so the prices in these examples are round numbers chosen to make the arithmetic easy, not today's tariffs.

<Example title="A kettle in Pune">

Priya's family boils water in a 2000 W kettle for about 15 minutes a day in total. That is 2 kW for a quarter of an hour, or $2 \times 0.25 = 0.5$ kWh a day, and 15 kWh in 30 days. At an example price of INR 8 per kWh, that costs $15 \times 8 =$ INR 120 a month.

</Example>

<Example title="A light in Manchester and a television in Mombasa">

A 9 W LED lamp on for 5 hours a day uses $0.009 \times 5 = 0.045$ kWh a day, or 1.35 kWh in 30 days. At GBP 0.25 per kWh, that is about GBP 0.34 for the whole month.

An 80 W television on for 4 hours a day uses $0.08 \times 4 = 0.32$ kWh a day, or 9.6 kWh in 30 days. At KES 25 per kWh, that costs KES 240.

</Example>

<Section id="calculator" kind="computation" title="A household energy calculator">

Here is a small program that adds up a day's electricity for an imaginary household. Each line of the list is a device: its name, its power in watts and how many hours a day it runs. The program turns watts × hours into kilowatt-hours (dividing by 1000), adds everything up and works out the cost. Press **Run**, then edit the list to match your own home, and change the price and currency to your own.

```python run
# A household energy calculator
# Each device: (name, power in watts, hours used per day)
devices = [
    ("6 LED bulbs, 9 W each", 54, 5),
    ("refrigerator (average)", 40, 24),
    ("ceiling fan", 60, 8),
    ("television", 80, 3),
    ("laptop", 45, 4),
    ("phone charging", 10, 2),
    ("Wi-Fi router", 8, 24),
    ("washing machine", 500, 0.5),
    ("electric kettle", 2000, 0.25),
]
price = 8            # an example price: INR 8 per kWh
currency = "INR"
days = 30

print(f"{'device':26}{'watts':>7}{'hours':>7}{'kWh/day':>9}")
total = 0
for name, watts, hours in devices:
    kwh = watts * hours / 1000          # watt-hours -> kilowatt-hours
    total += kwh
    print(f"{name:26}{watts:>7}{hours:>7}{kwh:>9.2f}")

print()
print(f"Energy per day:   {total:.2f} kWh")
print(f"Energy in {days} days: {total * days:.1f} kWh")
print(f"Cost in {days} days:   {currency} {total * days * price:.0f}  (at {currency} {price} per kWh)")

# What if the six LED bulbs were old 60 W filament bulbs instead?
extra_kwh = 6 * (60 - 9) * 5 / 1000 * days
print()
print(f"Six 60 W filament bulbs instead: {extra_kwh:.1f} kWh more, "
      f"costing {currency} {extra_kwh * price:.0f} more in {days} days")
```

The fridge is listed at 40 W because that is its *average*: its motor runs at about 150 W, but only some of the time.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Look at the fridge and the kettle. The kettle's power is fifty times bigger, yet the fridge uses more energy in a day: 0.96 kWh against 0.50 kWh, because it never stops. Energy is power multiplied by *time*. And the last line shows why lighting changed so fast: six old filament bulbs would add 45.9 kWh a month, about INR 367 at this price.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="leds" kind="human" title="Why LEDs beat filament bulbs">

An old-fashioned **filament bulb** makes light by heating a thin coil of tungsten wire until it glows white-hot, at about 2500 °C. Glowing because you are hot is a very wasteful way to make light: most of the energy comes out as invisible infrared (heat), and only a few per cent as visible light. That is why a filament bulb that has been on for a while is hot enough to burn your fingers.

An **LED** (light-emitting diode) is a tiny chip of semiconductor. When electrons cross from one layer of it to another, they give up energy directly as light, without having to make anything white-hot. An LED lamp gives the same amount of light as a 60 W filament bulb using only about 8 to 10 W, and lasts many times longer: typically 15 000 hours or more against about 1000.

The amount of light a lamp gives is measured in **lumens**, and the package of an LED lamp usually tells you both its lumens and its watts. Comparing lumens, not watts, is the honest way to compare lamps.

Because lighting is used by billions of people every evening, the switch to LEDs has been one of the world's biggest energy savings. Many countries have phased out most filament bulbs, and India's UJALA programme, launched in 2015, distributed hundreds of millions of LED bulbs to households at low prices.

</Section>

<Section id="batteries" kind="intuition" title="How a battery stores energy">

A battery stores energy as **chemical energy**, in the way its materials are arranged. Every battery cell has three parts:

- two different materials called **electrodes**, one for each terminal;
- between them, an **electrolyte**, a paste or liquid through which **ions** (atoms that carry a charge) can move, but electrons cannot.

One electrode material "wants" to give away electrons and the other "wants" to take them. But the electrolyte won't let electrons through, so the reaction can only happen when you connect the two terminals with a wire outside. Then electrons flow out of the negative electrode, through your circuit (lighting the bulb on the way), and into the positive electrode, while ions move through the electrolyte inside to keep everything balanced. When one of the materials is used up, the battery is flat.

The voltage of a cell depends on its chemistry, not its size: a tiny AAA cell and a big D cell are both 1.5 V. The big one simply holds more of the chemicals, so it lasts longer.

| Battery | Chemistry | Voltage per cell | Rechargeable? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AA, AAA (alkaline) | zinc and manganese dioxide | 1.5 V | No |
| AA, AAA (NiMH) | nickel–metal hydride | 1.2 V | Yes |
| Phone, laptop, electric car | lithium-ion | about 3.2–3.9 V, depending on the exact materials | Yes |
| Car starter battery | lead and lead dioxide in acid | 2 V (six cells make 12 V) | Yes |

A **rechargeable** battery uses a reaction that can be run backwards. The charger pushes current into the battery the "wrong" way, which pushes the chemicals back to their starting arrangement, ready to be used again. In a lithium-ion battery, charging moves lithium ions from the positive electrode into the graphite of the negative one, and using the battery lets them flow back.

### Capacity: mAh and Wh

A battery label often says something like "5000 mAh". The **milliampere-hour** is an amount of *charge*: 1 mAh is the charge that flows when a current of 1 mA runs for one hour. A 5000 mAh battery could supply 5000 mA (5 A) for one hour, or 500 mA for ten hours, and so on.

But charge alone doesn't tell you the energy: you also need the voltage. The **watt-hour** (Wh) measures energy directly:

$$
\text{energy (Wh)} = \text{voltage (V)} \times \text{capacity (Ah)}.
$$

- A phone battery of 5000 mAh at 3.85 V stores $3.85 \times 5 = 19.25$ Wh.
- An alkaline AA cell gives roughly 2500 mAh at 1.5 V, about 3.75 Wh, when used slowly (much less if you draw a big current).
- A power bank labelled 10 000 mAh, with lithium cells at 3.7 V, stores $3.7 \times 10 = 37$ Wh.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice the hidden assumption in a power bank's label: its 10 000 mAh are counted at the cells' own 3.7 V, but it delivers 5 V through its USB port. The energy is what matters, so at 5 V the most it could give is $37 \div 5 = 7.4$ Ah, and the conversion wastes some more as heat. If about 85% of the 37 Wh reaches the phone, that is enough to charge the 19.25 Wh phone battery only about 1.6 times, not twice.

</Narrator>

<Section id="warm-and-safe" kind="human" title="Why phones get warm, and caring for batteries">

No energy conversion is perfect. Some energy always ends up as heat: in the battery's own small internal resistance, in the charging circuits that convert one voltage to another, and in the processor when it works hard, for example during a game or a video call. Fast charging pushes more current, and since heat in a resistance grows with the current *times itself* (Gideon's $I \times I \times R$), it makes more heat. That is why phones get warm, and why a phone will slow its charging down, or pause it, when it gets too hot.

Lithium-ion batteries pack a lot of energy into a small space, so they deserve respect. Look after them like this:

- **Use the right charger**: the one that came with the device, or a certified one from a reputable maker.
- **Let heat escape**: don't charge a phone or tablet under a pillow or on a bed, and never leave one in direct sun or on the dashboard of a hot car.
- **Watch for warning signs.** If a battery swells, gets very hot, hisses or smells strange, stop using it and stop charging it, move away from it, and tell an adult. Never puncture, crush or bend a lithium battery.
- **Never put batteries in the household rubbish bin.** Crushed batteries start fires in rubbish trucks and recycling plants. Take them to a battery collection point; many shops and supermarkets have one.
- **Keep button batteries away from small children.** A swallowed coin-shaped battery can cause serious internal burns within hours. If you think a child has swallowed one, get medical help immediately.
- **Don't mix old and new batteries**, or different types, in one device, and never try to recharge a battery that isn't rechargeable.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Watts measure how much electricity I've used."** Watts measure *power*, how fast energy is being used right now. The energy you pay for is power multiplied by time, in kilowatt-hours. A 2000 W kettle used for a few minutes can cost less than a 10 W router left on all month.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A battery with more mAh always stores more energy."** Only if the voltages are the same. A 3000 mAh lithium-ion cell at 3.7 V stores about 11 Wh; a 3000 mAh NiMH cell at 1.2 V stores only about 3.6 Wh.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A device that is switched off uses no electricity."** Many devices still draw a little power on standby, to keep a clock, a remote-control receiver or a network connection alive. A device drawing 2 W on standby all year uses about 17.5 kWh: small for one device, but it adds up across a whole home.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A brighter lamp always has more watts."** Watts tell you the power going *in*, not the light coming out. A 9 W LED lamp can be as bright as a 60 W filament bulb. Compare lumens.

</Misconception>
