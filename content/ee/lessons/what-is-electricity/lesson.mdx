<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Something you use all day and never see">

Think about the first ten minutes of your day. Perhaps an alarm on a phone woke you, a light came on at the press of a switch, and somebody boiled water in an electric kettle. In cities from Lagos to Lima to Mumbai, electric trains were already carrying people to work.

All of these run on **electricity**, yet you have never actually *seen* electricity. You see what it does: light, heat, movement, sound, pictures. The thing itself is invisible.

This lesson answers a simple question: **what is actually moving in that wire, and what pushes it?** By the end you will know the three words engineers use every day, *charge*, *current* and *voltage*, and the units that measure them. You will also know why lightning happens, and how to stay safe when it does.

</Section>

<Section id="charge" kind="intuition" title="Charge: what electricity is made of">

Everything around you is made of **atoms**, far too small to see. Each atom has a tiny, heavy centre called the **nucleus**, made of **protons** and **neutrons**, and around it a cloud of much lighter **electrons**.

Protons and electrons have a property called **electric charge**. Protons carry a *positive* charge and electrons a *negative* charge of exactly the same size. Neutrons, as their name suggests, are neutral: no charge at all. An ordinary atom has the same number of protons and electrons, so the pluses and minuses cancel and the atom as a whole is neutral.

<Definition term="electric charge">

**Electric charge** is a property of particles that makes them push or pull on each other. It comes in two kinds, positive and negative: like charges **repel**, unlike charges **attract**. Charge is measured in **coulombs** (symbol C). One electron carries a charge of about $-0.000\,000\,000\,000\,000\,000\,16$ coulombs, written $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C, so a single coulomb is the charge of about six billion billion electrons.

</Definition>

Charge is never created or destroyed. It can only be moved from one place to another. When something becomes "charged", electrons have moved onto it (making it negative) or off it (leaving it positive).

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here is the question that drives this whole lesson: if every atom is neutral, where do all the pushes and pulls of electricity come from? The answer is that electrons can be *moved*. Separate some negative charge from its positive partners, and the two sides pull hard to get back together. Electricity is what happens when they try.

</Narrator>

### Static electricity

Rub a balloon on your hair or on a woollen jumper, and the balloon will stick to a wall and make your hair stand on end. Rubbing moves electrons: usually some electrons leave your hair and end up on the balloon. Now the balloon has extra electrons (it is negative) and your hair has lost some (it is positive). Each hair is positive, and like charges repel, so the hairs push away from each other and stand up.

This is **static electricity**: charge that has been moved and then stays put ("static" means standing still). It is made of exactly the same charges as the electricity in wires. You feel it as a small shock when you walk across a carpet on a dry day and then reach for a metal door handle: a spark jumps as the extra charge on you rushes away. That spark can be several thousand volts (you will meet volts in a moment) and still be harmless, because only a tiny amount of charge flows and it is all over in less than a millionth of a second.

### Conductors and insulators

Why did the spark jump to the *metal* handle, and not to a wooden door? In a metal, each atom's outermost electrons are only loosely held, and they wander freely through the whole piece, like a crowd drifting about a big hall. In plastic, rubber, glass or dry wood, every electron is held tightly by its own atom.

<Definition term="conductor and insulator">

A **conductor** is a material that lets charge flow through it easily, because it has charges that are free to move. Metals such as copper, aluminium and silver are good conductors, and so is graphite, the "lead" in a pencil. An **insulator** is a material that hardly lets charge flow at all: plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood, dry air.

</Definition>

That is why cables are copper wire wrapped in plastic: the copper carries the current, and the plastic keeps it away from your fingers. In between sit **semiconductors** such as silicon, whose ability to conduct can be controlled; every computer chip is built from them, and you will meet them in <LessonLink slug="semiconductors-and-diodes">Semiconductors and diodes</LessonLink>.

Watch out for water. Pure water is a poor conductor, but rain, tap water and sweat contain dissolved salts and conduct, and so does your body. That is why electricity can hurt people, and why you must never handle electrical things with wet hands.

</Section>

<Section id="current-and-voltage" kind="definitions" title="Current and voltage">

Static electricity is charge sitting still. Most of the electricity we use is charge on the move.

### Current: how much charge flows

Connect a battery to a small bulb with two wires and the bulb lights: electrons are flowing round the loop. We measure this flow as a **current**.

<Definition term="current">

**Current** is the amount of charge flowing past a point each second. It is measured in **amperes**, or **amps** (symbol A): one ampere is one coulomb per second. Written as a formula, with $I$ for current, $Q$ for charge and $t$ for time,

$$
I = \frac{Q}{t}.
$$

</Definition>

A small LED uses about 0.02 A, which engineers write as **20 mA** (milliamperes: thousandths of an amp). A phone on its charger might take 1 or 2 A, and an electric kettle about 10 A.

Here is something surprising: current is not used up as it goes round. Exactly as much charge flows *back* into the battery as flows out of it. What the bulb uses is the *energy* the charge carries, which brings us to voltage.

### Voltage: how hard the charge is pushed

A battery is a kind of pump for charge. Inside it, a chemical reaction pushes electrons out of its negative terminal and pulls them in at its positive terminal. Each bit of charge picks up energy in the battery and gives it up in the bulb, as light and heat.

<Definition term="voltage">

**Voltage** between two points is the energy each coulomb of charge gains or loses in moving from one point to the other. It is measured in **volts** (symbol V): one volt means one **joule** of energy for every coulomb. (A joule is a small amount of energy: about what it takes to lift an apple one metre.)

</Definition>

So a 1.5 V battery gives every coulomb that passes through it 1.5 joules to deliver round the circuit, and a 9 V battery gives every coulomb 9 joules. More voltage means a harder push, and usually a larger current.

| Source | Voltage | Safe to experiment with? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AA or AAA battery | 1.5 V | Yes |
| Phone battery (inside the phone) | about 3.7 V | Leave it inside the phone |
| USB port or power bank | 5 V | Yes, when used as designed |
| 9 V battery | 9 V | Yes |
| Car battery | 12 V | No: it can deliver enormous currents |
| Household sockets ("mains") | 220–240 V in most of Europe, Africa, Asia and Australia; 120 V in North America; 100 V in Japan | **Never.** Mains electricity can kill |
| Overhead power lines | thousands to hundreds of thousands of volts | **Never** go near them |

<Narrator who="gideon">

A careful point about direction. Long before anyone knew electrons existed, scientists agreed to say that current flows from the positive terminal round to the negative one. Electrons, being negative, actually drift the other way. Engineers still draw current arrows from + to −, called **conventional current**, and it gives the right answers every time: negative charge moving left has the same effect as positive charge moving right.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="water" kind="intuition" title="The water analogy, and where it breaks">

Electricity is invisible, so it helps to picture something we *can* see.

<Analogy>

Imagine water in a closed loop of pipe. A pump pushes the water round, and somewhere along the loop the moving water turns a little water wheel.

- The **pump** is the battery.
- The **pressure difference** the pump makes is the **voltage**.
- The **amount of water flowing per second** is the **current**.
- The **water wheel**, where the water's energy is put to use, is the **bulb**.

Notice that the water is not used up: it goes round and round. Only its energy is handed over at the wheel.

</Analogy>

The analogy is good, and engineers use it all the time. But every analogy breaks somewhere, and it is worth knowing where.

- **Cut a pipe and water pours out. Cut a wire and nothing pours out.** Charge needs a conductor to flow through; at a cut end it simply stops, and the whole loop stops with it. (Air is an insulator, at least until the voltage is enormous, as you will see with lightning.)
- **The electrons themselves crawl.** In a typical wire, each electron drifts along at well under a millimetre per second. Yet a lamp lights the instant you press the switch, because the wire is already full of free electrons, and the *push* travels along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light. (Here the water picture actually works, if the pipe was full of water to begin with.)
- **Electric currents do things water never does.** A current makes a magnetic field around its wire, which is how motors and loudspeakers work. You will see this in <LessonLink slug="magnetism-and-motors">Magnets, motors and generators</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="counting" kind="computation" title="Counting electrons">

Engineers rarely count individual electrons, because there are so many. The number flowing past a point each second is the current divided by the charge of one electron. Press **Run** to work it out for some everyday currents.

```python run
# How many electrons pass a point in a wire every second?
e = 1.602176634e-19        # the charge of one electron, in coulombs

currents = [
    ("a small LED", 0.02),
    ("a phone charging", 1.5),
    ("an electric kettle", 10),
    ("a typical lightning stroke", 30000),
]

for name, amps in currents:
    electrons_per_second = amps / e
    print(f"{name:28} {amps:>8} A  ->  {electrons_per_second:.2e} electrons per second")

# If you counted the electrons in ONE coulomb, one per second, how long would it take?
electrons_in_one_coulomb = 1 / e
seconds_per_year = 365.25 * 24 * 60 * 60
years = electrons_in_one_coulomb / seconds_per_year
print()
print(f"Electrons in one coulomb: {electrons_in_one_coulomb:.3e}")
print(f"Counting them one per second would take about {years:.1e} years")

# How fast does each electron actually move? (copper wire, 1 square millimetre, 1 ampere)
free_electrons_per_m3 = 8.5e28      # free electrons in a cubic metre of copper
area_m2 = 1e-6                      # 1 mm^2 = 0.000001 m^2
drift_speed = 1 / (free_electrons_per_m3 * e * area_m2)
print(f"Drift speed at 1 A: {drift_speed * 1000:.3f} mm per second")
print(f"Time to drift 1 metre: {1 / drift_speed / 3600:.1f} hours")
```

A number like `1.25e+17` is Python's way of writing $1.25 \times 10^{17}$: 125 followed by 15 zeros. So even a small LED has more than a hundred thousand million million electrons passing through it every second.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Look at the last lines of the output. At one amp, each electron in a 1 mm² copper wire drifts about 0.073 mm per second, so it would need about 3.8 hours to travel one metre. And counting the electrons in just one coulomb, one per second, would take about 200 billion years, which is more than ten times the age of the universe. Edit the code and try a current of your own.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="lightning" kind="examples" title="Lightning: static electricity on a giant scale">

A thunderstorm is a giant version of the balloon on your hair. Inside a tall storm cloud, strong winds carry tiny ice crystals upwards while heavier pellets of soft hail fall through them, and as they collide, electrons are rubbed from one to the other. The light, positive ice crystals are carried to the top of the cloud; the heavier, negative pellets gather lower down. The strongly negative base of the cloud pulls positive charge together in the ground beneath it.

Air is an insulator, but every insulator has a limit. When the voltage between the cloud and the ground climbs high enough, the air breaks down and becomes a path for current: a lightning stroke. According to the US National Weather Service, a typical flash carries about 30 000 amperes, driven by hundreds of millions of volts, and heats the air in its path to nearly 30 000 °C. That air expands so violently that it makes a boom: **thunder**.

<Example title="How far away is the storm?">

Light from the flash reaches you almost instantly, but sound travels about 343 metres per second, so it needs roughly 3 seconds per kilometre. Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder, then divide by 3.

If Tomás in Mexico City counts 12 seconds, the lightning was about $12 \div 3 = 4$ kilometres away. If he counts 3 seconds, it was only about a kilometre away.

</Example>

### Staying safe

Lightning strikes many thousands of times every hour somewhere on Earth, and it is dangerous. The rule is simple: **if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck, so go indoors.**

- The safest place is inside a proper building, the kind with electrical wiring and water pipes, or inside a car or bus with a metal roof and the windows shut.
- Never shelter under a tree, especially one standing alone, and get out of open fields, off hilltops and out of the water (swimming pools, lakes and the sea).
- Small open shelters, such as huts, tents and bus stops, do **not** protect you.
- Indoors, keep away from baths and showers and from anything plugged into the mains until the storm has passed.
- Wait about half an hour after the last thunder before you go back outside.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="The people behind the units">

People noticed electricity long before they understood it. The Greek thinker Thales of Miletus is said to have noticed, around 600 BCE, that amber rubbed with fur attracts bits of straw. The Greek word for amber, *ēlektron*, gave us the words "electricity" and "electron".

In the 1740s and 1750s, the American printer and scientist **Benjamin Franklin** proposed that objects can have too much or too little of a single electric "fluid", and named the two states *positive* and *negative*. He argued that lightning is electricity, and invented the lightning rod, which gives lightning a safe path to the ground and still protects buildings today. His famous experiment with a kite in a thunderstorm was deadly dangerous: in 1753 the scientist Georg Wilhelm Richmann was killed in St Petersburg while measuring electricity from a storm. Never try anything like it.

In 1800 the Italian physicist **Alessandro Volta** built the first battery, the "voltaic pile": a stack of zinc and copper discs separated by cloth soaked in salt water, which gave the first steady current. In the 1820s the French mathematician **André-Marie Ampère** measured how currents in wires push and pull on each other, turning electricity into a mathematical science.

The scientists who built the subject are remembered in its units. When a unit is named after a person, its full name is written with a small letter (volt, ampere) but its symbol with a capital (V, A).

| Unit | Symbol | Measures | Named after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| coulomb | C | charge | Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, France |
| ampere | A | current | André-Marie Ampère, France |
| volt | V | voltage | Alessandro Volta, Italy |
| ohm | Ω | resistance | Georg Simon Ohm, Germany |
| watt | W | power | James Watt, Scotland |
| joule | J | energy | James Prescott Joule, England |
| hertz | Hz | how often something repeats | Heinrich Hertz, Germany |
| tesla | T | strength of a magnetic field | Nikola Tesla, born in today's Croatia, worked in the USA |

You will meet the ohm in the next lesson, <LessonLink slug="circuits-and-ohms-law">Circuits and Ohm's law</LessonLink>, and the watt in the one after.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A battery is full of electricity that gets used up in the bulb."** The charge is not used up: as much flows back into the battery as flows out. What runs down is the battery's store of *chemical energy*, which it hands to the charges to deliver to the bulb.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Electrons race from the battery to the bulb at the speed of light."** The electrons drift very slowly, a fraction of a millimetre per second. What travels fast is the push, passed along through a wire that is already full of electrons.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Rubber-soled shoes or a car's rubber tyres protect you from lightning."** Lightning has just crossed kilometres of air, which is a far better insulator than a few centimetres of rubber. A car protects you because of its *metal* body, which carries the current round the outside.

</Misconception>

</Section>
