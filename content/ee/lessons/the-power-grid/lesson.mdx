<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="The largest machine">

Flip a switch in Lisbon and a power station in Poland feels it. The Continental European grid is one machine, tens of thousands of kilometres of transmission lines and thousands of generators, all turning in lockstep at 50 Hz, from Portugal to Turkey and, since February 2025, the Baltic states. North America has its Eastern, Western, Texas and Québec interconnections; India runs a single national synchronous grid; China operates several very large regional grids tied together by direct-current links. Each is, in a precise sense, a single machine, because the rotors of every synchronous generator in it are coupled through the network and turn at the same electrical frequency.

That machine has a property no factory machine has: it has almost no storage of its own. At every instant, the power generated must equal the power consumed plus the losses, and nobody controls the consumption. This lesson is about how engineers keep that balance and keep the machine intact: why power travels at hundreds of kilovolts, what happens in the seconds after a power station suddenly trips, how faults are found and cleared in a tenth of a second, why some links are DC, how markets decide which plant runs, and what the great blackouts taught.

<Note>

**Safety.** Overhead lines, substations and distribution equipment carry voltages that can kill at a distance, without contact, by arcing. Never climb a pylon or a substation fence, never fly a kite or a drone near overhead lines, and keep well away from any fallen line: report it to the network operator or the emergency services. Grid equipment is operated only by trained, authorised staff under strict switching and isolation procedures.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="structure" kind="definitions" title="Generation, transmission and distribution">

A grid has three layers, each at its own range of voltages.

- **Generation.** Large synchronous generators make three-phase AC at 10 to 25 kV, and step-up transformers raise it at once to transmission voltage. Wind and solar farms and batteries connect through inverters, often at lower voltages, and rooftop solar connects directly to the distribution network.
- **Transmission.** A meshed network of overhead lines and cables at high voltage (typically 220 to 500 kV, up to 765 kV in several countries and 1000 kV in China) carries bulk power over hundreds of kilometres. Its mesh means that the loss of any one line or generator leaves another path, the **N − 1 criterion** every transmission operator plans to.
- **Distribution.** At substations, transformers step the voltage down to sub-transmission (66 to 132 kV) and then medium voltage (from about 4 to 35 kV, commonly 11 to 33 kV), carried along feeders to local transformers that supply the low-voltage network of streets and buildings: 230/400 V in most of the world, 120/240 V split-phase in North America. Distribution networks are mostly radial, a tree rather than a mesh.

Engineers draw all this as a **one-line diagram**: one line stands for the three phases of each circuit, by the per-phase method of <LessonLink slug="three-phase-power">AC power and three-phase systems</LessonLink>, with symbols for generators, transformers, busbars and circuit breakers.

### Why transmit at high voltage?

A balanced three-phase line delivering power $P$ at line voltage $V$ and power factor $\mathrm{pf}$ carries a current $I = P/(\sqrt3\,V\,\mathrm{pf})$ in each conductor, and loses $3I^2R$ in a resistance $R$ per phase:
$$P_{\text{loss}} = \frac{P^2 R}{V^2\,\mathrm{pf}^2}.$$
For given conductors, **losses fall as the square of the voltage**. Equivalently, for a given loss, the conductor needed falls as the square of the voltage. The program sends 1000 MW over 200 km both ways.

```python run
import numpy as np

P = 1000e6                    # send 1000 MW ...
length = 200e3                # ... over 200 km, three-phase AC at unity power factor
rho_al = 2.8e-8               # resistivity of aluminium conductor, ohm m
r_per_km = 0.03               # one fixed set of conductors: 0.03 ohm per km per phase
R = r_per_km * length / 1e3   # 6 ohm per phase

print("Same conductors (6 ohm per phase) at different voltages:")
print(" line kV   current A   loss MW   loss %")
for kV in [66, 132, 220, 400, 765]:
    I = P / (np.sqrt(3) * kV * 1e3)
    loss = 3 * I**2 * R
    note = "   (more than is sent: impossible in practice)" if loss > P else ""
    print(f"{kV:7d}   {I:9.0f}   {loss / 1e6:7.1f}   {100 * loss / P:6.1f}{note}")

print("\nAluminium needed to keep the loss at 2% (three conductors, 200 km):")
print(" line kV   R allowed (ohm)   cross-section (mm^2)   aluminium (tonnes)")
for kV in [132, 220, 400, 765]:
    V = kV * 1e3
    R_allowed = 0.02 * V**2 / P            # from loss = P^2 R / V^2 for a balanced three-phase line
    area = rho_al * length / R_allowed
    tonnes = 3 * area * length * 2700 / 1e3
    print(f"{kV:7d}   {R_allowed:15.2f}   {area * 1e6:20.0f}   {tonnes:18.0f}")
```

On the same 6 Ω conductors, 1000 MW at 132 kV would lose a third of itself; at 400 kV, 3.8%; at 765 kV, 1.0%. Turned the other way, keeping the loss to 2% at 132 kV would take about 26,000 tonnes of aluminium, against 2,835 tonnes at 400 kV. The limits on raising the voltage are insulation (longer insulator strings, wider clearances, taller towers) and **corona**, the discharge into the air around a conductor whose surface field is too strong, which is why high-voltage lines use **bundles** of two to eight conductors per phase, spreading the field.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Check the square law in the second table: 400 kV is 3.03 times 132 kV, and the aluminium falls from 26,033 to 2,835 tonnes, a factor of 9.2, which is $3.03^2$. At 765 kV it is 775 tonnes. Tower steel, insulators and land are a large part of a line's cost too, but the conductor alone makes the case for high voltage.

</Narrator>

Losses are not the only limit. An AC line also has series inductance and capacitance to ground, so it absorbs or generates reactive power depending on its loading, and the voltage along it shifts with the load. A long AC line's capacity is often limited not by heating but by voltage and stability, which is one reason the longest links are DC.

</Section>

<Section id="frequency" kind="intuition" title="Frequency, inertia and the swing equation">

Every synchronous generator's rotor stores kinetic energy, $\tfrac12 J\omega^2$. Engineers express it through the **inertia constant** $H$, the stored energy at rated speed divided by the machine's rating: typically 2 to 9 seconds for large generators. When the electrical power a generator delivers, $P_e$, differs from the mechanical power its turbine supplies, $P_m$, the difference comes out of (or goes into) that store, and the rotor slows down (or speeds up). Linearised around rated frequency $f_0$, and in per-unit on the machine's rating, this is the **swing equation**:
$$\frac{2H}{f_0}\,\frac{d\,\Delta f}{dt} = P_m - P_e.$$

Because all the machines of a synchronous area are coupled, a sudden imbalance anywhere is shared by all of them in proportion to their inertia, and the whole area's frequency moves together (apart from fast local swings between regions, which settle in a second or two). So the same equation holds for a whole area, with $H$ now the inertia of everything spinning in synchronism, $S$ the total rating online, and two further terms: some demand, notably motors, falls when the frequency falls (**load relief**, about 1% to 2% of demand per 1% of frequency), and governors raise generation in response.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Frequency is how a grid measures its own energy balance. No operator can see total demand directly, but every generator's speed feels the imbalance at once, so a frequency below 50 Hz *means* "demand exceeds supply", everywhere in the area at the same time. That is why frequency can be the signal for an automatic response by thousands of machines that never talk to each other.

</Narrator>

Right after a generator trips, nothing else has yet responded, so the initial **rate of change of frequency** (RoCoF) is set by the inertia alone:
$$\left.\frac{d\,\Delta f}{dt}\right|_{t = 0^+} = -\frac{\Delta P_{\text{trip}}}{2HS}\,f_0.$$
Then reserves respond, the fall slows, and the frequency reaches its lowest point, the **nadir**, before recovering. Operators must keep the nadir above the threshold where **under-frequency load shedding** starts automatically to disconnect blocks of customers (from 49.0 Hz in Continental Europe), and the RoCoF below what generators' protection tolerates.

The simulation lumps a 30 GW synchronous area into one machine, trips a 1320 MW generating unit at $t = 0$, and lets governors deploy 1500 MW of primary reserve with a time constant of 6 s. It compares a system with plenty of inertia ($H = 5$ s), one with half as much, as happens when many synchronous plants are replaced by inverter-connected wind, solar and batteries ($H = 2.5$ s), and the low-inertia system again with 800 MW of batteries providing **fast frequency response** (FFR), which they can deliver in a fraction of a second.

```python run
# One synchronous area lumped into a single machine. Powers in MW, frequency deviation df in Hz.
f0 = 50.0
S = 30_000.0             # MW of generation online
P_trip = 1320.0          # a large generating unit trips at t = 0
D = 1.0                  # load relief: demand falls 1% for each 1% fall in frequency
FCR = 1500.0             # primary (frequency containment) reserve held on governors, MW
FCR_full = 0.2           # ... fully deployed at a deviation of 200 mHz
T_fcr = 6.0              # how quickly governors and turbines deliver it, s
TIMES = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 15, 20, 30, 60)

def run(H, ffr_mw=0.0, t_end=60.0, dt=0.002):
    """The swing equation for the whole area:  2 H S / f0 * d(df)/dt = P_fcr + P_ffr - P_trip - D S / f0 * df."""
    M = 2 * H * S / f0                                     # inertia, in MW per (Hz/s)
    df = P_fcr = P_ffr = 0.0
    nadir, t_nadir, t_trigger, record = 0.0, 0.0, None, {}
    for n in range(int(round(t_end / dt)) + 1):
        t = n * dt
        if n % int(round(1 / dt)) == 0 and int(round(t)) in TIMES:
            record[int(round(t))] = f0 + df
        target = FCR * min(1.0, max(0.0, -df / FCR_full))   # droop response, capped at the reserve
        P_fcr += dt * (target - P_fcr) / T_fcr
        if ffr_mw > 0 and t_trigger is None and df < -0.2:   # batteries trigger at 49.8 Hz ...
            t_trigger = t
        if t_trigger is not None:                            # ... and reach full power 0.5 s later
            P_ffr = ffr_mw * min(1.0, (t - t_trigger) / 0.5)
        df += dt * (P_fcr + P_ffr - P_trip - D * S / f0 * df) / M
        if df < nadir:
            nadir, t_nadir = df, t
    return P_trip / M, f0 + nadir, t_nadir, record

cases = [("H = 5 s", 5.0, 0.0), ("H = 2.5 s", 2.5, 0.0), ("H = 2.5 s + 800 MW FFR", 2.5, 800.0)]
results = [run(H, ffr) for _, H, ffr in cases]
for (label, _, _), (rocof, f_min, t_min, _) in zip(cases, results):
    print(f"{label:24} initial fall {rocof:.2f} Hz/s, lowest {f_min:.3f} Hz at t = {t_min:.1f} s")
print("\n t (s) " + "".join(f"{label:>25}" for label, _, _ in cases))
for t in TIMES:
    print(f"{t:6d} " + "".join(f"{r[3][t]:25.3f}" for r in results))
```

Halving the inertia doubles the initial RoCoF, from 0.22 to 0.44 Hz/s, and drags the nadir from 49.401 Hz down to 49.149 Hz, uncomfortably close to load shedding at 49.0 Hz, because the governors' slow response arrives when the frequency has already fallen further. The batteries change the picture: they detect the fall as it crosses 49.8 Hz, reach full output half a second later, and hold the nadir at 49.674 Hz, better than the high-inertia system without them. (In this simple model they hold full output, so the frequency even overshoots 50 Hz briefly; real fast-response services are proportional to the deviation or taper as it recovers.) This is the engineering behind a large change in grid operation: as inverter-based resources replace synchronous machines, operators buy fast response from batteries, contract **synchronous condensers** for inertia, and increasingly require **grid-forming** inverters, the subject of <LessonLink slug="renewables-and-storage">Renewables, storage and electric vehicles</LessonLink>.

<Section id="balancing" kind="definitions" title="Balancing and reserves">

The simulation's final frequency is not 50 Hz. Primary response stops the fall but does not undo it, by design.

<Theorem name="Steady-state frequency deviation">

Suppose an area loses generation $\Delta P$ (MW), its demand falls by $D\,(S/f_0)\,\Delta f$ for a frequency deviation $\Delta f$, and its governors raise output in proportion to the deviation, by $K$ MW per Hz (their **droop**), without reaching their limits. Then the frequency settles at
$$\Delta f_{\text{ss}} = -\frac{\Delta P}{\beta}, \qquad \beta = D\,\frac{S}{f_0} + K,$$
where $\beta$, in MW/Hz, is the area's **frequency response characteristic**.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

In steady state $d\,\Delta f/dt = 0$, so the swing equation says total power balances: the extra generation $K\,|\Delta f|$ plus the load relief $D\,(S/f_0)\,|\Delta f|$ must equal the lost $\Delta P$. Solving for $\Delta f$ gives the result. Nothing in the steady state depends on $H$: inertia shapes the path, not the destination.

</Proof>

In the simulation, 1500 MW of reserve fully deployed at 0.2 Hz gives $K = 7500$ MW/Hz, load relief adds $1 \times 30\,000/50 = 600$ MW/Hz, so $\beta = 8100$ MW/Hz and $\Delta f_{\text{ss}} = -1320/8100 = -0.163$ Hz: the 49.837 Hz the program printed.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The theorem needs the governors to stay on their droop lines. If the loss exceeded the reserve held (here 1500 MW), the governors would saturate, $K$ would effectively fall to zero for any further deviation, and the frequency would keep falling until load shedding stopped it. This is why operators size primary reserve to the largest credible loss, the **reference incident**: 3000 MW for Continental Europe under ENTSO-E's rules, roughly the simultaneous loss of two of the largest generating units.

</Narrator>

Restoring 50 Hz and handing the work back from primary reserve is the job of slower layers, which differ in name from region to region but share a logic:

| Layer (ENTSO-E name) | Acts within | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inertia | instantly | stored kinetic energy of synchronous machines (and synthetic inertia from grid-forming inverters) |
| Fast frequency response | under a second or two | batteries and fast loads, triggered by frequency |
| Primary: frequency containment reserve (FCR) | seconds; full in 30 s in Continental Europe | governor droop on many units, shared across the whole synchronous area |
| Secondary: automatic frequency restoration reserve (aFRR) | 30 s to several minutes | automatic generation control in each control area returns frequency to nominal and exchanges to schedule |
| Tertiary: manual reserve (mFRR) and replacement reserves | minutes to an hour | operators dispatch units to free up the faster reserves |

Behind the reserves sits **scheduling**: forecasts of demand, wind and solar a day ahead and hour by hour, generation committed to match them, and markets or central dispatch to decide which plant runs.

</Section>

<Section id="protection" kind="examples" title="Protection and fault currents">

A **fault**, a short circuit from a lightning strike, a fallen tree, failed insulation or a digger through a cable, draws a current many times larger than normal and must be removed before it damages equipment, endangers people or destabilises the system. Every part of a grid is guarded by **protection**: instrument transformers measure currents and voltages, **relays** decide within a few cycles whether a fault is inside their zone, and **circuit breakers** interrupt the current. An AC breaker separates its contacts, an arc forms, and the breaker extinguishes the arc at a natural current zero, using vacuum, gas or an air blast; transmission breakers interrupt within two or three cycles of being told to.

Relays use several principles. **Overcurrent** relays trip when the current exceeds a setting, with time delays graded so that the relay closest to the fault acts first. **Distance** relays on transmission lines compute the impedance $V/I$ seen from their end, which a fault makes small and proportional to the distance to the fault. **Differential** relays on transformers, busbars and generators compare the current going in with the current coming out, which differ only when there is a fault inside.

<Example title="A fault on an 11 kV busbar">

A primary substation's 11 kV busbar is fed by a 20 MVA, 33/11 kV transformer with an impedance of 10%, and the 33 kV network behind it can supply a three-phase fault of 500 MVA (its **fault level**). On a 100 MVA base, the source impedance is $100/500 = 0.2$ pu and the transformer's is $0.10 \times 100/20 = 0.5$ pu. A bolted three-phase fault on the busbar sees $0.7$ pu in total, so the fault current is $1/0.7 = 1.43$ pu: a fault level of $100/0.7 = 142.9$ MVA, or
$$I_f = \frac{142.9 \times 10^6}{\sqrt3 \times 11 \times 10^3} \approx 7.5\ \text{kA},$$
which the 11 kV breakers must be rated to interrupt. In per-unit, the whole calculation is one division.

</Example>

Fault current is also how protection *sees* a fault, which raises a new problem. A synchronous generator can feed five to ten times its rated current into a nearby fault for a short time; an inverter, whose transistors would be destroyed by such currents, is usually limited to about 1.1 to 1.5 times its rating. A grid dominated by inverters therefore has lower and differently shaped fault currents, and protection schemes, settings and grid codes are being revised to match.

</Section>

<Section id="hvdc" kind="human" title="HVDC links">

Most of the grid is AC because transformers make changing voltage easy. **High-voltage direct current** (HVDC) wins in three situations:

- **Long distances.** A DC line needs two conductors instead of three, has no reactive power flow and no stability limit on its length, and its towers are cheaper. Beyond several hundred kilometres for overhead lines, the savings outweigh the cost of the converter stations at each end.
- **Cables under the sea or ground.** An AC cable is a long capacitor: its charging current grows with length and, within some tens of kilometres, uses up much of its rating. DC has no charging current, so long submarine links, such as the 1.4 GW links between Norway and Germany (NordLink) and Norway and Great Britain (North Sea Link), both opened in 2021, are DC, as are the connections of many offshore wind farms.
- **Linking grids that are not synchronous.** A DC link, or a **back-to-back** converter station with no line at all, couples two AC systems without forcing them to share a frequency, as between Japan's 50 Hz east and 60 Hz west, and lets power flow be set precisely, which also helps stop a disturbance spreading from one grid to the other.

Older **line-commutated** converters (LCC) use thyristors and still carry the largest powers, such as China's ±1100 kV Changji–Guquan link, about 3300 km long and rated 12 GW, energised in 2019. **Voltage-source converters** (VSC), built from IGBT modules arranged as **modular multilevel converters**, are now the usual choice for new links and offshore wind: they can control reactive power, start up a dead network (**black start**) and connect weak grids. They are built from the switches and modulation of <LessonLink slug="power-electronics">Power electronics</LessonLink>, thousands of them in series.

</Section>

<Section id="markets" kind="computation" title="How generators are dispatched">

Which power stations run at any moment? In much of Europe, North America, Australia and parts of Latin America and Asia, a **market** decides, in auctions a day ahead and then closer to real time. In simplest form: each generator offers its output at its **marginal cost**, the cost of producing one more megawatt-hour (fuel and carbon for a gas or coal plant, almost nothing for wind and solar); the operator accepts offers in order of price, the **merit order**, until demand is met; and every accepted generator is paid the price of the last, most expensive one needed, the **clearing price**.

```python run
# A toy electricity market: every generator offers its capacity at its marginal cost,
# the cheapest offers are accepted until demand is met, and the last one accepted sets the price.
# Costs are illustrative, in currency units per MWh.
fleet = [
    # name,          capacity MW, marginal cost
    ("nuclear",           4000,   12),
    ("hydro (run-of-river)", 1500, 3),
    ("coal",              3000,   55),
    ("gas, combined cycle", 6000, 75),
    ("gas, open cycle",   2000,  140),
    ("oil",                600,  230),
]

def dispatch(demand, wind, solar):
    offers = [("wind", wind, 0), ("solar", solar, 0)] + fleet
    offers.sort(key=lambda o: o[2])                     # the merit order: cheapest first
    remaining, used, price = demand, [], None
    for name, cap, cost in offers:
        if remaining <= 0:
            break
        take = min(cap, remaining)
        if take > 0:
            used.append((name, take))
            remaining -= take
            price = cost
    if remaining > 0:
        return None, used                               # not enough capacity: shortage
    return price, used

hours = [("03:00, windy night", 12000, 7000, 0),
         ("13:00, sunny", 16000, 1000, 7000),
         ("13:00, same demand, overcast", 16000, 1000, 1500),
         ("19:00, calm evening peak", 17000, 800, 0)]
for label, demand, wind, solar in hours:
    price, used = dispatch(demand, wind, solar)
    print(f"{label}: demand {demand} MW, wind {wind} MW, solar {solar} MW")
    print("   " + "; ".join(f"{n} {mw:.0f}" for n, mw in used))
    if price is None:
        print("   not enough capacity: the operator must cut demand")
    else:
        print(f"   clearing price {price} per MWh, set by {used[-1][0]}")
```

The same 16 GW costs 55 per MWh on the sunny day and 75 under cloud: zero-cost solar pushed gas out of the merit order. On the windy night, wind and nuclear set a price of just 12. In the calm evening peak, expensive open-cycle gas turbines are needed and set a price of 140, paid to every generator running. This **merit-order effect** is why wholesale prices in grids with much wind and solar swing widely, are sometimes negative, and peak in still evenings, and why storage, which can buy cheap and sell dear, has become valuable.

Real markets add a great deal: the network's limits (which, in **nodal** markets such as several in the United States, give every location its own price, while European markets use larger price **zones**), generators' start-up costs and minimum outputs (making dispatch an optimisation problem, **unit commitment**, solved with mixed-integer programming), reserve markets, and capacity mechanisms that pay for being available at the peak. Many countries do not use markets for this at all: a single utility or system operator dispatches plants centrally by cost, under regulation. In every arrangement, the physics of the previous sections must be respected second by second.

</Section>

<Section id="blackouts" kind="history" title="Blackouts as lessons">

Grids are planned so that no single failure causes a blackout, and almost always none does. The large blackouts come from combinations, and their investigations are among the best teaching documents the field has.

**North America, 14 August 2003.** A little after 4 pm Eastern time, a cascade of line trips spread from northern Ohio and blacked out parts of eight US states and the Canadian province of Ontario: about 50 million people and 61,800 MW of load. The joint US–Canadian task force's [final report](https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/oeprod/DocumentsandMedia/BlackoutFinal-Web.pdf) found that it was preventable. In the afternoon, several heavily loaded 345 kV lines in Ohio sagged into trees that had not been trimmed back and tripped; a failure of the alarm software in the local utility's control room meant its operators did not realise what was happening; and the regional reliability coordinator lacked the real-time tools to see it either. As load shifted onto the remaining lines, they overloaded and tripped in turn, and within minutes the cascade was unstoppable. The report's first recommendation was that reliability standards be made mandatory and enforceable; in the United States that followed in 2005, with NERC as the standards body under federal oversight.

**The Iberian Peninsula, 28 April 2025.** At 12:33 CEST, almost all of continental Spain and Portugal lost power. ENTSO-E's expert panel published a factual report in October 2025 and its [final report](https://www.entsoe.eu/publications/blackout/28-april-2025-iberian-blackout/) on 20 March 2026. The panel found no single cause but "a combination of many interacting factors": oscillations in the system earlier that day, gaps in voltage and reactive-power control, and rapid output reductions and disconnections of generation, which together led to fast rises in voltage and a cascade of generators disconnecting in Spain; with the generation gone, the frequency collapsed and the interconnection with France opened. It was an unusual kind of blackout, driven by **overvoltage** rather than by an overload or a shortage of power, and the panel's recommendations concern voltage control, the capabilities and settings of generators of every technology, and monitoring. Supply was restored to most of both countries by the following morning. Some questions about responsibility remain disputed between the parties, so read the panel's report itself rather than summaries of it.

**Great Britain, 9 August 2019** is a smaller event that illustrates this lesson's simulation. A lightning strike on a transmission line was followed, within seconds, by the near-simultaneous loss of an offshore wind farm and a gas-fired power station, plus smaller generators on the distribution network whose protection reacted to the disturbance. The total lost exceeded the reserve held, the frequency fell to about 48.8 Hz, and under-frequency load shedding disconnected around a million customers, exactly as designed, to save the rest of the system.

The common threads are the engineering of this lesson: situational awareness, protection settings, reactive power and voltage control, enough reserve and inertia for the largest credible event, and generators that ride through disturbances instead of disconnecting.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Electricity from a particular power station flows to particular customers."** Power flows through a meshed network according to the impedances of its lines, not to contracts. Contracts and markets settle who pays whom; the physics of power flow decides which lines carry what, which is why one overloaded line can push flows onto lines hundreds of kilometres away.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A blackout happens because there isn't enough generation."** Shortages are handled by controlled load shedding long before a system collapses. Large blackouts usually start with something else: lines tripping, protection acting unexpectedly, voltage problems or oscillations, and a cascade the operators cannot see or stop in time.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Frequency is a fixed number; only voltage varies."** The frequency is a continuously changing measure of the balance between generation and demand, typically moving tens of millihertz around nominal through the day, and the main signal automatic balancing responds to.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Renewables make grids unstable."** Inverter-based generation changes how stability is achieved: less natural inertia and fault current, but much faster and more controllable response. Systems running with very high instantaneous shares of wind and solar operate reliably when the needed services (fast response, voltage control, grid-forming capability, synchronous condensers where useful) are specified and procured. Investigations of individual events, including the Iberian one, point to the control and settings of all kinds of generation, not to the energy source as such.

</Misconception>
