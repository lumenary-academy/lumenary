<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="When a wire stops being a wire">

In circuit analysis a wire is a perfect connection: the voltage at one end is the voltage at the other, instantly. That is an approximation, and it fails in a way every engineer meets sooner or later. A microcontroller drives a 3.3 V clock signal along a 16 cm trace to another chip, and the oscilloscope at the far end shows not a clean step but a spike to 5 V, a dip, and a ringing tail. The signal leaves an antenna amplifier perfectly and half of it comes back. An Ethernet cable works over 100 m but a home-made extension with the wrong cable fails at 10 m.

All of these are the same phenomenon. A signal does not appear at the far end of a wire instantly: it travels as an **electromagnetic wave** guided by the conductors, at a large fraction of the speed of light, exactly as in <LessonLink slug="electromagnetic-waves">the previous lesson</LessonLink> but confined to the space around two conductors. When the wave reaches a point where the impedance changes, part of it reflects. If the travel time is short compared with how fast the signal changes, the reflections blur into the lumped circuit you know. If not, you must treat the connection as a **transmission line**.

This lesson builds the model, computes the impedance of real cables and circuit-board traces, simulates the reflections, and shows how engineers **match** impedances so that the wave arrives and stays.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Length, wavelength and rise time">

Two comparisons decide whether a connection is "electrically long".

**Length against wavelength.** For a sine wave of frequency $f$ travelling at speed $v$, the wavelength is $\lambda = v/f$. If the line is much shorter than $\lambda$, the voltage is nearly the same along it at any instant; if it is a sizeable fraction of $\lambda$, the voltage at the two ends can be quite different, even opposite. A common rule of thumb draws the line at about $\lambda/10$.

- The 50 Hz mains in a house: $\lambda = c/f \approx 6000$ km. House wiring is lumped. A 1000 km high-voltage line is not, and power engineers model it as a transmission line.
- A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi signal on a circuit board, where $v \approx 0.55c$: $\lambda \approx 7$ cm. A 1 cm trace to the antenna is already a transmission line.

**Delay against rise time.** Digital signals are not sine waves; what matters is how fast the edges are. A step with a 10–90 % **rise time** $t_r$ contains significant frequencies up to roughly $0.35/t_r$ (the bandwidth of a single-pole system with that rise time, from <LessonLink slug="frequency-response-and-filters">frequency response</LessonLink>). If the line's one-way delay $T_d$ is more than a fraction of $t_r$ (rules of thumb range from a sixth to a half, depending on how much ringing a design can tolerate), the reflections are visible as separate steps rather than smoothed into the edge.

<Example title="Is this trace a transmission line?">

A 10 cm microstrip trace on an FR-4 circuit board has a delay of about 6 ps per millimetre (computed below), so $T_d \approx 0.6$ ns. A slow logic family with $t_r = 5$ ns gives $T_d/t_r = 0.12$: lumped is fine. A modern microcontroller output with $t_r \approx 1$ ns gives $0.6$: a transmission line, whose reflections will show. A 10 Gbit/s serial link with $t_r \approx 30$ ps gives 20: the trace holds twenty rise times of signal at once and must be designed as a transmission line from the start.

</Example>

The clock frequency is not what matters. A 1 MHz clock with 1 ns edges rings as badly as a 100 MHz clock with 1 ns edges; it just rings less often. Engineers who forget this are surprised when a "slow" design fails after a supplier switches to a faster part.

</Section>

<Section id="telegrapher" kind="definitions" title="The telegrapher's model">

Any two parallel conductors (the core and shield of a coaxial cable, a circuit-board trace and the copper plane beneath it, the two wires of a twisted pair) store magnetic energy when current flows and electric energy when there is a voltage between them. Apply Kirchhoff's voltage law around one section and the current law at its node, divide by $\Delta z$ and let $\Delta z \to 0$. For a lossless line ($R = G = 0$) this gives the **telegrapher's equations**

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = -L\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial I}{\partial z} = -C\frac{\partial V}{\partial t},$$

the same pair of coupled first-order equations as Faraday's and the Ampère–Maxwell laws for a plane wave. Eliminating $I$ gives the wave equation $\partial^2 V/\partial z^2 = LC\,\partial^2 V/\partial t^2$, so voltage and current travel as waves.

<Definition term="characteristic impedance and propagation speed">

A lossless line with inductance $L$ and capacitance $C$ per unit length carries waves at speed $v = 1/\sqrt{LC}$. A wave travelling in one direction has a fixed ratio of voltage to current, the **characteristic impedance**

$$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}.$$

With losses, in the sinusoidal steady state, $Z_0 = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)/(G + j\omega C)}$ and the wave decays as it travels.

</Definition>

$Z_0$ is not a resistance you can measure with an ohmmeter: it is the ratio $V/I$ seen by a wave that has not yet met anything. A step launched into a long 50 Ω cable draws exactly the current it would draw from a 50 Ω resistor, until the reflection from the far end returns. For a line filled with a dielectric of permittivity $\varepsilon_r$, $LC = \varepsilon_r/c^2$ always, so $v = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$, with $\varepsilon_r$ replaced by an **effective** permittivity when the field is partly in air.

</Section>

<Section id="impedance" kind="human" title="Coaxial cables and circuit-board traces">

For a **coaxial cable** with inner conductor diameter $d$, outer diameter $D$ and dielectric $\varepsilon_r$, Gauss's and Ampère's laws give exact formulas:

$$L = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{D}{d}, \qquad C = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln(D/d)}, \qquad Z_0 = \frac{\eta_0}{2\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac{D}{d} \approx \frac{60\ \Omega}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac{D}{d}.$$

A **microstrip** (a trace of width $w$ on a board of thickness $h$ over a ground plane) has part of its field in the board and part in the air above, so it has no exact formula. Engineers use fitted closed-form approximations, such as Hammerstad's below, for first estimates, and field solvers for final designs. The program computes a coaxial cable with dimensions like the common RG-58 family, finds the trace width for 50 Ω on FR-4 board material for two board thicknesses, and asks a historical question.

```python run
import math

c, eps0, mu0 = 299_792_458.0, 8.8541878188e-12, 1.25663706127e-6
eta0 = math.sqrt(mu0 / eps0)

def coax(d_inner, d_outer, eps_r):
    """Per-metre L and C, characteristic impedance and speed of a coaxial line."""
    ln = math.log(d_outer / d_inner)
    L = mu0 / (2 * math.pi) * ln
    C = 2 * math.pi * eps0 * eps_r / ln
    return L, C, math.sqrt(L / C), 1 / math.sqrt(L * C)

def microstrip(w, h, eps_r):
    """Hammerstad's closed-form approximations: (Z0, effective permittivity)."""
    u = w / h
    if u <= 1:
        e_eff = (eps_r + 1) / 2 + (eps_r - 1) / 2 * (1 / math.sqrt(1 + 12 / u) + 0.04 * (1 - u) ** 2)
        z0 = 60 / math.sqrt(e_eff) * math.log(8 / u + u / 4)
    else:
        e_eff = (eps_r + 1) / 2 + (eps_r - 1) / 2 / math.sqrt(1 + 12 / u)
        z0 = eta0 / (math.sqrt(e_eff) * (u + 1.393 + 0.667 * math.log(u + 1.444)))
    return z0, e_eff

L, C, z0, v = coax(0.81e-3, 2.95e-3, 2.25)    # dimensions like the common RG-58 family
print(f"coax: L = {L * 1e9:.0f} nH/m, C = {C * 1e12:.0f} pF/m, Z0 = {z0:.1f} ohm, "
      f"v = {v / c:.3f} c, delay = {1e9 / v:.2f} ns/m")

def width_for(z_target, h, eps_r):
    lo, hi = 0.01 * h, 20 * h          # Z0 falls as the trace gets wider: bisect on w
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if microstrip(mid, h, eps_r)[0] > z_target:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return mid

for h in (1.6e-3, 0.2e-3):                 # FR-4 laminate, eps_r about 4.3
    w = width_for(50, h, 4.3)
    z0, e_eff = microstrip(w, h, 4.3)
    print(f"50-ohm microstrip, h = {h * 1e3:.1f} mm: w = {w * 1e3:.2f} mm, "
          f"eps_eff = {e_eff:.2f}, delay = {math.sqrt(e_eff) / c * 1e10:.1f} ps/cm")

# Why 50 ohm? For an air-filled coax of fixed outer size, loss and power handling depend on b/a.
best_loss = min((x / 1000 for x in range(1100, 10000)), key=lambda x: (1 + x) / math.log(x))
best_power = max((x / 1000 for x in range(1100, 10000)), key=lambda x: math.log(x) / x**2)
for name, x in [("lowest loss", best_loss), ("highest power", best_power)]:
    print(f"air coax, {name:13}: b/a = {x:.2f}, Z0 = {eta0 / (2 * math.pi) * math.log(x):.1f} ohm")
```

The coaxial cable carries signals at two-thirds of the speed of light, 5 ns per metre. The 50 Ω trace is about twice as wide as the board is thick, whatever the thickness, because $Z_0$ depends only on the ratio $w/h$; so a thin four-layer board needs narrow traces (0.39 mm here) and a 1.6 mm two-layer board needs wide ones (3.1 mm). The effective permittivity, 3.27, lies between that of air (1) and of FR-4 (4.3), and gives 60 ps per centimetre.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The last two lines explain a number you will see on every RF connector. For an air-filled coaxial line of a given outer size, conductor loss is lowest at $D/d \approx 3.59$, which is about 77 Ω, and the power it can carry before the air breaks down is highest at $D/d = \sqrt{e} \approx 1.65$, which is 30 Ω. A value of 50 Ω is a compromise between the two, which is the usual explanation of where it came from; cable television, which cares mostly about loss, uses 75 Ω.

</Narrator>

<Narrator who="gideon">

Hammerstad's formulas ignore the thickness of the copper, the roughness of its surface, the solder mask on top and the fact that FR-4's permittivity varies with frequency and from one batch of laminate to another. They are good to a few per cent, which is why boards that need controlled impedance are specified to the manufacturer as "50 Ω ± 10 %", and the manufacturer adjusts the trace width to its own process and checks test coupons.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="reflections" kind="human" title="Reflections, standing waves and VSWR">

Suppose a line of impedance $Z_0$ ends in a load $Z_L$. The incident wave has $V^+/I^+ = Z_0$, but the load insists on $V/I = Z_L$. If $Z_L \ne Z_0$, both conditions can only hold if a **reflected wave** $V^-$ (with $V^-/I^- = -Z_0$, since it travels the other way) is added. Solving $V^+ + V^- = Z_L\,(V^+ - V^-)/Z_0$ gives the central formula of the subject.

<Definition term="reflection coefficient">

The **reflection coefficient** at a load $Z_L$ on a line of impedance $Z_0$ is

$$\Gamma = \frac{V^-}{V^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}.$$

A matched load ($Z_L = Z_0$) gives $\Gamma = 0$; an open circuit gives $\Gamma = +1$ (the voltage doubles); a short gives $\Gamma = -1$ (the voltage cancels, as at the perfect conductor in the previous lesson's simulation). For a passive load $|\Gamma| \le 1$.

</Definition>

With a sinusoidal signal, the incident and reflected waves add to a **standing wave**. At a distance $d$ back from the load the voltage is $V^+e^{j\beta d}\,(1 + \Gamma e^{-2j\beta d})$, where $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda$. Its amplitude swings between $|V^+|(1 + |\Gamma|)$ and $|V^+|(1 - |\Gamma|)$, with maxima half a wavelength apart. Three numbers describe the mismatch, and you will meet all of them on datasheets:

- the **voltage standing-wave ratio** $\text{VSWR} = V_{\max}/V_{\min} = (1 + |\Gamma|)/(1 - |\Gamma|)$, from 1 (matched) to infinity;
- the **return loss** $-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|$ dB, how far below the incident power the reflection is (larger is better);
- the **mismatch loss** $-10\log_{10}(1 - |\Gamma|^2)$ dB, the power that fails to reach the load because it was reflected.

| VSWR | $\lvert\Gamma\rvert$ | Return loss | Power reflected | Mismatch loss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.1 | 0.048 | 26.4 dB | 0.2 % | 0.01 dB |
| 1.5 | 0.200 | 14.0 dB | 4.0 % | 0.18 dB |
| 2.0 | 0.333 | 9.5 dB | 11.1 % | 0.51 dB |
| 3.0 | 0.500 | 6.0 dB | 25.0 % | 1.25 dB |

A VSWR of 2 sounds bad and costs only half a decibel of power; mismatch matters more for what the reflection *does* (ringing in a digital link, an unstable amplifier, a distorted measurement) than for the power it loses.

Because of the standing wave, the impedance seen looking into the line changes with its length. A lossless line of length $\ell$ ending in $Z_L$ presents

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + jZ_0\tan\beta\ell}{Z_0 + jZ_L\tan\beta\ell}.$$

Three special cases are worth knowing by heart: a half-wavelength line repeats the load ($Z_{\text{in}} = Z_L$); a quarter-wavelength line **inverts** it ($Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0^2/Z_L$); and a short-circuited line of length $\ell$ presents a pure reactance $jZ_0\tan\beta\ell$, an inductor if shorter than $\lambda/4$ and a capacitor if between $\lambda/4$ and $\lambda/2$. Microwave circuits use such **stubs** in place of components.

</Section>

<Section id="bounce" kind="computation" title="Bounce diagrams: reflections in time">

Now return to the ringing clock signal. A driver with output resistance $R_s$ steps from 0 to $V_s$ at $t = 0$, into a line of impedance $Z_0$ and one-way delay $T$, ending in a load $R_L$. At first the driver sees only $Z_0$, so it launches a wave $V_1 = V_s Z_0/(R_s + Z_0)$. At $t = T$ this reaches the load and reflects with $\Gamma_L$; at $2T$ the reflection reaches the source and reflects again with $\Gamma_s = (R_s - Z_0)/(R_s + Z_0)$; and so on. A **bounce diagram** (or lattice diagram) draws the waves zigzagging down a time axis, and the voltage anywhere is the sum of every wave that has passed.

The program adds up the waves for three designs: a strong (15 Ω) driver with no termination, the same line with the driver's resistance raised to 50 Ω (**series termination**), and the strong driver with a 50 Ω resistor at the far end (**parallel termination**). The delay of 1 ns is about 16 cm of the microstrip computed above.

```python run
import numpy as np

def bounce(vs, rs, z0, rl, delay, t_end, dt):
    """Voltage at the load of a lossless line driven by a step from vs through rs.
    Returns (times, load voltages) by adding up the waves of the lattice diagram."""
    gs = (rs - z0) / (rs + z0)                     # reflection coefficient at the source
    gl = 1.0 if rl == float("inf") else (rl - z0) / (rl + z0)
    v1 = vs * z0 / (rs + z0)                       # the first wave launched at t = 0
    t = np.arange(0, t_end, dt)
    v = np.zeros_like(t)
    k = 0
    while (2 * k + 1) * delay < t_end:             # the k-th forward wave arrives at (2k+1)*delay
        forward = v1 * (gl * gs) ** k
        v += np.where(t >= (2 * k + 1) * delay, forward * (1 + gl), 0.0)
        k += 1
    return t, v

T = 1e-9                                           # one-way delay: about 16 cm of microstrip
cases = [("15-ohm driver, open load", 15, np.inf),
         ("50-ohm series termination", 50, np.inf),
         ("15-ohm driver, 50-ohm load", 15, 50)]
print(f"{'time (ns):':26}" + "".join(f"{x:6.1f}" for x in np.arange(0.5, 10, 1.0)))
for name, rs, rl in cases:
    t, v = bounce(3.3, rs, 50, rl, T, 10e-9, 0.5e-9)
    print(f"{name:26}" + "".join(f"{x:6.2f}" for x in v[1::2]))

# An oscilloscope view of the first case: the load voltage from 0 to 12 ns, 0.25 ns per column
t, v = bounce(3.3, 15, 50, np.inf, T, 12e-9, 0.25e-9)
for level in np.arange(5.0, -0.1, -0.5):
    row = "".join("*" if abs(x - level) < 0.25 else " " for x in v)
    print(f"{level:4.1f} V |{row}")
print("       " + "+" + "-" * (len(v) - 1) + "> 12 ns")
```

The unterminated line overshoots to 5.08 V, more than 1.5 V above the supply, then undershoots to 2.34 V and rings for many nanoseconds. A receiving chip would see a voltage outside its rated range (its protection diodes would clamp it, with currents nobody designed for), and the dip to 2.34 V comes close to the logic threshold: on a slightly longer line it could be read as a false edge. Both terminations fix it, in different ways:

- **Series termination** makes $R_s = Z_0$, so $\Gamma_s = 0$. The driver launches only half the voltage, the open end doubles it to exactly 3.3 V, and the reflection is absorbed when it gets back. It costs no static power, but points along the line see a half-height step for a while, so it suits point-to-point links with the receiver at the end.
- **Parallel termination** makes $R_L = Z_0$, so $\Gamma_L = 0$ and nothing reflects at all. But the load is now a 50 Ω resistor drawing current whenever the line is high, and the final voltage is the divider value $3.3 \times 50/65 = 2.54$ V. Real designs terminate to a mid-supply voltage, or use a capacitor in series with the resistor, to limit the power.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why does the open-line voltage converge to 3.3 V, as a circuit-theory answer says it must? Each round trip multiplies the wave by $\Gamma_L\Gamma_s$, so the load voltage is $2V_1(1 + \Gamma_s + \Gamma_s^2 + \cdots) = 2V_1/(1 - \Gamma_s)$, a geometric series. With $V_1 = V_s Z_0/(R_s + Z_0)$ and $1 - \Gamma_s = 2Z_0/(R_s + Z_0)$, that is exactly $V_s$: the lumped answer is the sum of infinitely many reflections.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="matching" kind="human" title="Matching">

In RF engineering the goal is usually the opposite of a digital link's: not just to stop ringing but to deliver as much power as possible from a source (a transmitter, an amplifier) into a load (an antenna, the next stage) whose impedance is not $Z_0$. A **matching network** placed between them transforms the load so that the source sees $Z_0$. It must be lossless, so it is built from reactances and pieces of line, and it works only over a band of frequencies.

<Theorem name="Quarter-wave transformer">

A section of line with impedance $Z_1$ and length $\lambda/4$ at frequency $f_0$, ending in a resistive load $R_L$, presents an input impedance $Z_1^2/R_L$ at $f_0$. Choosing $Z_1 = \sqrt{Z_0 R_L}$ therefore matches $R_L$ to $Z_0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

At $\ell = \lambda/4$, $\beta\ell = \pi/2$ and $\tan\beta\ell \to \infty$. Dividing the numerator and denominator of the input-impedance formula by $\tan\beta\ell$ and letting it grow gives $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_1 \cdot (jZ_1)/(jR_L) = Z_1^2/R_L$. With $Z_1^2 = Z_0R_L$ this equals $Z_0$.

</Proof>

The **L-network** uses two reactances instead: one in series on the low-resistance side and one in shunt on the high-resistance side. To match $R_{\text{lo}}$ to $R_{\text{hi}}$, define $Q = \sqrt{R_{\text{hi}}/R_{\text{lo}} - 1}$; the series reactance is $X_s = QR_{\text{lo}}$ and the shunt reactance is $X_p = R_{\text{hi}}/Q$, one inductive and one capacitive. (The series reactance turns $R_{\text{lo}}$ into an impedance whose parallel equivalent has resistance $R_{\text{hi}}$; the shunt element cancels the leftover reactance.) The program checks both designs at and around their design frequency.

```python run
import numpy as np

def z_in(zl, z0, beta_l):
    """Input impedance of a lossless line of electrical length beta*l (radians) ending in zl."""
    t = np.tan(beta_l)
    return z0 * (zl + 1j * z0 * t) / (z0 + 1j * zl * t)

def gamma(z, z0=50):
    return (z - z0) / (z + z0)

def vswr(g):
    return (1 + abs(g)) / (1 - abs(g))

def show(z):
    re, im = round(z.real, 1) + 0.0, round(z.imag, 1) + 0.0     # + 0.0 avoids printing -0.0
    return f"{re:6.1f} {'+' if im >= 0 else '-'} j{abs(im):5.1f} ohm"

# 1. A 100-ohm load on a 50-ohm line: the impedance the source sees depends on the line's length
print("100-ohm load on a 50-ohm line")
for frac in [0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5]:
    z = z_in(100, 50, 2 * np.pi * frac)
    print(f"  {frac:6.4f} wavelengths: Zin = {show(z)}, |Gamma| = {abs(gamma(z)):.3f}")

# 2. A quarter-wave transformer with Z1 = sqrt(50 * 100), a quarter wavelength long at 1 GHz
z1 = np.sqrt(50 * 100)
print(f"\nquarter-wave transformer, Z1 = {z1:.1f} ohm, from 100 ohm to 50 ohm")
for f in [0.8e9, 0.9e9, 1.0e9, 1.1e9, 1.2e9]:
    z = z_in(100, z1, np.pi / 2 * f / 1e9)
    g = abs(gamma(z))
    rl = "  perfect" if g < 1e-6 else f"{-20 * np.log10(g):6.1f} dB"
    print(f"  {f / 1e9:.1f} GHz: Zin = {show(z)}, VSWR = {vswr(g):.2f}, return loss = {rl}")

# 3. An L-network matching a 12-ohm antenna to 50 ohm at 433 MHz
r_lo, r_hi, f0 = 12.0, 50.0, 433e6
q = np.sqrt(r_hi / r_lo - 1)
x_series, x_shunt = q * r_lo, r_hi / q              # series inductor, then shunt capacitor
L = x_series / (2 * np.pi * f0)
C = 1 / (2 * np.pi * f0 * x_shunt)
print(f"\nL-network: Q = {q:.2f}, series L = {L * 1e9:.2f} nH, shunt C = {C * 1e12:.2f} pF")
for f in [400e6, 420e6, 433e6, 446e6, 466e6]:
    w = 2 * np.pi * f
    z_series = r_lo + 1j * w * L                    # the antenna plus the series inductor
    z = 1 / (1 / z_series + 1j * w * C)             # in parallel with the capacitor
    print(f"  {f / 1e6:5.0f} MHz: Zin = {show(z)}, VSWR = {vswr(gamma(z)):.2f}")
```

Three lessons are in the output. First, the impedance of a 100 Ω load seen through a 50 Ω line goes round a cycle every half wavelength while $|\Gamma|$ stays 0.333: the line does not change how mismatched the load is, only how it looks. A quarter wavelength turns 100 Ω into 25 Ω, the inversion $50^2/100$. Second, the quarter-wave transformer is perfect at 1 GHz and still has a VSWR below 1.25 across ±20 %. Third, the L-network for a 12 Ω antenna at 433 MHz (a band used for short-range remote controls in many countries) needs a 7.85 nH inductor and a 13.08 pF capacitor, and is good to a VSWR of about 1.1 over ±13 MHz. The larger the ratio $R_{\text{hi}}/R_{\text{lo}}$, the higher $Q$ and the narrower the band; wideband matches use several sections in cascade.

</Section>

<Section id="smith-chart" kind="visualization" title="The Smith chart">

Matching by algebra is tedious, and for eighty years RF engineers have done it with a picture instead. In 1939 Phillip Smith at Bell Laboratories published a chart (a similar chart had been published in Japan by Tosaku Mizuhashi in 1937) based on one idea: **plot the reflection coefficient, not the impedance.** Every passive load has $|\Gamma| \le 1$, so the whole of the right half of the impedance plane, infinite as it is, fits in a unit disc.

Normalise the impedance, $z = Z_L/Z_0 = r + jx$, so that $\Gamma = (z - 1)/(z + 1)$. Lines of constant resistance $r$ map to circles that all touch the point $\Gamma = 1$, and lines of constant reactance $x$ map to arcs of circles through the same point. The grid of those circles and arcs, drawn over the $\Gamma$ disc, is the Smith chart.

The chart turns the input-impedance formula into geometry. Moving a distance $\ell$ along a lossless line towards the source multiplies $\Gamma$ by $e^{-2j\beta\ell}$: the point **rotates clockwise** about the centre on a circle of constant $|\Gamma|$ (a constant-VSWR circle), and one full turn is half a wavelength. Adding a series reactance moves the point along a circle of constant $r$; adding a shunt element moves it along a circle of constant conductance (the same chart rotated by 180°, which printed charts overlay in a second colour).

<Example title="Matching 100 + j50 Ω with a line and an inductor">

On a 50 Ω line, $Z_L = 100 + j50\ \Omega$ normalises to $z = 2 + j1$, so $\Gamma = (1 + j)/(3 + j) = 0.4 + j0.2$ and $|\Gamma| = 0.447$ (VSWR 2.62). Rotating clockwise on that circle, the point meets the $r = 1$ circle after exactly $\lambda/8$, at $z = 1 - j1$, i.e. $50 - j50\ \Omega$ (check it with `z_in(100 + 50j, 50, np.pi / 4)` in the program above). The resistance is now right; a series reactance of $+j50\ \Omega$ cancels the rest. At 1 GHz that is an inductor of $50/(2\pi \times 10^9) \approx 7.96$ nH. Two steps on the chart, each obvious, replace a page of complex algebra.

</Example>

<Narrator who="chloe">

The map $z \mapsto (z - 1)/(z + 1)$ is a Möbius transformation, and Möbius transformations send circles and lines to circles and lines and preserve angles. That single fact from <TopicLink id="math.analysis.complex-analysis">complex analysis</TopicLink> is why the chart's grid is made of circles, and why the right half-plane, every passive impedance there is, lands exactly on the unit disc.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="s-parameters" kind="human" title="S-parameters">

At high frequencies, voltages and currents at a component's terminals are hard to measure directly, but incident and reflected **waves** are easy: directional couplers separate them. So RF components are described by **scattering parameters**. At each port $i$ of a network, let $a_i$ be the incident wave and $b_i$ the outgoing wave (normalised so that $|a_i|^2$ is power). For a two-port,

$$\begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} \\ S_{21} & S_{22} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix},$$

measured with every port terminated in $Z_0$ (normally 50 Ω). $S_{11}$ is the input reflection coefficient, $\Gamma$ again; $S_{21}$ is the forward transmission (the gain of an amplifier, the loss of a cable or filter); $S_{12}$ is the reverse transmission (isolation); $S_{22}$ is the output reflection. All are complex and depend on frequency, and datasheets quote their magnitudes in dB.

<Example title="S-parameters of a series resistor">

Put a 25 Ω resistor in series between two 50 Ω ports. Port 1 sees the resistor plus the 50 Ω termination, 75 Ω, so $S_{11} = (75 - 50)/(75 + 50) = 0.2$ (return loss 14 dB). The transmitted wave works out to $S_{21} = 2Z_0/(2Z_0 + R) = 100/125 = 0.8$. Of the incident power, $|S_{11}|^2 = 4\%$ is reflected and $|S_{21}|^2 = 64\%$ transmitted; the missing 32 % is turned into heat in the resistor. For a lossless network $|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{21}|^2 = 1$ (the S-matrix is unitary), so a shortfall measures loss.

</Example>

S-parameters are measured with a **vector network analyser** (VNA), which sweeps a source across frequency and measures the magnitude and phase of the waves at each port, after a calibration with known standards (short, open, load and through) removes the effects of its own cables. Results are exchanged as **Touchstone** files (`.s2p` for two ports), which circuit simulators import directly. The open-source [scikit-rf](https://scikit-rf.org/) package reads, plots and calibrates them in Python. A two-port is the start: a high-speed differential link is routinely characterised as a four-port, and a phased-array antenna module as dozens.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view">

- **Signal integrity.** Every fast digital interface is a transmission-line design: DDR memory, PCIe, USB, HDMI and Ethernet specify their line impedances (USB pairs 90 Ω differential, Ethernet twisted pairs 100 Ω) and termination schemes. Engineers simulate links using driver and receiver models in the [IBIS](https://ibis.org/) format and channel S-parameters, and look at eye diagrams (met in <LessonLink slug="modulation">modulation</LessonLink>).
- **Controlled-impedance boards.** As in <LessonLink slug="printed-circuit-boards">printed circuit boards</LessonLink>, the stack-up is agreed with the manufacturer, who tunes trace widths to its process and verifies them with **time-domain reflectometry** (TDR): send a fast step down a test trace and watch the reflections, which map impedance along the line exactly as in the bounce diagram.
- **RF front ends.** Every antenna, filter and amplifier in a phone is matched to 50 Ω with small L-networks of chip inductors and capacitors, tuned by measurement on a VNA because parasitics move every value. Free tools such as [Qucs-S](https://github.com/ra3xdh/qucs_s) simulate such networks.
- **Power systems.** Long overhead lines and cables are modelled as transmission lines too, and lightning surges travel along them as waves; that is part of <LessonLink slug="the-power-grid">the power grid</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A 50 Ω cable has a resistance of 50 Ω."** An ohmmeter across the ends of an unterminated coaxial cable reads an open circuit, and along one conductor a fraction of an ohm. $Z_0$ is the ratio of voltage to current in a travelling wave, set by the cable's geometry and dielectric, and a lossless line with $Z_0 = 50\ \Omega$ dissipates nothing.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Reflections only matter at high clock frequencies."** It is the edge rate, not the clock rate, that matters. A 1 kHz signal with 1 ns edges rings on a 16 cm trace just as much as a fast clock does.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Matching always means making the load equal to Z₀, and it always maximises efficiency."** Maximum power transfer from a source of impedance $Z_s$ needs the **conjugate** match $Z_L = Z_s^*$, which equals $Z_0$ only when the source is $Z_0$. And maximum power transfer wastes half the power in the source; power amplifiers are often deliberately mismatched to the impedance that gives the best efficiency or output power (found by "load-pull" measurements).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A good VSWR means a good antenna."** A 50 Ω resistor has a perfect VSWR and radiates nothing. VSWR says only that power is accepted, not where it goes; a lossy antenna or a lossy cable in front of a bad one also looks well matched.

</Misconception>
