<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="From the physical world to a number">

A weather station reports 18.4 °C. A bathroom scale says 61.2 kg. A phone knows which way up it is. Each of those numbers began as something that is not electrical at all — heat, force, gravity — and passed through a chain of conversions before a program could print it. Every link in the chain can add error, and the engineer's job is to know how much.

<Section id="sensors" kind="definitions" title="Sensors: turning quantities into signals">

<Definition term="Sensor, transducer, sensitivity">

A **sensor** responds to a physical quantity (the **measurand**) with a change in an electrical property; a **transducer** converts energy from one form to another, and the word covers sensors and actuators alike. A sensor's **sensitivity** is the change in output per unit change of the measurand, its **range** the span it can measure, and its **resolution** the smallest change it can detect.

</Definition>

Five sensors cover a large share of real designs:

- **Thermistor.** A ceramic resistor whose resistance changes strongly with temperature. An NTC (negative temperature coefficient) thermistor of 10 kΩ at 25 °C might fall to 1.3 kΩ at 80 °C. A common model is the **Beta equation**, $R(T) = R_0 \exp\!\big(B(1/T - 1/T_0)\big)$ with $T$ in kelvin; more accurate is the three-term Steinhart–Hart equation. Thermistors are cheap and sensitive but nonlinear; platinum **RTDs** (100 Ω at 0 °C) are linear and stable, and **thermocouples** reach over 1,000 °C.
- **Strain gauge.** A zig-zag of thin metal foil glued to a part; when the part stretches by a fraction $\varepsilon$ (the **strain**), the foil's resistance changes by $\Delta R/R = \mathrm{GF}\cdot\varepsilon$, with a **gauge factor** GF of about 2. Strains are tiny, often below $10^{-3}$, so gauges sit in a **Wheatstone bridge** that outputs only the difference. A load cell in a kitchen scale is a metal beam with four gauges.
- **MEMS accelerometer.** A microscopic silicon proof mass on silicon springs, etched with comb-shaped electrodes. Acceleration deflects the mass by nanometres, changing the capacitance between combs by a tiny fraction of a picofarad, which an on-chip circuit measures. MEMS gyroscopes measure rotation with vibrating masses and the Coriolis force. They come with their own ADC and an I²C or SPI interface: the sensor *is* a small mixed-signal chip.
- **Photodiode.** A PN junction (from <LessonLink slug="semiconductors-and-diodes">Semiconductors and diodes</LessonLink>) in which absorbed photons create a current proportional to light power, roughly 0.4 to 0.6 A per watt for silicon in red and near-infrared light. The current is tiny, so a **transimpedance amplifier** — an op amp with a feedback resistor $R_f$ — turns it into a voltage $V = I R_f$: 2 µA through 1 MΩ gives 2 V.
- **Hall sensor.** A current through a thin semiconductor plate in a magnetic field $B$ is pushed sideways, giving a small **Hall voltage** proportional to $B$. Hall sensors detect magnets (a laptop's lid, a motor's rotor position), measure current without touching the conductor, and sense position in joysticks and throttles.

Most sensors produce small, offset, noisy or high-impedance signals. **Signal conditioning** is the analogue circuitry that fixes them before conversion, mostly built from the amplifiers of <LessonLink slug="operational-amplifiers">Operational amplifiers</LessonLink>:

1. **Amplify** so the signal spans most of the ADC's input range, without clipping.
2. **Shift** (offset) a signal that swings around zero into an ADC that accepts only 0 to $V_{\text{ref}}$.
3. **Filter**, above all with an **anti-aliasing** low-pass filter that removes everything above half the sampling rate, because once a signal has aliased no software can tell it apart from a real one.
4. **Buffer** a high-impedance source so the ADC's input does not load it.
5. **Protect** the input against overvoltage and electrostatic discharge.

<Example title="A strain gauge needs an amplifier">

A quarter bridge (one active gauge, three fixed resistors) excited by 3.3 V outputs approximately $V_{\text{ex}}\,\mathrm{GF}\,\varepsilon / 4$. At a strain of $10^{-3}$ that is $3.3 \times 2 \times 0.001 / 4 = 1.65$ mV. A 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference has steps of $3.3/4096 = 0.81$ mV, so the whole signal is two codes. An **instrumentation amplifier** with a gain of 1,000 lifts it to 1.65 V, or a 24-bit sigma-delta converter with a built-in programmable-gain amplifier, as in most commercial scales, measures it directly. Either way the bridge's own excitation should be the ADC's reference, so that a drifting supply cancels.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="thermistor" kind="examples" title="Worked example: a thermistor thermometer">

Put a 10 kΩ NTC thermistor ($B = 3950$ K) at the bottom of a divider, a fixed 10 kΩ resistor at the top, and connect the midpoint to a 12-bit ADC whose reference is the same 3.3 V that feeds the divider. The ADC's code is then $4096 \cdot R_T/(R_{\text{fixed}} + R_T)$, and the supply voltage cancels out: a **ratiometric** measurement, immune to the supply's accuracy. The program tabulates the codes and the temperature change per code, and converts codes back to temperatures.

```python run
import math

R0, T0, B = 10_000.0, 298.15, 3950.0     # NTC thermistor: 10 kilohms at 25 C, beta 3950 K
R_FIXED = 10_000.0                        # the divider's other resistor, from the reference to the ADC pin
BITS = 12

def thermistor_ohms(t_celsius):
    t = t_celsius + 273.15
    return R0 * math.exp(B * (1 / t - 1 / T0))

def adc_input(t_celsius):
    """The divider's output as a fraction of the reference: the ADC and the divider share it."""
    r = thermistor_ohms(t_celsius)
    return r / (R_FIXED + r)

def adc_code(t_celsius):
    return math.floor(2**BITS * adc_input(t_celsius))     # an ideal 12-bit ADC

def code_to_celsius(code):
    r = R_FIXED * (code + 0.5) / (2**BITS - code - 0.5)   # +0.5: the middle of the code's interval
    return 1 / (1 / T0 + math.log(r / R0) / B) - 273.15

print(f"{'T (C)':>6} {'R (ohm)':>9} {'V at 3.3 V':>11} {'code':>6} {'C per code':>11}")
for t in range(-20, 101, 20):
    slope = (adc_input(t + 0.01) - adc_input(t - 0.01)) / 0.02 * 2**BITS   # codes per degree
    print(f"{t:>6} {thermistor_ohms(t):>9.0f} {3.3 * adc_input(t):>9.3f} V {adc_code(t):>6} {1 / abs(slope):>11.3f}")

print()
for code in (3000, 2048, 1000, 300):
    print(f"code {code:4} -> {code_to_celsius(code):6.2f} C")
```

The resolution is finest, about 0.022 °C per code, near room temperature, where the thermistor equals the fixed resistor and the divider is steepest, and is six times coarser at 100 °C. That is why the fixed resistor is chosen to match the thermistor at the temperature you care most about. Resolution is not accuracy, though: a typical thermistor is specified to ±1% in resistance, about ±0.25 °C near 25 °C, and the Beta model itself is off by a few tenths of a degree away from its reference points. A code that changes every 0.022 °C still has to be calibrated to mean anything at that level.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The ADC does not see the divider directly: it briefly connects a small sampling capacitor, a few picofarads, to the pin and waits for it to charge. The divider's source resistance is 5 kΩ (10 kΩ in parallel with 10 kΩ), and to settle within half an LSB at 12 bits the capacitor needs about $\ln(2^{13}) \approx 9$ time constants. With 5 pF and 5 kΩ that is about 0.23 µs; a sensor with 100 kΩ of source resistance would need 4.5 µs, longer than many microcontrollers' default sampling time. Either lengthen the sampling time or put a capacitor of about 100 nF from the pin to ground as a charge reservoir.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="architectures" kind="definitions" title="How an ADC works: four architectures">

<Definition term="Analog-to-digital converter">

An **analog-to-digital converter** (ADC) samples an input voltage at instants set by a clock and outputs, for each sample, an $N$-bit code $k$ such that the input lies within the code's interval: $k \cdot \mathrm{LSB} \le v < (k+1)\cdot\mathrm{LSB}$ for an ideal converter, where $\mathrm{LSB} = V_{\text{ref}}/2^N$ is the **least significant bit**, the width of one step. A **sample-and-hold** circuit freezes the input while it is converted.

</Definition>

Four architectures dominate, trading speed against resolution and power:

| Architecture | How it works | Typical resolution and speed | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SAR** (successive approximation) | binary search: one comparator and an internal DAC decide one bit per clock | 8–18 bits, up to a few MS/s (tens of MS/s in fast parts) | inside almost every microcontroller; general data acquisition |
| **Sigma-delta** ($\Sigma\Delta$) | a 1-bit (or few-bit) converter running far faster than needed, with feedback that pushes its error to high frequencies, then a digital filter | 16–32 bits, from a few S/s to a few MS/s | weigh scales, precision instruments, audio |
| **Flash** | $2^N - 1$ comparators compare the input against every threshold at once | 4–8 bits, up to several GS/s | the fastest oscilloscopes and radar, often as a building block |
| **Pipelined** | a chain of stages, each resolving a few bits and passing on the amplified remainder | 10–16 bits, tens to hundreds of MS/s, a few cycles of delay | software-defined radio, ultrasound imaging, communications |

The fastest converters of 2026, inside the receivers of data-centre links running at over 200 Gbit/s per lane, interleave dozens of SAR converters in time to sample at more than 100 GS/s. Murmann's [ADC performance survey](https://github.com/bmurmann/ADC-survey) tracks where the frontier lies.

The program runs both of the commonest architectures. The SAR is ten comparisons. The **first-order sigma-delta modulator** is stranger: each clock it outputs a single bit, and an integrator accumulates the difference between the input and what has been output so far, so the bits' running average is forced to follow the input.

```python run
def sar_convert(vin, vref, bits, verbose=False):
    """Successive approximation: decide one bit per clock, most significant first."""
    code = 0
    for bit in reversed(range(bits)):
        trial = code | (1 << bit)                 # tentatively set this bit
        v_dac = vref * trial / 2**bits            # the internal DAC's voltage for the trial code
        keep = vin >= v_dac                       # one comparator decision
        if keep:
            code = trial
        if verbose:
            print(f"  bit {bit}: try {trial:010b} = {v_dac:.4f} V  -> {'keep' if keep else 'clear'}")
    return code

vin, vref = 1.234, 3.3
code = sar_convert(vin, vref, 10, verbose=True)
print(f"10-bit SAR result: {code} = {code:010b}, i.e. {vref * code / 1024:.4f} V to {vref * (code + 1) / 1024:.4f} V\n")

def sigma_delta(x, n):
    """First-order sigma-delta modulator for an input 0 <= x <= 1 (as a fraction of the reference).
    Each clock it outputs one bit; the integrator accumulates the error between input and output."""
    integrator, bits = 0.0, []
    for _ in range(n):
        bit = 1 if integrator >= 0 else 0
        integrator += x - bit                     # feedback: subtract what was output
        bits.append(bit)
    return bits

x = vin / vref
stream = sigma_delta(x, 4096)
print("input as a fraction of the reference:", round(x, 6))
print("first 40 output bits:", "".join(map(str, stream[:40])))
for n in (16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096):
    estimate = sum(stream[:n]) / n
    print(f"average of {n:4} bits = {estimate:.6f}   error {abs(estimate - x):.6f}   (bound 1/n = {1 / n:.6f})")
```

<Theorem name="The first-order sigma-delta average converges">

For a constant input $0 \le x < 1$, the fraction of ones among the first $n$ output bits of the modulator above differs from $x$ by less than $1/n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $I_k$ be the integrator after $k$ steps, with $I_0 = 0$. If $I_k \ge 0$ the bit is 1 and $I_{k+1} = I_k + x - 1$; otherwise the bit is 0 and $I_{k+1} = I_k + x$. By induction $x - 1 \le I_k < x$ for every $k \ge 1$: a non-negative value in $[0, x)$ moves down into $[x - 1, 2x - 1) \subseteq [x - 1, x)$, and a negative value in $[x - 1, 0)$ moves up into $[2x - 1, x) \subseteq [x - 1, x)$. Summing the updates, $I_n = n x - (\text{number of ones})$, so $|\text{ones}/n - x| = |I_n|/n < \max(x, 1 - x)/n \le 1/n$.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

So a one-bit converter becomes a 12-bit one by trading amplitude resolution for time: 4,096 bits, averaged, pin the input down to about one part in 4,096. Real sigma-delta converters do far better than this simple average, because the feedback loop pushes the quantisation error to high frequencies (**noise shaping**), where a sharp digital low-pass filter, the **decimation filter**, removes it. A second-order loop gains about 15 dB of signal-to-noise ratio, two and a half bits, for every doubling of the oversampling ratio.

</Narrator>

<Section id="resolution" kind="computation" title="Resolution, quantisation noise and ENOB">

An ideal $N$-bit converter rounds every input to its code, so it makes an error $e$ between $-\tfrac12$ and $+\tfrac12$ LSB. For a busy signal the error behaves like random noise spread evenly over that interval.

<Theorem name="Quantisation noise and the ideal SNR">

If the quantisation error is uniformly distributed on $[-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]$, where $\Delta$ is one LSB, its RMS value is $\Delta/\sqrt{12}$. For a full-scale sine wave, the ideal signal-to-noise ratio of an $N$-bit converter is then

$$
\mathrm{SNR} = 6.02\,N + 1.76\ \text{dB}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The mean square of a uniform error is $\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2} e^2\,de = \frac{\Delta^2}{12}$. A full-scale sine spans $2^N \Delta$ from peak to peak, so its amplitude is $2^{N-1}\Delta$ and its mean square is $(2^{N-1}\Delta)^2/2 = 2^{2N}\Delta^2/8$. The ratio is $\frac{2^{2N}/8}{1/12} = 1.5 \cdot 2^{2N}$, and $10\log_{10}(1.5 \cdot 2^{2N}) = 20N\log_{10}2 + 10\log_{10}1.5 = 6.02N + 1.76$ dB.

</Proof>

Real converters fall short because of thermal noise, distortion and clock jitter, so datasheets quote the measured **SINAD** (signal to noise and distortion) and its translation into the **effective number of bits**, $\mathrm{ENOB} = (\mathrm{SINAD} - 1.76)/6.02$: the number of bits an ideal converter would need to be as good. The program checks the formula, shows what input noise does to ENOB, and then tests the popular trick of **averaging** many readings.

```python run
import numpy as np

def quantise(v, bits):
    """An ideal ADC for inputs in [-1, 1): find the code, then return the middle of its interval."""
    lsb = 2 / 2**bits
    codes = np.clip(np.floor(v / lsb), -2**(bits - 1), 2**(bits - 1) - 1)
    return (codes + 0.5) * lsb

N = 4096
n = np.arange(N)
x = 0.999 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 127 * n / N)           # a near-full-scale sine, 127 whole cycles

print("bits  SNR measured  6.02N + 1.76")
for bits in (6, 8, 10, 12, 14):
    err = quantise(x, bits) - x
    snr = 10 * np.log10(np.mean(x**2) / np.mean(err**2))
    print(f"{bits:4} {snr:10.2f} dB {6.02 * bits + 1.76:10.2f} dB")

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
bits = 12
lsb = 2 / 2**bits
noisy = x + rng.normal(0, 1.5 * lsb, N)               # analog noise of 1.5 LSB rms in front of the ADC
err = quantise(noisy, bits) - x
sinad = 10 * np.log10(np.mean(x**2) / np.mean(err**2))
print(f"\n12-bit ADC with 1.5 LSB of input noise: SINAD {sinad:.2f} dB, ENOB {(sinad - 1.76) / 6.02:.2f} bits")

print("\nAveraging m readings of a constant input (rms error over 2000 trials, in LSB):")
print("      m   no noise   0.5 LSB noise")
true = 100.3 * lsb
for k in range(0, 5):
    m = 4**k
    row = []
    for sigma in (0.0, 0.5):
        readings = quantise(true + rng.normal(0, sigma * lsb, (2000, m)), bits)
        estimate = readings.mean(axis=1)
        row.append(np.sqrt(np.mean((estimate - true)**2)) / lsb)
    print(f"{m:7} {row[0]:10.4f} {row[1]:14.4f}")
```

The formula holds to within a fraction of a decibel from 8 bits up. Input noise of 1.5 LSB RMS costs this 12-bit converter 2.4 bits, leaving an ENOB of 9.6, which is why a 12-bit ADC on a noisy microcontroller board often behaves like a 9- or 10-bit one.

The averaging table holds the lesson's most useful surprise. With a perfectly clean input of 100.3 LSB, every reading is the same code, and averaging 256 of them gives exactly the same answer, 0.2 LSB off. With half an LSB of random noise added, each reading is *worse*, but the noise makes readings land on neighbouring codes in the right proportions, and the average converges: every fourfold increase in readings halves the error, one extra bit per factor of four, because the standard deviation of a mean of $m$ independent readings falls as $1/\sqrt{m}$ (Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistics">Statistics</TopicLink> proves it). This is **oversampling with dither**: to gain $k$ bits, average $4^k$ samples, which needs a sampling rate $4^k$ times higher than the signal requires, and enough noise, about half an LSB or more, to decorrelate the quantisation error. Some designs add the noise deliberately.

<Narrator who="margaret">

What does a 24-bit converter's last bit mean? With a 2.5 V reference one LSB is $2.5/2^{24} \approx 0.15$ µV. The thermal noise of a 1 kΩ resistor at room temperature over a 1 kHz bandwidth is $\sqrt{4kTRB} \approx 0.13$ µV RMS, already about one LSB, before any amplifier noise. That is why 24-bit converters quote "noise-free bits" or ENOB of 20 or so at low data rates, and why the extra bits are still useful: they let the noise, not the converter, set the limit.

</Narrator>

The sampling rate itself must exceed twice the highest frequency present, after the anti-aliasing filter, and in practice it is several times higher, because real filters roll off gradually. A microcontroller ADC sampling a thermistor ten times a second is limited by everything except speed; one digitising audio at 48 kHz needs a filter that is flat to 20 kHz and deep by 24 kHz, a sharp transition that is much easier to build digitally after an oversampling sigma-delta converter than in analogue circuitry before a SAR.

</Section>

<Section id="dacs" kind="examples" title="Back to analogue: DACs and PWM">

A **digital-to-analogue converter** (DAC) runs the chain backwards: code in, voltage or current out. Three structures are common. An **R-2R ladder** uses only two resistor values; each bit switches its branch between the reference and ground, and the ladder's halving at every node weights bit $k$ by $2^k$. A **resistor string** taps a chain of $2^N$ equal resistors, inherently monotonic but large for many bits. **Current-steering** DACs switch weighted current sources and reach GS/s for radio transmitters. Many microcontrollers include a 12-bit DAC.

If yours does not, a timer's **pulse-width modulation** (PWM) output and a resistor and capacitor make a cheap DAC. A PWM signal with duty cycle $D$ is high for a fraction $D$ of each period $T$, so its average is $D\,V_{DD}$. An RC low-pass filter with time constant $\tau = RC$ much longer than $T$ passes the average and attenuates the switching. While the output is high, the capacitor charges with current about $(V_{DD} - DV_{DD})/R$ for a time $DT$, so the peak-to-peak ripple is approximately

$$
\Delta V \approx V_{DD}\,\frac{D(1-D)\,T}{RC},
$$

largest at $D = 0.5$. The resolution is set by the timer: with a 64 MHz timer clock and 1,024 counts per period (10 bits), the PWM frequency is 62.5 kHz.

The simulation solves the RC circuit exactly, period by period, for two filters, and also measures how long each takes to settle after a step.

```python run
import math

VDD = 3.3                     # PWM high level, volts
F_CLK, BITS = 64e6, 10        # timer clock and PWM resolution
T = 2**BITS / F_CLK           # PWM period: 1024 timer ticks
print(f"PWM frequency {1 / T / 1e3:.1f} kHz, 1 LSB = {VDD / 2**BITS * 1e3:.2f} mV")

def rc_filtered_pwm(duty, tau, periods):
    """Exact response of a first-order RC low-pass to a PWM wave, period by period.
    Returns the capacitor voltage at the end of each high and each low phase."""
    v, highs, lows = 0.0, [], []
    for _ in range(periods):
        v = VDD + (v - VDD) * math.exp(-duty * T / tau)       # charging towards VDD
        highs.append(v)
        v = v * math.exp(-(1 - duty) * T / tau)               # discharging towards 0
        lows.append(v)
    return highs, lows

for r, c in [(10e3, 100e-9), (1e3, 100e-9)]:
    tau = r * c
    print(f"\nR = {r / 1e3:.0f} kilohm, C = {c * 1e9:.0f} nF: tau = {tau * 1e3:.1f} ms, cut-off {1 / (2 * math.pi * tau):.0f} Hz")
    print("  duty   average   ripple (simulated)   ripple (formula)")
    for duty in (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.9):
        highs, lows = rc_filtered_pwm(duty, tau, periods=int(20 * tau / T))
        ripple = highs[-1] - lows[-1]
        approx = VDD * duty * (1 - duty) * T / tau
        print(f"  {duty:4.2f} {(highs[-1] + lows[-1]) / 2:8.3f} V {ripple * 1e3:12.2f} mV {approx * 1e3:16.2f} mV")
    # settling: after a step from 0 to 50% duty, when is the mid-ripple voltage within 1 LSB of 1.65 V?
    highs, lows = rc_filtered_pwm(0.5, tau, periods=int(20 * tau / T))
    settle = next(i for i, (h, l) in enumerate(zip(highs, lows)) if abs((h + l) / 2 - VDD / 2) < VDD / 2**BITS)
    print(f"  after a step to 50% duty it settles within 1 LSB in {(settle + 1) * T * 1e3:.2f} ms")
```

The formula matches the exact solution. With $\tau = 1$ ms the ripple at 50% duty is 13.2 mV, about four LSBs of a 10-bit DAC, and a change of output takes 6.2 ms to settle; with $\tau = 0.1$ ms it settles ten times faster but ripples ten times more. A single RC stage cannot have both. The fixes are a second RC stage or an active filter (ripple falls with the square of the ratio of PWM frequency to cut-off), or a higher PWM frequency with fewer bits. PWM's other weakness is that its accuracy is only as good as the supply, since the output swings between ground and $V_{DD}$. For many jobs — dimming an LED, setting a motor's speed, a slow control voltage — none of this matters, and the motor or the eye is the filter.

</Section>

<Section id="calibration" kind="human" title="Calibration and the professional view">

Every real chain has an **offset** (the reading at zero input is not zero) and a **gain error** (the slope is not exactly right), and many have **nonlinearity** too. **Two-point calibration** measures the raw reading at two known inputs and fits a straight line through them:

$$
\text{value} = \text{ref}_0 + (\text{raw} - \text{raw}_0)\,\frac{\text{ref}_1 - \text{ref}_0}{\text{raw}_1 - \text{raw}_0}.
$$

<Example title="Calibrating a kitchen scale">

A load cell and a 24-bit ADC read 8,192 counts with nothing on the scale and 520,384 counts with a 5,000 g reference mass. The gain is $5000/(520384 - 8192) = 5000/512192 \approx 0.009762$ g per count. A reading of 300,000 counts is then $(300000 - 8192) \times 0.009762 \approx 2848.6$ g. Firmware stores the two calibration constants in flash, and the "tare" button simply re-measures the offset. For a nonlinear sensor such as a thermistor, calibrate at three or more points and fit the Steinhart–Hart equation, or interpolate in a stored lookup table.

</Example>

How good is the 5,000 g mass? Its maker calibrated it against a better one, which was calibrated against a better one still, in an unbroken chain that ends at a national metrology institute — NPL in the UK, NIST in the USA, PTB in Germany, NMIJ in Japan, NIM in China and their peers — and, through the BIPM, at the SI units themselves, which since 2019 have all been defined by fixed constants of nature. That chain, with a stated uncertainty at each link, is **traceability**, and it is what lets a pharmacy in Lagos and a lab in Seoul agree on a milligram.

**The professional view.** A converter's datasheet specifies far more than its bit count: **offset** and **gain error**, **INL** and **DNL** (integral and differential nonlinearity: how far the code transitions are from ideal, and whether any code is missing), SNR, SINAD, ENOB and **SFDR** (spurious-free dynamic range), all over temperature, with the test conditions defined by IEEE 1241. The precision of a system usually depends at least as much on the **voltage reference** as on the converter: a reference that drifts 50 ppm/°C moves a full-scale 16-bit reading by over three LSB per degree. Makers of converters and sensor front ends include Analog Devices and Texas Instruments (USA), STMicroelectronics (France and Italy), Infineon (Germany), Renesas (Japan), Bosch Sensortec (Germany) and TDK InvenSense (Japan and USA) for MEMS motion sensors, Sensirion (Switzerland) for humidity, Melexis (Belgium) for Hall sensors, and ams OSRAM (Austria) for optical sensors. More and more sensors are **smart sensors**: the transducer, conditioning, ADC, calibration constants and a digital bus in one package, talking over the interfaces of <LessonLink slug="buses-and-interfaces">Buses and interfaces</LessonLink>. Robots fuse dozens of them; Lumenary Robotics' [sensing](/en/robotics/sensing/) and [Kalman filter](/en/robotics/kalman-filters/) lessons pick up the story.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A 16-bit ADC gives 16 accurate bits."** It gives 16-bit codes. Noise, nonlinearity, the reference and the circuit around it decide how many of them mean anything; ENOB and the error budget tell you.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Averaging always adds resolution."** Only if the readings differ: a perfectly clean input reads the same code every time and averaging changes nothing. It needs about half an LSB or more of random noise, and it removes only random error, never offset, gain error or drift.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Resolution is accuracy."** The thermistor example resolves 0.02 °C but is accurate to only a few tenths of a degree without calibration. Resolution is the smallest change you can see; accuracy is how close the reading is to the truth.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The ADC measures volts."** It measures the ratio of its input to its reference. With a reference that drifts, every reading drifts; with a ratiometric circuit, the supply's errors cancel.

</Misconception>
