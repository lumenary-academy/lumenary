<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A valve you can turn with a fingertip">

A diode is a valve that the circuit opens and closes by itself, according to which way the voltage points. A **transistor** is a valve with a handle: a third terminal through which a *small* current or voltage controls a *large* current between the other two. That one idea does two very different jobs.

- **Amplifying.** If a microphone's few millivolts move the handle, the large current follows those millivolts faithfully, only bigger. Every radio, phone and audio system amplifies this way.
- **Switching.** If a 3.3 V logic pin moves the handle all the way, the transistor turns a motor, a heater or a light fully on or fully off. And if one transistor's output works the handle of the next, transistors can compute: a modern processor contains tens of billions of them switching billions of times a second.

This lesson explains the two great families, the **bipolar junction transistor** (BJT) and the **metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor** (MOSFET), builds the models engineers calculate with, and then uses transistors as switches: first for an LED, a relay and a motor, and then for each other, in the CMOS inverter that begins the <LessonLink slug="logic-gates-and-boolean-algebra">Digital level</LessonLink>. The <LessonLink slug="transistor-amplifiers">next lesson</LessonLink> uses the same devices as amplifiers.

<Analogy>

Think of a garden tap. Turning the handle takes a tiny effort, yet it controls a flow of water driven by the whole pressure of the mains. The tap does not create water or pressure; it only controls how much passes. A transistor likewise creates no energy: the power supply provides the current, and the transistor decides how much flows.

</Analogy>

</Section>

<Section id="bjt" kind="definitions" title="The bipolar junction transistor">

A BJT is a sandwich of three doped layers: for an **npn** transistor, an n-type **emitter**, a very thin p-type **base**, and an n-type **collector**. (A **pnp** transistor reverses every doping and every polarity.) It is two p–n junctions back to back, but it is *not* two diodes, because the base is so thin, typically well under a micrometre, that the junctions interact.

Forward-bias the base–emitter junction, as in <LessonLink slug="semiconductors-and-diodes">Semiconductors and diodes</LessonLink>, and the heavily doped emitter injects electrons into the base. In a diode they would recombine with holes there. But the base is thin and lightly doped, so almost all of them diffuse across it before recombining, reach the reverse-biased base–collector junction, and are swept into the collector by its field. A small base current, mostly holes supplying the few electrons that *do* recombine, controls a much larger collector current.

<Definition term="BJT equations in the forward-active region">

With the base–emitter junction forward-biased and the base–collector junction reverse-biased, an npn transistor obeys
$$I_C = I_S\, e^{V_{BE}/V_T}, \qquad I_B = \frac{I_C}{\beta}, \qquad I_E = I_C + I_B,$$
where $I_S$ is a saturation current (around $10^{-15}$ to $10^{-14}$ A for a small transistor), $V_T = kT/q \approx 25.9$ mV, and $\beta$ (also written $h_{FE}$) is the **current gain**, typically 100 to 400 for a small-signal transistor.

</Definition>

The collector current is set by $V_{BE}$ through the same exponential as a diode: about 60 mV more on the base multiplies $I_C$ by ten. It is equally true, and more useful for hand design, to say that $I_C = \beta I_B$. But $\beta$ is a poorly controlled number: it varies by a factor of two or three between transistors of the same type, and with temperature and current. A good design works for *any* $\beta$ above some minimum.

The two junctions can each be on or off, giving four **regions of operation**:

| Region | Base–emitter | Base–collector | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cutoff | off | reverse-biased | almost no current: an open switch |
| Forward active | forward-biased | reverse-biased | $I_C = \beta I_B$: an amplifier |
| Saturation | forward-biased | forward-biased | $V_{CE} \approx 0.1$–$0.2$ V: a closed switch |
| Reverse active | reverse-biased | forward-biased | a poor transistor, rarely used |

In **saturation** the base is driven harder than the collector circuit can follow: the collector current is limited by the external load, so $I_C \lt \beta I_B$, and the transistor sits fully on with only $V_{CE(\text{sat})}$ across it. The collector current in the active region also rises slightly with $V_{CE}$, the **Early effect**, which the next lesson uses to find the transistor's output resistance.

<Section id="mosfet" kind="definitions" title="The MOSFET">

A MOSFET controls current with a *voltage* and draws essentially no steady current at its control terminal. An n-channel MOSFET starts with a p-type body. Two heavily doped n-type regions, the **source** and the **drain**, sit a short distance apart, and between them, separated from the silicon by a very thin insulating layer of oxide, lies the **gate**. With no gate voltage, the source and drain are two back-to-back p–n junctions and no current flows.

Raise the gate above the source and the gate's positive charge repels holes from the surface beneath it and attracts electrons. Above a **threshold voltage** $V_{th}$ the surface holds so many electrons that it **inverts** into a thin n-type **channel** joining source to drain. The more the gate exceeds the threshold, by the **overdrive** $V_{ov} = V_{GS} - V_{th}$, the more electrons the channel holds, and the more easily current flows. The gate, oxide and channel form a capacitor, which is why the gate draws current only while that capacitor charges or discharges.

<Definition term="Square-law MOSFET model">

For an n-channel MOSFET with gate–source voltage $V_{GS}$, drain–source voltage $V_{DS} \ge 0$ and overdrive $V_{ov} = V_{GS} - V_{th}$:

- **Cutoff** ($V_{ov} \le 0$): $I_D = 0$.
- **Triode** ($V_{ov} \gt 0$ and $V_{DS} \lt V_{ov}$): $I_D = k\left(V_{ov}V_{DS} - \tfrac12 V_{DS}^2\right)$.
- **Saturation** ($V_{ov} \gt 0$ and $V_{DS} \ge V_{ov}$): $I_D = \tfrac12 k\, V_{ov}^2\,(1 + \lambda V_{DS})$.

Here $k = \mu_n C_{ox} W/L$ depends on the electron mobility $\mu_n$, the oxide capacitance per area $C_{ox}$ and the channel's width $W$ and length $L$; $\lambda$ models **channel-length modulation**, the slight rise of the saturation current with $V_{DS}$.

</Definition>

Two warnings about names. A MOSFET's **saturation** region (current nearly independent of $V_{DS}$) corresponds to a BJT's *active* region, not to BJT saturation; the MOSFET's switch-like region is triode. And a p-channel MOSFET (pMOS) is the mirror image, with holes in the channel, conducting when its gate is *below* its source by more than $|V_{th}|$.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why does the current stop growing with $V_{DS}$? The channel's charge at each point is set by how far the gate is above the *local* channel voltage. Near the drain that difference is $V_{GS} - V_{DS}$; when $V_{DS}$ reaches $V_{ov}$ it falls to the threshold, the channel **pinches off** at the drain end, and any extra drain voltage is dropped across the short pinched region instead of speeding up the channel.

</Narrator>

```python run
VTH = 1.0      # threshold voltage, V
K = 2e-3       # k' W/L, the device's current factor, A/V^2
LAM = 0.02     # channel-length modulation, 1/V

def drain_current(vgs, vds):
    """Square-law (level 1) model of an n-channel MOSFET; returns (current in A, region)."""
    vov = vgs - VTH                                 # overdrive: how far the gate is above threshold
    if vov <= 0:
        return 0.0, "cutoff"
    if vds < vov:                                   # channel continuous from source to drain
        return K * (vov * vds - vds ** 2 / 2) * (1 + LAM * vds), "triode"
    return K / 2 * vov ** 2 * (1 + LAM * vds), "saturation"   # channel pinched off at the drain

vds_list = [0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0]
print("drain current in mA (t = triode, s = saturation, c = cutoff)")
print("V_GS \\ V_DS " + "".join(f"{v:>9.2f}" for v in vds_list))
for vgs in [0.8, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0]:
    cells = []
    for vds in vds_list:
        i, region = drain_current(vgs, vds)
        cells.append(f"{i * 1e3:8.3f}{region[0]}")
    print(f"{vgs:8.1f} V  " + "".join(cells))

print("\nAs a switch: on-resistance near V_DS = 0 is 1 / (K (V_GS - V_TH))")
for vgs in [1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0]:
    print(f"  V_GS = {vgs:.1f} V: R_on = {1 / (K * (vgs - VTH)):6.1f} ohm")
```

(The program multiplies the triode current by $1 + \lambda V_{DS}$ too, as SPICE's level-1 model does, so that the two formulas meet at the boundary.) Read across a row and you see the two regions: in triode the current rises roughly in proportion to $V_{DS}$, as in a resistor, and in saturation it barely changes. Read down the 2 V column, where both are in saturation, and doubling the overdrive from 1 V to 2 V quadruples the current, from 1.040 to 4.160 mA: the square law. Near $V_{DS} = 0$ the transistor is a resistor whose value the gate sets, from 1 kΩ at 1.5 V down to 125 Ω at 5 V, which is why a switch's gate should be driven well above threshold.

### Where the square law stops being true

The square law is a long-channel model, derived for transistors whose channels are many micrometres long. It is excellent for intuition and acceptable for hand design of discrete and older devices, but in the short channels of modern chips several effects take over:

- **Velocity saturation.** In very high fields, electrons stop speeding up and travel at a limiting velocity of about $10^7$ cm/s. The saturation current then grows roughly in proportion to $V_{ov}$, not its square.
- **Short-channel effects.** When source and drain are only tens of nanometres apart, the drain's field reaches under the gate and helps turn the channel on: the threshold falls as $V_{DS}$ rises (**drain-induced barrier lowering**) and as $L$ shrinks (**threshold roll-off**), and the gate loses control.
- **Subthreshold conduction.** Below threshold the current does not drop to zero but falls exponentially, by at best a factor of ten for every 60 mV at room temperature, the same $V_T \ln 10$ as a diode. With billions of transistors, this leakage costs real power.
- **Gate leakage and mobility degradation.** Oxides a few atoms thick leak by quantum tunnelling, which is why chips moved to thicker high-permittivity ("high-k") gate dielectrics, and strong vertical fields slow the electrons.

Industry simulates transistors with **compact models** such as BSIM, developed at Berkeley, with hundreds of parameters fitted to measurements by each chip factory. And it changed the transistor's shape: since the 2010s leading-edge chips have used **FinFETs**, whose gate wraps a thin fin of silicon on three sides, and as of 2026 the most advanced processes have moved to **gate-all-around** nanosheet transistors, whose gate surrounds the channel entirely. <LessonLink slug="transistor-scaling">Transistor scaling</LessonLink> tells that story.

<Section id="switch" kind="examples" title="The transistor as a switch">

A switch should be either fully off (no current, so no power lost) or fully on (little voltage across it, so little power lost). In between, with both current and voltage, a transistor gets hot. So switching design is about driving the transistor firmly into cutoff or firmly into saturation (BJT) or triode (MOSFET).

<Example title="An LED from a 3.3 V logic pin, through an npn transistor">

A microcontroller pin can supply only a few milliamperes, and its 3.3 V may be too low for the load. Put an npn transistor's emitter to ground, the LED and a resistor from the 5 V rail to its collector, and drive its base from the pin through a resistor $R_B$.

- **Collector side.** For 20 mA through a red LED (2.0 V) with $V_{CE(\text{sat})} = 0.2$ V: $R_C = (5 - 2.0 - 0.2)/0.020 = 140\ \Omega$; round *up* to the standard 150 Ω, giving 18.7 mA.
- **Base side.** With a minimum $\beta$ of 100 the base needs at least $18.7/100 = 0.187$ mA. Designers **overdrive** the base, here by a factor of 5, to stay in saturation whatever the actual $\beta$: $I_B \approx 0.93$ mA and $R_B = (3.3 - 0.75)/0.93\ \text{mA} \approx 2.7\ \text{k}\Omega$.

The transistor then dissipates only $18.7\ \text{mA} \times 0.2\ \text{V} \approx 3.7$ mW.

</Example>

For a bigger load the BJT's base current becomes a problem. A 12 V motor drawing 2 A through a BJT in saturation, where designers assume a "forced" $\beta$ of about 10, needs 200 mA of base current, far beyond any logic pin. A MOSFET needs essentially no steady gate current, only brief pulses to charge and discharge its gate capacitance, and a power MOSFET's on-resistance $R_{DS(\text{on})}$ can be a few milliohms. That is why MOSFETs switch almost every load above a few hundred milliamperes. Choose one specified as **logic-level**, meaning its $R_{DS(\text{on})}$ is guaranteed at the gate voltage you actually have (say 2.5 V or 4.5 V); a part whose threshold is "2 V" may still be only half on at 3.3 V.

```python run
import math

E24 = [1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.5, 1.6, 1.8, 2.0, 2.2, 2.4, 2.7, 3.0,
       3.3, 3.6, 3.9, 4.3, 4.7, 5.1, 5.6, 6.2, 6.8, 7.5, 8.2, 9.1]

def e24(r, up):
    """The standard E24 resistor value nearest r, rounding up (up=True) or down."""
    values = [v * 10 ** d for d in range(0, 7) for v in E24]
    return min(v for v in values if v >= r) if up else max(v for v in values if v <= r)

# 1. An NPN transistor lets a 3.3 V logic pin switch a red LED from a 5 V rail.
V_RAIL, V_LED, I_LED, V_CESAT = 5.0, 2.0, 0.020, 0.2
R_C = e24((V_RAIL - V_LED - V_CESAT) / I_LED, up=True)     # round up: never exceed 20 mA
I_C = (V_RAIL - V_LED - V_CESAT) / R_C
BETA_MIN, OVERDRIVE, V_PIN, V_BE = 100, 5, 3.3, 0.75
I_B = OVERDRIVE * I_C / BETA_MIN            # base current: five times the bare minimum
R_B = e24((V_PIN - V_BE) / I_B, up=False)                # round down: a little more drive
print(f"LED resistor {R_C:.0f} ohm -> {I_C * 1e3:.1f} mA; base resistor {R_B:.0f} ohm "
      f"-> {(V_PIN - V_BE) / R_B * 1e3:.2f} mA from the pin")
print(f"transistor dissipation {I_C * V_CESAT * 1e3:.1f} mW")

# 2. A 12 V motor drawing 2 A: compare a BJT with a MOSFET as the switch.
I_M = 2.0
print(f"\nBJT, forced beta 10, V_CE(sat) 0.3 V: base current {I_M / 10 * 1e3:.0f} mA, "
      f"dissipation {I_M * 0.3:.2f} W")
for r_on in (0.050, 0.020, 0.005):
    print(f"MOSFET with R_DS(on) = {r_on * 1e3:.0f} milliohm: gate current ~0 when steady, "
          f"dissipation {I_M ** 2 * r_on:.3f} W")

# 3. A relay coil: what the flyback diode has to deal with.
L, R_COIL, V_COIL, C_STRAY = 20e-3, 70.0, 5.0, 100e-12
i0 = V_COIL / R_COIL
print(f"\ncoil current {i0 * 1e3:.1f} mA, stored energy {0.5 * L * i0 ** 2 * 1e6:.0f} uJ")
print(f"no diode: the current charges the stray capacitance to about "
      f"{i0 * math.sqrt(L / C_STRAY):.0f} V")
print(f"with a diode: the current decays with time constant L/R = {L / R_COIL * 1e6:.0f} us")
```

### Inductive loads and the flyback diode

Relays, solenoids and motors are coils: inductors, as in <LessonLink slug="capacitors-and-inductors">Capacitors and inductors</LessonLink>. An inductor's current cannot change instantly, because $v = L\,di/dt$ would need an infinite voltage. When the transistor turns off, the coil's current keeps flowing and drives the drain voltage up until *something* conducts. With nothing provided, that something is the transistor itself breaking down, or the energy rings in the stray capacitance. The program estimates that the 71 mA in a 20 mH coil would charge 100 pF of stray capacitance to about $i\sqrt{L/C} \approx 1010$ V, from a 5 V supply.

The cure is a **flyback diode** (also called a freewheeling or snubber diode) across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation. At turn-off the coil's current simply transfers into the diode and circulates through it, the drain rises only one diode drop above the supply, and the stored energy, $\tfrac12 L i^2 \approx 51$ µJ here, is dissipated in the coil's own resistance.

ngspice reports 71.1 mA in each coil while on. <Narrator who="gideon">

The 1008 V is the model telling the truth about a circuit that cannot exist: our level-1 MOSFET has no breakdown, but a real 30 V part would **avalanche** at perhaps 35–40 V and absorb the coil's energy in its own junction, repeatedly, until it fails. Note the price of the plain diode too: the slow decay holds the relay closed longer after turn-off. Where release time matters, designers add a Zener in series with the diode, letting the voltage rise to a safe, chosen clamp so the energy is removed faster.

</Narrator>

Three habits complete a robust switch: a **gate resistor** of tens of ohms to tame ringing in the gate wiring, a **pull-down resistor** so the transistor stays off while the controlling pin is still undefined at power-up, and a check of the transistor's dissipation and temperature at the largest load. Keep experiments to small loads from batteries or a current-limited bench supply: a stalled motor can draw several times its running current.

<Section id="cmos" kind="computation" title="Transistors switching transistors: the CMOS inverter">

Put a pMOS transistor between the supply $V_{DD}$ and the output, and an nMOS between the output and ground, with their gates joined as the input. When the input is low, the nMOS is off and the pMOS is on, so the output is pulled up to $V_{DD}$. When the input is high, the nMOS is on and the pMOS off, so the output is pulled down to ground. The output is the logical NOT of the input: an **inverter**, the simplest gate of **complementary MOS** (CMOS) logic.

In either steady state one transistor is off, so no current flows from the supply to ground. That is CMOS's great virtue: a gate consumes power essentially only while it switches, charging and discharging the capacitance it drives. Between the two states there is a narrow region where both transistors conduct. The input voltage at which the output equals the input is the **switching threshold** $V_M$. Setting the two saturation currents equal (and ignoring $\lambda$) gives
$$V_M = \frac{V_{DD} - |V_{tp}| + V_{tn}\sqrt{k_n/k_p}}{1 + \sqrt{k_n/k_p}},$$
so equal transistors make $V_M$ low, because electrons are about twice as mobile as holes and the nMOS wins the tug of war. The program below finds the transfer curve by bisection on the output voltage, using the same level-1 equations as SPICE, for $V_{DD} = 1.8$ V, thresholds of 0.45 V, and transistors of equal size.

```python run
VDD = 1.8
VTN, VTP = 0.45, 0.45          # threshold magnitudes, V
KN, KP = 200e-6, 100e-6        # k' W/L for the nMOS and the (same-sized) pMOS, A/V^2
LAM = 0.05                     # channel-length modulation, 1/V

def square_law(vov, vds, k):
    """Level-1 drain current for overdrive vov and drain-source voltage vds (both >= 0)."""
    if vov <= 0:
        return 0.0
    if vds < vov:
        return k * (vov * vds - vds ** 2 / 2) * (1 + LAM * vds)
    return k / 2 * vov ** 2 * (1 + LAM * vds)

def vout(vin):
    """Output where the pMOS current (pulling up) equals the nMOS current (pulling down)."""
    lo, hi = 0.0, VDD
    for _ in range(60):                      # bisection: the mismatch falls as vout rises
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        i_n = square_law(vin - VTN, mid, KN)
        i_p = square_law(VDD - vin - VTP, VDD - mid, KP)
        if i_p > i_n:
            lo = mid                          # pull-up wins: the output must be higher
        else:
            hi = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

print(" V_in   V_out   (equal-sized transistors)")
for vin in [0.0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.85, 0.9, 1.1, 1.3, 1.5, 1.8]:
    print(f"{vin:5.2f}  {vout(vin):6.3f}")

lo, hi = 0.0, VDD                             # switching threshold: where vout(vin) = vin
for _ in range(50):
    mid = (lo + hi) / 2
    if vout(mid) > mid:
        lo = mid
    else:
        hi = mid
print(f"switching threshold V_M = {lo:.4f} V")
gain = (vout(lo + 1e-4) - vout(lo - 1e-4)) / 2e-4
print(f"slope of the transfer curve at V_M = {gain:.1f}")
```

The output stays within 3 mV of 1.8 V up to an input of 0.5 V, then falls from 1.46 V to 0.24 V as the input moves just 50 mV, from 0.80 to 0.85 V. The slope at the threshold is about −96: a gate is a high-gain amplifier operated at its extremes, which is why logic levels are restored at every stage instead of degrading. The threshold, 0.8236 V, is below $V_{DD}/2$, as the formula predicted. SPICE agrees, and shows the remedy of making the pMOS twice as wide:

```spice check
* Two CMOS inverters, input swept from 0 to 1.8 V
VDD1 vdd1 0 DC 1.8
VDD2 vdd2 0 DC 1.8
VIN in 0 DC 0
* inverter 1: nMOS and pMOS the same size
MP1 out1 in vdd1 vdd1 PMOD W=1u L=1u
MN1 out1 in 0 0 NMOD W=1u L=1u
* inverter 2: pMOS twice as wide, to make up for the holes' lower mobility
MP2 out2 in vdd2 vdd2 PMOD W=2u L=1u
MN2 out2 in 0 0 NMOD W=1u L=1u
* textbook level-1 (square-law) models, not a real process
.model NMOD NMOS(LEVEL=1 VTO=0.45 KP=200u LAMBDA=0.05)
.model PMOD PMOS(LEVEL=1 VTO=-0.45 KP=100u LAMBDA=0.05)
.control
dc VIN 0 1.8 0.001
meas dc vm1 WHEN v(out1)=v(in)
meas dc vm2 WHEN v(out2)=v(in)
let idd2 = -i(VDD2)
meas dc ipeak MAX idd2
meas dc ilow FIND idd2 AT=0.1
meas dc ihigh FIND idd2 AT=1.7
echo "switching threshold: equal sizes $&vm1 V, wider pMOS $&vm2 V"
echo "inverter 2 supply current: $&ilow A (input low), $&ihigh A (input high), peak $&ipeak A"
.endc
.end
```

ngspice finds $V_M$ = 0.823591 V for equal sizes, matching the Python, and exactly 0.9 V, half the supply, with the pMOS twice as wide. The second inverter draws 1.81 pA from the supply with its input low or high (the simulator's tiny built-in leakage conductances) and 21.2 µA at the peak, when the input sits at 0.9 V and both transistors conduct.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Scale that up. A chip with ten billion transistors leaking even 1 nA each would burn 10 A from a 1 V supply doing nothing at all, and real nanometre transistors leak more than a level-1 model admits, through subthreshold conduction. That is why phones switch whole blocks of a chip off, and why leakage is a design constraint as important as speed.

</Narrator>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="From a crystal on a bench to billions on a chip">

The idea of controlling a semiconductor's current with an electric field is older than the transistor: Julius Lilienfeld filed patents on field-effect devices in 1925 and 1926, and Oskar Heil did so in 1934, but nobody could make the surfaces clean enough for them to work. In December 1947, at Bell Labs in New Jersey, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain pressed two gold contacts onto a germanium crystal and saw a signal amplified: the **point-contact transistor**. Within weeks William Shockley, their group's leader, had worked out the more robust **junction transistor**, which was first made in 1950–51. The three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. Transistor radios followed in 1954, the Regency TR-1 in the United States, and in 1955 the TR-55 from a young Japanese company, Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo, soon renamed Sony.

Wiring discrete transistors together became the bottleneck, the "tyranny of numbers". In September 1958 Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments built a working circuit on a single piece of germanium, and in 1959 Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor described a monolithic silicon **integrated circuit** with its wiring deposited on top, made possible by Jean Hoerni's **planar process**, which protected the junctions under a layer of oxide. Kilby received the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics; Noyce had died in 1990.

In 1959, also at Bell Labs, Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng made the first working **MOSFET**, using a thermally grown oxide to tame silicon's surface. It was slower than the BJTs of the day, but it was simpler to make and could be packed far more densely. In 1963 Frank Wanlass and Chih-Tang Sah at Fairchild described **CMOS**, with its near-zero idle power. Those two properties, density and frugality, are why the MOSFET became the most manufactured device in history and the subject of <LessonLink slug="transistor-scaling">Transistor scaling</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Transistors in professional practice">

Discrete transistors are still everywhere on circuit boards, chosen from datasheets published by makers such as Infineon, onsemi, Nexperia, STMicroelectronics, Toshiba, ROHM, Vishay and Diodes Incorporated. For a switching MOSFET an engineer reads first: the maximum drain–source voltage (with margin above any spike), $R_{DS(\text{on})}$ *at the gate voltage actually available*, the gate charge (how much charge the driver must move, which sets switching speed and losses), the thermal resistance from junction to ambient, and the **safe operating area**, the combinations of voltage, current and time the part survives.

Beyond silicon MOSFETs, power engineers use **IGBTs** (a MOS gate controlling a bipolar structure, for hundreds of amperes at kilovolts) and **silicon carbide** and **gallium nitride** transistors, which switch faster with lower losses, now common in electric-vehicle inverters and compact chargers (<LessonLink slug="power-electronics">Power electronics</LessonLink>). BJTs remain the choice where their exponential law is an asset: precise analog circuits, low-noise amplifiers, and the radio-frequency silicon–germanium transistors of high-speed links. Inside chips, nearly everything is CMOS.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A transistor amplifies by creating extra current."** It creates nothing. The power supply provides every joule; the transistor only controls how much current the supply delivers through the load, the way a tap controls water.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"MOSFET saturation and BJT saturation are the same thing."** They are opposites. A BJT in saturation is a closed switch with about 0.2 V across it. A MOSFET in saturation is a current source, the region used for amplifiers; a MOSFET used as a closed switch is in triode.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If the gate voltage is above the threshold, the MOSFET is fully on."** At threshold the channel is barely formed and conducts microamperes. $R_{DS(\text{on})}$ is specified at a much higher gate voltage, often 4.5 V or 10 V; a datasheet's "threshold 2 V" part may be poorly on at 3.3 V.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The flyback diode is optional on a small relay."** Even a small coil stores enough energy to drive a switch's voltage far beyond its rating, as the simulation showed. Without the diode the transistor avalanches on every turn-off and eventually fails.

</Misconception>
