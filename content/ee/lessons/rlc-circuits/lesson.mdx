<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Circuits that ring">

In <LessonLink slug="capacitors-and-inductors">Capacitors and inductors</LessonLink> each energy store was paired with a resistor, and every response was a single exponential: the voltage crept towards its final value with time constant $\tau = RC$ or $L/R$ and never went past it. Put a capacitor *and* an inductor in the same circuit and something new happens. The capacitor's stored charge drives a current through the inductor; the inductor, which resists any change in current, keeps that current flowing after the capacitor is empty and charges it the other way; and the energy sloshes back and forth, a little smaller each time, until the resistance has turned it all into heat.

That sloshing, and its driven counterpart, **resonance**, are everywhere in electrical engineering:

- A radio picks one station out of hundreds because an inductor and a capacitor respond strongly at one frequency and weakly at all others. Wireless phone chargers, contactless payment cards and the coils of MRI scanners are tuned the same way.
- The quartz crystal that keeps time in a watch or a microcontroller behaves electrically like an RLC circuit with an extraordinarily small resistance.
- A fast digital signal on a circuit board *rings* after every edge, overshooting its logic level, because the wires have inductance and the pins have capacitance. Too much ringing corrupts data or damages inputs.
- The inductor and capacitor that smooth the output of a switching power supply form an RLC circuit whose damping decides whether the output voltage settles cleanly after a load change.

All of these obey one second-order differential equation. This lesson derives it, solves it exactly, simulates it numerically, and then drives it with a sinusoid to find resonance.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Energy sloshing between two stores">

Start with no resistor at all: a capacitor charged to some voltage, connected across an inductor. At first the capacitor's energy $\tfrac12 C v^2$ pushes current into the inductor, and the current grows. When the capacitor is empty, all the energy is in the inductor's magnetic field, $\tfrac12 L i^2$, and the current is at its largest. The inductor will not let the current stop suddenly, so it keeps flowing and charges the capacitor with the opposite polarity. Then the whole process runs in reverse. Without resistance this would go on forever, a perfect electrical oscillator at the frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$.

A resistor takes a bite of energy every cycle. A small resistance lets the oscillation ring for many cycles while it decays. A large one damps it so heavily that the voltage never swings past its final value at all. Between the two lies one special resistance, where the circuit settles without overshoot as quickly as any non-overshooting response can.

<Analogy>

A mass on a spring, moving through air, water or honey, is the same system. The inductor is the **mass**: just as a force is needed to change a mass's velocity ($F = m\,dv/dt$), a voltage is needed to change an inductor's current ($v = L\,di/dt$). The capacitor is the **spring**: it stores energy in how far it has been "stretched", which is its charge, and $1/C$ plays the role of stiffness. The resistor is the **friction** of the fluid. In air the mass bounces for a long time; in honey it oozes back to rest without overshooting.

</Analogy>

<Section id="equation" kind="definitions" title="The second-order equation">

Take the series circuit first. Kirchhoff's voltage law round the loop, with the capacitor law $i = C\,dv_C/dt$ substituted for the current, gives

$$
v_s(t) = Ri + L\frac{di}{dt} + v_C
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
LC\,\frac{d^2 v_C}{dt^2} + RC\,\frac{dv_C}{dt} + v_C = v_s(t).
$$

Now the parallel circuit. Kirchhoff's current law at the top node, with $v = L\,di_L/dt$ for the inductor, gives

$$
\frac{v}{R} + i_L + C\frac{dv}{dt} = i_s(t)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
LC\,\frac{d^2 i_L}{dt^2} + \frac{L}{R}\,\frac{di_L}{dt} + i_L = i_s(t).
$$

Both are the same equation in disguise. Dividing by $LC$ puts each into the **standard form** that engineers, physicists and mathematicians all use:

$$
\ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_0\,\dot x + \omega_0^2\,x = \omega_0^2\,u(t),
$$

where $x$ is the capacitor voltage (series) or the inductor current (parallel), $u$ is the source, and the dots mean time derivatives.

<Definition term="Natural frequency, damping ratio, quality factor">

For an equation in standard form, $\omega_0$ is the **undamped natural frequency** (in rad/s; divide by $2\pi$ for hertz), the frequency at which the circuit would oscillate with no resistance. $\zeta$ (zeta) is the **damping ratio**, a pure number. Two more quantities follow from them: the **decay rate** or neper frequency $\alpha = \zeta\omega_0$ (in s⁻¹), and the **quality factor** $Q = 1/(2\zeta)$. For the two circuits:

| | Series RLC | Parallel RLC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| natural frequency $\omega_0$ | $1/\sqrt{LC}$ | $1/\sqrt{LC}$ |
| decay rate $\alpha$ | $R/(2L)$ | $1/(2RC)$ |
| damping ratio $\zeta$ | $\dfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{C}{L}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{2R}\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}}$ |
| quality factor $Q$ | $\dfrac{1}{R}\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}} = \dfrac{\omega_0 L}{R}$ | $R\sqrt{\dfrac{C}{L}} = \omega_0 RC$ |
| more resistance means | more damping | **less** damping |

</Definition>

The quantity $\sqrt{L/C}$ has units of ohms (a henry is a volt-second per ampere, a farad an ampere-second per volt) and is called the circuit's **characteristic impedance**. Damping is a comparison of $R$ with it. In the series circuit, current flows *through* the resistor, so a big $R$ dissipates a lot. In the parallel circuit, a big $R$ is nearly an open circuit that takes almost no current, so it dissipates little, and the circuit rings longer.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Look at what swapped when we went from series to parallel: voltage and current, KVL and KCL, $L$ and $C$, resistance and conductance $1/R$. This is **duality**, and it is why one equation serves both circuits. Whatever you prove about the series capacitor voltage, you have also proved about the parallel inductor current.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="responses" kind="examples" title="Three kinds of response">

To solve the equation we need the roots of its characteristic polynomial, and these may be complex. Electrical engineers write $j$ for the imaginary unit, $j^2 = -1$, because the letter $i$ is already taken by current. (Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.number.complex-numbers">Complex numbers</TopicLink> covers everything used here, including Euler's formula $e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$.)

<Theorem name="The three responses of a second-order circuit">

Let $\omega_0 > 0$ and $\zeta \ge 0$. Every solution of $\ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_0\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0$ has one of three forms, according to $\zeta$. With $\alpha = \zeta\omega_0$:

- **Overdamped**, $\zeta > 1$: $x(t) = A e^{s_1 t} + B e^{s_2 t}$, where $s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}$ are distinct, real and negative.
- **Critically damped**, $\zeta = 1$: $x(t) = (A + Bt)\,e^{-\omega_0 t}$.
- **Underdamped**, $0 \le \zeta < 1$: $x(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\left(A\cos\omega_d t + B\sin\omega_d t\right)$, where $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ is the **damped natural frequency**.

The constants $A$ and $B$ are fixed by $x(0)$ and $\dot x(0)$. If $\zeta > 0$, every solution decays to zero.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Try $x = e^{st}$. Substituting gives $(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0 s + \omega_0^2)\,e^{st} = 0$, so $s$ must be a root of the characteristic polynomial, $s = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}$. If $\zeta > 1$ the roots are real and distinct, and since $\sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2} < \alpha$ both are negative. If $\zeta = 1$ there is one double root $-\omega_0$; because it is a root of the polynomial *and* of its derivative, $t e^{-\omega_0 t}$ is a solution too, as direct substitution confirms. If $\zeta < 1$ the roots are the complex pair $-\alpha \pm j\omega_d$, and by Euler's formula $e^{(-\alpha + j\omega_d)t} = e^{-\alpha t}(\cos\omega_d t + j\sin\omega_d t)$; since the equation has real coefficients, the real and imaginary parts of a solution are themselves solutions. In each case we have two independent solutions, and a linear second-order equation has exactly a two-dimensional family of solutions, one for each choice of $x(0)$ and $\dot x(0)$, so these are all of them. Finally, when $\zeta > 0$ every exponent has a negative real part, so every solution decays.

</Proof>

The driven problem is then easy. Suppose the series circuit is at rest ($v_C = 0$, $i = 0$) and a constant voltage $V$ is switched on at $t = 0$. The final value is $v_C = V$, so $v_C = V + x(t)$ with $x$ from the theorem, and the initial conditions $v_C(0) = 0$ and $\dot v_C(0) = i(0)/C = 0$ fix $A$ and $B$. The three **step responses** are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{overdamped:}\quad & v_C(t) = V\left[1 - \frac{s_2 e^{s_1 t} - s_1 e^{s_2 t}}{s_2 - s_1}\right],\\
\text{critically damped:}\quad & v_C(t) = V\left[1 - (1 + \omega_0 t)\,e^{-\omega_0 t}\right],\\
\text{underdamped:}\quad & v_C(t) = V\left[1 - e^{-\alpha t}\left(\cos\omega_d t + \frac{\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_d t\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Differentiating the underdamped one gives the current, $i(t) = \dfrac{V}{\omega_d L}\,e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega_d t$. It first returns to zero at $t_p = \pi/\omega_d$, and that is where $v_C$ peaks. Substituting $t_p$ gives the **overshoot**, the fraction by which the peak exceeds $V$:

$$
\text{overshoot} = e^{-\alpha\pi/\omega_d} = \exp\!\left(\frac{-\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\right).
$$

It depends on $\zeta$ alone: about 73% at $\zeta = 0.1$, 16% at $\zeta = 0.5$, 4.3% at $\zeta = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707$, and none at $\zeta \ge 1$.

<Example title="A 1 mH, 1 µF series circuit">

Take $L = 1\ \text{mH}$ and $C = 1\ \mu\text{F}$, driven by a 5 V step. Then $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{10^{-9}} = 31\,623$ rad/s, so $f_0 = 5033$ Hz, and $\sqrt{L/C} = 31.6\ \Omega$. Critical damping ($\zeta = 1$) needs $R = 2\sqrt{L/C} = 63.2\ \Omega$.

With $R = 20\ \Omega$: $\zeta = 20/63.2 = 0.316$, so the circuit is underdamped. The decay rate is $\alpha = R/(2L) = 10\,000\ \text{s}^{-1}$, and $\omega_d^2 = \omega_0^2 - \alpha^2 = 10^9 - 10^8 = 9 \times 10^8$, so $\omega_d = 30\,000$ rad/s exactly, a ringing frequency of $30\,000/2\pi = 4775$ Hz, about 5% below $f_0$. The voltage peaks at $t_p = \pi/30\,000 = 104.7\ \mu\text{s}$ with overshoot $e^{-\pi/3} = 0.351$, so it reaches $5 \times 1.351 = 6.75$ V before settling back. The ringing envelope $e^{-\alpha t}$ falls below 2% when $\alpha t = \ln 50 \approx 3.9$, about 390 µs after the step.

With $R = 200\ \Omega$: $\zeta = 3.16$, overdamped. The roots are $s_1 = -5132\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $s_2 = -194\,868\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The fast root dies out within a few microseconds; the slow one, with time constant $1/5132 \approx 195\ \mu\text{s}$, sets how long you wait.

</Example>

<Section id="simulation" kind="computation" title="Simulating the circuit">

Exact solutions exist only for simple circuits. Add a diode, a second inductor or a time-varying source and you must integrate numerically, which is what every circuit simulator does. To integrate, write the second-order equation as two first-order ones in the **state** $(v_C, i)$, the two quantities that hold the circuit's energy:

$$
\frac{dv_C}{dt} = \frac{i}{C}, \qquad \frac{di}{dt} = \frac{v_s - Ri - v_C}{L}.
$$

The program below integrates these with the classical fourth-order **Runge–Kutta** method (RK4; see Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.comp.numerical-methods">Numerical methods</TopicLink>), which samples the slope four times per step and combines the samples so that the error per unit time shrinks like $h^4$ in the step $h$. It runs the 1 mH, 1 µF circuit at the three resistances of the figure and checks every point against the exact formulas.

```python run
import numpy as np

L, C, V = 1e-3, 1e-6, 5.0          # 1 mH, 1 uF, and a 5 V step at t = 0
w0 = 1 / np.sqrt(L * C)
R_crit = 2 * np.sqrt(L / C)

def exact(R, t):
    """Capacitor voltage of a series RLC after a step, starting from rest."""
    a = R / (2 * L)                                    # alpha = zeta * w0
    if np.isclose(a, w0):                              # critically damped
        return V * (1 - (1 + w0 * t) * np.exp(-w0 * t))
    if a > w0:                                         # overdamped: two real roots
        r = np.sqrt(a * a - w0 * w0)
        s1, s2 = -a + r, -a - r
        return V * (1 - (s2 * np.exp(s1 * t) - s1 * np.exp(s2 * t)) / (s2 - s1))
    wd = np.sqrt(w0 * w0 - a * a)                      # underdamped: complex pair
    return V * (1 - np.exp(-a * t) * (np.cos(wd * t) + a / wd * np.sin(wd * t)))

def rk4(R, h, t_end):
    """Integrate the state x = [v_C, i] with the classical Runge-Kutta method."""
    def f(x):
        v, i = x
        return np.array([i / C, (V - R * i - v) / L])
    n = round(t_end / h)
    x = np.zeros(2)
    vs = [0.0]
    for _ in range(n):
        k1 = f(x)
        k2 = f(x + h / 2 * k1)
        k3 = f(x + h / 2 * k2)
        k4 = f(x + h * k3)
        x = x + h / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)
        vs.append(x[0])
    return np.arange(n + 1) * h, np.array(vs)

print(f"w0 = {w0:.0f} rad/s (f0 = {w0 / (2 * np.pi):.1f} Hz), critical R = {R_crit:.2f} ohm\n")
cases = [("overdamped", 200.0), ("critical", R_crit), ("underdamped", 20.0)]
runs = {name: rk4(R, 1e-6, 1e-3) for name, R in cases}

print("t (us)  " + "".join(f"{name:>13}" for name, _ in cases))
for k in (0, 25, 50, 100, 150, 200, 300, 400, 600, 1000):
    print(f"{k:6d}  " + "".join(f"{runs[name][1][k]:11.4f} V" for name, _ in cases))
print("\nlargest |RK4 - exact| over 1 ms:")
for name, R in cases:
    t, v = runs[name]
    print(f"  {name:12} zeta = {R / R_crit:5.3f}   {np.abs(v - exact(R, t)).max():.1e} V")

# Fourth order: halving the step should divide the error by about 2**4 = 16.
print()
for h in (20e-6, 10e-6, 5e-6, 2.5e-6):
    t, v = rk4(20.0, h, 1e-3)
    print(f"underdamped, h = {h * 1e6:4.1f} us: max error = {np.abs(v - exact(20.0, t)).max():.2e} V")
```

The table tells the same story as the figure. At 100 µs the underdamped circuit is already at 6.73 V, past its target; the critically damped one is at 4.12 V and closing in without overshoot; the overdamped one has reached only 1.93 V and is still 0.03 V short after a full millisecond. RK4 with a 1 µs step agrees with the exact solutions to better than a microvolt. The last four lines show the fourth order directly: each halving of the step divides the error by about 16 (from $8.97 \times 10^{-3}$ to $5.44 \times 10^{-4}$ to $3.28 \times 10^{-5}$ to $2.02 \times 10^{-6}$ V).

<Narrator who="margaret">

Notice which case has the *largest* error: the overdamped one, which looks the gentlest. Its fast root $s_2 = -194\,868\ \text{s}^{-1}$ means the step must be small compared with $1/|s_2| \approx 5\ \mu\text{s}$, while its slow root means you must simulate for a long time. A system with time scales this far apart is called **stiff**, and it is one reason circuit simulators such as SPICE integrate with implicit methods (the trapezoidal rule and Gear's methods), which stay stable with much larger steps.

</Narrator>

### Checking with SPICE

SPICE, the circuit simulator first written at Berkeley in the early 1970s, is how engineers check this kind of calculation. Its descendants, such as the open-source **ngspice** used here, read a **netlist**: one line per component, naming the nodes it connects. The netlist below applies a 5 V step (rising in 10 ns) to the $R = 20\ \Omega$ circuit, runs a transient analysis for 1 ms, and measures the peak, the time between the first and third crossings of 5 V (one full ringing period), and the final value.

```spice check
* Series RLC step response: 5 V step, R = 20 ohm, L = 1 mH, C = 1 uF
V1 in 0 PWL(0 0 10n 5)
R1 in a 20
L1 a b 1m
C1 b 0 1u
.control
tran 0.1u 1m 0 0.1u
meas tran vpeak MAX v(b)
meas tran tcross1 WHEN v(b)=5 CROSS=1
meas tran tcross3 WHEN v(b)=5 CROSS=3
let fd = 1/(tcross3 - tcross1)
print fd
meas tran vend FIND v(b) AT=1m
.endc
.end
```

ngspice prints (among its other messages):

```text
vpeak               =  6.754601e+00 at=  1.046918e-04
tcross1             =  6.308993e-05
tcross3             =  2.725296e-04
fd = 4.774645e+03
vend                =  5.000040e+00
```

A peak of 6.7546 V at 104.7 µs and a ringing frequency of 4774.6 Hz, exactly as the hand calculation and the RK4 program predicted (the hand values are 6.7545 V, 104.7 µs and $30\,000/2\pi = 4774.6$ Hz).

<Section id="resonance" kind="examples" title="Resonance and Q">

So far the source was a step. Now drive the series circuit with a sinusoid, $v_s = V\cos\omega t$, and wait for the transient to die away. What remains is the **sinusoidal steady state**: every voltage and current oscillates at the source's frequency $\omega$, and only the amplitudes and timings are left to find.

A short calculation finds them, using a trick that the next lesson, <LessonLink slug="ac-and-phasors">AC circuits, phasors and impedance</LessonLink>, turns into a general method. Since $\cos\omega t = \operatorname{Re} e^{j\omega t}$, look for a solution $v_C = \operatorname{Re}(A e^{j\omega t})$ with a complex constant $A$. Each time derivative just multiplies $e^{j\omega t}$ by $j\omega$, so the differential equation becomes an algebraic one:

$$
\left(1 - \omega^2 LC + j\omega RC\right)A = V
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|A| = \frac{V}{\sqrt{(1 - \omega^2 LC)^2 + (\omega RC)^2}}.
$$

The current is $i = C\,dv_C/dt$, of amplitude $\omega C|A|$, which simplifies to

$$
|I| = \frac{V}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}}.
$$

At $\omega = \omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, the term $\omega L - 1/(\omega C)$ vanishes: the inductor's and the capacitor's opposition to current cancel exactly, the current reaches its maximum $V/R$, limited only by the resistance, and it is in step with the source.

<Definition term="Resonance, bandwidth, quality factor">

A circuit is at **resonance** when it is driven at the frequency where its inductive and capacitive effects cancel; for the series and parallel RLC circuits this is $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$. The **half-power points** are the frequencies $\omega_1 < \omega_0 < \omega_2$ at which the response amplitude has fallen to $1/\sqrt2$ of its peak, so the power delivered has halved. The **bandwidth** is $\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1$, and the **quality factor** is

$$
Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega}.
$$

</Definition>

For the series circuit, setting $|\omega L - 1/(\omega C)| = R$ gives a quadratic in $\omega$ whose positive roots differ by exactly $R/L$, so $Q = \omega_0 L/R$, matching the table above, and $Q = 1/(2\zeta)$. The same Q has three other faces, all worth knowing:

- **Energy:** $Q = 2\pi \times \dfrac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy dissipated per cycle}}$ at resonance. A high-Q circuit loses only a small fraction of its energy each cycle.
- **Ring-down:** left to itself, a circuit's oscillation amplitude falls by a factor $e^{-\pi} \approx 0.04$ in about Q cycles. A bell with a high Q rings for a long time.
- **Magnification:** at resonance the capacitor voltage has amplitude $|V_C| = |I|/(\omega_0 C) = V/(\omega_0 RC) = QV$, and the inductor voltage has the same amplitude in the opposite direction. The two cancel in the loop, but each component really sees Q times the source voltage.

The program below checks all of this the way you would on a bench: it drives the 1 mH, 1 µF circuit, now with only 2 Ω of resistance, at nine frequencies, simulates 12 ms with RK4 until the transient has decayed, and records the largest current and capacitor voltage in the last 2 ms.

```python run
import math

L, C, R = 1e-3, 1e-6, 2.0          # the same L and C, now with only 2 ohm of resistance
V = 1.0                            # source amplitude: v_s(t) = 1 V * sin(w t)
w0 = 1 / math.sqrt(L * C)

def simulate(w, t_end=12e-3, h=1e-6):
    """RK4 on the driven circuit; return the largest |i| and |v_C| in the last 2 ms."""
    def f(t, v, i):
        return i / C, (V * math.sin(w * t) - R * i - v) / L
    t = v = i = 0.0
    i_max = v_max = 0.0
    for n in range(round(t_end / h)):
        a1, b1 = f(t, v, i)
        a2, b2 = f(t + h / 2, v + h / 2 * a1, i + h / 2 * b1)
        a3, b3 = f(t + h / 2, v + h / 2 * a2, i + h / 2 * b2)
        a4, b4 = f(t + h, v + h * a3, i + h * b3)
        v += h / 6 * (a1 + 2 * a2 + 2 * a3 + a4)
        i += h / 6 * (b1 + 2 * b2 + 2 * b3 + b4)
        t += h
        if t > t_end - 2e-3:                       # the transient has died away by now
            i_max, v_max = max(i_max, abs(i)), max(v_max, abs(v))
    return i_max, v_max

def formula(w):
    """Steady-state amplitudes from the complex-exponential trial solution."""
    i_amp = V / math.sqrt(R**2 + (w * L - 1 / (w * C))**2)
    return i_amp, i_amp / (w * C)

print(f"f0 = {w0 / (2 * math.pi):.1f} Hz,  Q = w0 L / R = {w0 * L / R:.2f}\n")
print("   f (Hz)   |i| sim (mA) formula   |v_C| sim (V) formula")
for ratio in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0, 1.05, 1.1, 1.25, 2.0):
    w = ratio * w0
    (i_s, v_s), (i_f, v_f) = simulate(w), formula(w)
    print(f"{w / (2 * math.pi):9.1f}   {1e3 * i_s:8.2f} {1e3 * i_f:8.2f}   {v_s:10.3f} {v_f:8.3f}")

# Half-power (-3 dB) points of the current: where |i| falls to 1/sqrt(2) of its peak.
a = R / (2 * L)
w_lo, w_hi = math.sqrt(a**2 + w0**2) - a, math.sqrt(a**2 + w0**2) + a
print(f"\nhalf-power points: {w_lo / (2 * math.pi):.1f} Hz and {w_hi / (2 * math.pi):.1f} Hz")
print(f"bandwidth = {(w_hi - w_lo) / (2 * math.pi):.1f} Hz,  f0 / bandwidth = {w0 / (w_hi - w_lo):.2f}")
```

The simulation and the formula agree to within one unit in the last digit printed. At resonance a 1 V source pushes 500 mA through the circuit, limited only by the 2 Ω, and the capacitor swings to 15.81 V, which is Q = 15.81 times the source. Only 20% below resonance, at $0.8 f_0$, the current is already down to 70 mA. The half-power points sit at 4876.3 Hz and 5194.6 Hz, a bandwidth of 318.3 Hz, and $f_0$ divided by that bandwidth is again 15.81.

Notice also that the currents at $0.5 f_0$ and $2 f_0$ are identical (21.06 mA), and so are those at $0.8 f_0$ and $1.25 f_0$. Replace $\omega$ by $\omega_0^2/\omega$ and the reactance $\omega L - 1/(\omega C)$ only changes sign, so $|I|$ is unchanged: the current response is symmetric on a *logarithmic* frequency axis, which is one reason engineers plot frequency responses that way, as <LessonLink slug="frequency-response-and-filters">Frequency response, Bode plots and filters</LessonLink> will.

<Example title="Tuning a medium-wave radio">

Medium-wave AM broadcasting spans roughly 530 to 1700 kHz, with stations 9 kHz apart in most of the world and 10 kHz apart in the Americas. A receiver's first tuned circuit with a 220 µH coil reaches 1 MHz with $C = 1/(\omega^2 L) = 115$ pF. To sweep from 531 kHz to 1602 kHz the variable capacitor must run from 408 pF down to 45 pF: since $f_0 \propto 1/\sqrt C$, a frequency ratio of about 3 needs a capacitance ratio of about 9. For a bandwidth of one channel, 9 kHz at 1 MHz, the circuit needs $Q = 10^6/9000 \approx 111$. A single coil-and-capacitor circuit can just about manage that, which is why early receivers had several tuned stages, and why nearly every receiver since the 1930s has used the **superheterodyne** design that does most of its filtering at one fixed frequency (more in <LessonLink slug="modulation">Modulation</LessonLink>).

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

Magnification is a safety and reliability condition, not a curiosity. A circuit with Q = 50 driven at 10 V puts about 500 V across its capacitor at resonance, even though nothing in it is labelled 500 V. Size component voltage ratings for Q times the drive, and do your own resonance experiments in simulation or at a few volts from a signal generator.

</Narrator>

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="Where RLC circuits appear">

**Tuned circuits.** Besides radio receivers, resonant LC circuits set the operating frequency of contactless cards and NFC tags (13.56 MHz), of inductive phone chargers built to the Wireless Power Consortium's Qi standard (of the order of 100 kHz), and of the radio-frequency coils of MRI scanners, which must resonate at the frequency at which hydrogen nuclei precess in the magnet: about 64 MHz in a 1.5-tesla scanner. In each, the designer chooses Q: high enough to select the right frequency or transfer power efficiently, low enough that component tolerances and temperature drift do not detune it.

**Crystals and resonators.** A quartz crystal vibrates mechanically, and through the piezoelectric effect it looks to the circuit like a series RLC (with a capacitor in parallel) whose Q is in the tens of thousands or more. That is why a 32.768 kHz watch crystal keeps time to seconds per month. <LessonLink slug="feedback-and-oscillators">Feedback and oscillators</LessonLink> shows how an amplifier keeps such a resonator ringing forever.

**Ringing on digital lines.** A logic output switching in a fraction of a nanosecond drives the inductance of its package and the trace, into the capacitance of the trace and the receiving pins. As long as the trace is short compared with the distance a signal travels during the edge, this is a lumped series RLC circuit, and its resistance is just the driver's output resistance, often far below critical. The cure is a small **series termination resistor** next to the driver. The program below models a 3.3 V output with 10 Ω of drive resistance, 10 nH of inductance and 10 pF of load, and tries standard resistor values.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A 3.3 V logic output driving a short trace and a few input pins, as a lumped series RLC.
L = 10e-9          # package lead, bond wire and trace inductance: 10 nH
C = 10e-12         # trace and input-pin capacitance: 10 pF
R_drv = 10.0       # the driver's own output resistance
V = 3.3

w0 = 1 / np.sqrt(L * C)
R_crit = 2 * np.sqrt(L / C)
print(f"f0 = {w0 / 2 / np.pi / 1e6:.0f} MHz; critical damping needs {R_crit:.1f} ohm in total\n")

def step(R, t):
    a = R / (2 * L)
    if np.isclose(a, w0):
        return V * (1 - (1 + w0 * t) * np.exp(-w0 * t))
    if a > w0:
        r = np.sqrt(a * a - w0 * w0)
        s1, s2 = -a + r, -a - r
        return V * (1 - (s2 * np.exp(s1 * t) - s1 * np.exp(s2 * t)) / (s2 - s1))
    wd = np.sqrt(w0 * w0 - a * a)
    return V * (1 - np.exp(-a * t) * (np.cos(wd * t) + a / wd * np.sin(wd * t)))

t = np.linspace(0, 20e-9, 200001)      # 0 to 20 ns in 0.1 ps steps
print("added R   zeta  peak (V)  overshoot  10-90% rise  within 2% after")
for R_s in (0, 10, 22, 33, 47, R_crit - R_drv, 68):
    R = R_drv + R_s
    v = step(R, t)
    zeta = R / R_crit
    rise = t[np.argmax(v >= 0.9 * V)] - t[np.argmax(v >= 0.1 * V)]
    outside = np.nonzero(np.abs(v - V) > 0.02 * V)[0]
    settle = t[outside[-1] + 1]
    over = max(0.0, 100 * (v.max() / V - 1))
    print(f"{R_s:5.1f} ohm {zeta:5.2f}  {v.max():7.2f}  {over:7.1f} %  "
          f"{rise * 1e9:7.2f} ns  {settle * 1e9:9.2f} ns")
```

With no added resistor, $\zeta = 0.16$ and the 3.3 V output rings at 503 MHz up to 5.30 V, a 60.5% overshoot that would exceed the absolute maximum input voltage of most 3.3 V parts and could be read as two edges instead of one. A 33 Ω resistor brings the overshoot to 5.4%; 47 Ω ($\zeta = 0.90$) to 0.1%. Each step up slows the rise (from 0.37 ns to 1.44 ns across the table), the price of damping. Real designs predict this with simulators fed by the chip makers' IBIS models of their output buffers. When a trace is long enough that the edge is over before the signal reaches the far end, the lumped model fails and the trace must be treated as a **transmission line** (<LessonLink slug="transmission-lines">Transmission lines</LessonLink>), but the cure, a resistor matched to the line, is close kin to this one.

**LC filters in power supplies.** A switching regulator chops its input into pulses at hundreds of kilohertz and smooths them with an inductor and a capacitor, a second-order low-pass filter whose damping comes mostly from the load and the capacitor's small internal resistance. Too little damping and the output rings after every load step; an input filter with too little damping can even interact with the regulator and make it oscillate, a problem analysed in a classic 1976 paper by R. D. Middlebrook. <LessonLink slug="power-electronics">Power electronics</LessonLink> returns to this.

**Power distribution on chips and boards.** A processor's supply is decoupled by many capacitors, each with a little series inductance, connected through the inductance of the package and the board. Between them these form parallel resonances, and at those frequencies the supply's impedance can peak, letting current pulses from the chip shake the supply voltage. Designers choose capacitor values and their small series resistances to keep the impedance below a target across the whole band; <LessonLink slug="printed-circuit-boards">Printed circuit boards</LessonLink> shows how.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Measured ringing is not always in the circuit. An oscilloscope probe with a 15 cm ground clip adds about 150 nH of inductance in series with roughly 10 pF of probe capacitance, a resonance near 130 MHz. Fast edges will make *that* ring on the screen, however clean the real signal is. Before you add a damping resistor, measure again with the shortest ground connection you can.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Critical damping always settles fastest."** It is the fastest response that *never* overshoots. If a small overshoot is acceptable, a slightly underdamped circuit gets inside a tolerance band sooner: in the digital-line table, $\zeta = 0.90$ settled within 2% after 1.49 ns, and critical damping took 1.84 ns. That is why control and filter designers often aim for $\zeta$ between about 0.6 and 0.9 rather than exactly 1.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"At resonance the current becomes infinite."** Only in a circuit with no resistance, which does not exist. At resonance the inductor and capacitor cancel each other, so the current is limited by the resistance alone, $V/R$. What *does* become large is the voltage across the inductor and the capacitor separately, Q times the source.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A higher Q is always better."** A high Q means a narrow bandwidth, a long ring-down and great sensitivity to component values. With Q = 100 the bandwidth is 1% of $f_0$, but a 5% capacitor shifts $f_0$ by about 2.5%, so the circuit may miss the frequency it was meant to select. Filters, sensors and power circuits are often designed for a deliberately modest Q.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Adding resistance always adds damping."** Only in series. In the parallel circuit, $\zeta = \frac{1}{2R}\sqrt{L/C}$, so a larger resistance means *less* damping, and an open circuit ($R \to \infty$) means none.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The ringing frequency is the resonant frequency."** They differ: a step response rings at $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, which was 4775 Hz against $f_0 = 5033$ Hz in our example. (The peak of the capacitor-voltage response to a sinusoid lies lower again, at $\omega_0\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$.) All three coincide only as $\zeta \to 0$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

In 1853 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, analysed the discharge of a Leyden jar (an early capacitor) through a coil and showed that when the resistance is small enough the discharge oscillates, which is the underdamped case of this lesson. A few years later Wilhelm Feddersen photographed such sparks in a rotating mirror and saw the oscillations. Heinrich Hertz used spark-driven resonant circuits in 1886–88 to generate and detect the electromagnetic waves Maxwell had predicted. Within a decade Oliver Lodge and Guglielmo Marconi were patenting ways to tune transmitters and receivers to each other so that several stations could share the air, and the tuned LC circuit became the heart of radio. The same mathematics describes swinging pendulums, ringing bells and vibrating bridges, and Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.analysis.ordinary-differential-equations">Ordinary differential equations</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.applied.classical-mechanics">Classical mechanics</TopicLink> treat those. Robotics makes the analogy run the other way: [impedance control](/en/robotics/force-and-impedance-control/) deliberately makes a robot arm behave like a chosen mass, spring and damper.
