<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="From a specification to a circuit">

A **combinational** circuit is a network of gates with no loops: its outputs depend only on its present inputs. Adders, comparators, decoders, the arithmetic logic unit (ALU) at the heart of a processor, the logic that turns a key code into a character on a display: all combinational. The previous lesson, <LessonLink slug="logic-gates-and-boolean-algebra">Logic gates and Boolean algebra</LessonLink>, gave you the gates and the algebra. This one is about *design*: given what a circuit must do, find a good circuit that does it.

"Good" has three measures that pull against each other. **Area**: fewer and smaller gates cost less silicon or fewer FPGA resources. **Delay**: the slowest path from an input to an output limits the clock rate. **Power**: every gate that switches spends energy, and so does every glitch. In industry a synthesis tool does most of the searching, so why learn to do it by hand? Because a designer who knows what the tool is doing can read its reports, write descriptions it can optimise, spot when it has been asked something impossible, and fix the problems tools do not see, such as the glitches later in this lesson.

</Section>

<Section id="canonical" kind="definitions" title="Canonical forms and implicants">

Number the rows of an $n$-input truth table $0$ to $2^n - 1$ by reading the inputs as a binary number, first input most significant.

<Definition term="Minterm, maxterm, canonical forms">

A **minterm** is an AND of all $n$ inputs, each complemented or not, that is 1 on exactly one row: for inputs $A, B, C$, row 5 ($101$) has minterm $m_5 = A\overline{B}C$. A **maxterm** is an OR of all $n$ inputs that is 0 on exactly one row: $M_5 = \overline{A} + B + \overline{C}$. Every function is the OR of the minterms of its 1-rows, its **canonical sum of products**, written $f = \sum m(\ldots)$; and the AND of the maxterms of its 0-rows, its **canonical product of sums**, $f = \prod M(\ldots)$.

</Definition>

The three-input majority function of the previous lesson is $\sum m(3, 5, 6, 7) = \prod M(0, 1, 2, 4)$. Canonical forms are unique, which makes them good for comparing functions, but wasteful as circuits: majority needs four 3-input ANDs in canonical form, against three 2-input ANDs as $AB + AC + BC$.

Often some input combinations can never happen, or their output does not matter. These are **don't-cares**, written $d(\ldots)$: the designer may choose 0 or 1 for each, whichever makes the circuit smaller.

<Definition term="Implicant, prime implicant, essential prime implicant">

A product term $P$ is an **implicant** of $f$ if $f = 1$ wherever $P = 1$ (counting don't-care rows as allowed). It is a **prime implicant** if removing any literal from it stops it being an implicant: it cannot be made any bigger. A prime implicant is **essential** if it is the only prime implicant covering some 1-row of $f$.

</Definition>

<Theorem name="Minimal sums of products use only primes">

Some sum of products with the fewest literals for $f$ consists only of prime implicants.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take any minimal sum of products. If one of its terms $P$ is not prime, remove literals from $P$ until it is a prime implicant $P'$. $P'$ covers every row $P$ covered and is still an implicant, so the sum still equals $f$ on every row that matters, and it has fewer or equally many literals. Repeat for every term.

</Proof>

So minimisation is two problems: **find all the prime implicants**, then **choose the fewest (and cheapest) that cover every 1**. Karnaugh maps solve both by eye for small functions; the Quine–McCluskey method solves them by computation.

</Section>

<Section id="kmaps" kind="examples" title="Karnaugh maps">

A **Karnaugh map** (K-map) is a truth table folded into a grid so that rows differing in one input sit next to each other. The trick is the order of the row and column labels: **Gray code**, 00, 01, 11, 10, in which neighbours differ in exactly one bit. The grid also wraps around: the left column neighbours the right one, and the top row the bottom one. Two adjacent 1-cells differ in one variable $X$, so their minterms combine as $PX + P\overline{X} = P$, and the variable disappears. A rectangle of $2^k$ cells, whose sides are powers of two, is a product term with $k$ fewer literals.

<Example title="Segment e of a seven-segment display">

A BCD (binary-coded decimal) to seven-segment decoder receives a digit 0–9 on four inputs $ABCD$ ($A$ most significant) and lights the segments a–g of a display. Inputs 10 to 15 never occur, so they are don't-cares. Segment e, the lower-left bar, is lit for the digits 0, 2, 6 and 8:

$$e = \sum m(0, 2, 6, 8) + d(10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15).$$

Place the 1s and the don't-cares (×) on the map below. Two groups cover all the 1s:

- the whole column $CD = 10$: cells 2, 6, 14 and 10. $C = 1$ and $D = 0$ throughout, while $A$ and $B$ take every value, so the term is $C\overline{D}$;
- the four corners: cells 0, 2, 8 and 10, adjacent through the wrap-around. Here $B = 0$ and $D = 0$, giving $\overline{B}\,\overline{D}$.

So $e = C\overline{D} + \overline{B}\,\overline{D} = \overline{D}(\overline{B} + C)$: two 2-input gates and an OR, or three gates in factored form, instead of four 4-input minterms. Both groups used don't-cares (cells 10 and 14) to grow larger; a designer who set the don't-cares to 0 would need bigger terms.

</Example>

The rules for reading a map: make every group a rectangle of 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 cells (wrapping allowed); make each group as large as possible (that makes it prime); use as few groups as possible, starting with 1s that only one group can cover (those groups are essential); include don't-cares only when they help. Grouping the 0s instead gives a minimal product of sums.

<Narrator who="chloe">

What is a K-map, really? The $2^n$ rows of a truth table are the corners of an $n$-dimensional cube, with edges between rows that differ in one bit, and a product term with $k$ missing literals is a $k$-dimensional face of that cube. A four-variable K-map is the 4-cube drawn on a torus, which is why the edges wrap; at five or six variables the cube no longer flattens onto a grid, and the method falls apart.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="quine-mccluskey" kind="computation" title="Quine–McCluskey: minimisation by computation">

Willard Quine (1952) and Edward McCluskey (1956) turned the map into an algorithm a computer can run for any number of variables.

1. Write each 1 and don't-care as a binary string, such as `0110`.
2. **Merge** any two strings that differ in exactly one position, writing a dash there: `0110` and `0010` merge into `-010`. Repeat with the merged strings (dashes must line up). A string that never merges with anything is a **prime implicant**.
3. **Cover**: choose the fewest primes such that every 1 (not every don't-care) is covered by at least one of them.

The program below implements both steps. For the covering step it simply tries all choices of 1 prime, then 2, and so on, which is fine for the handful of primes a four-input function has. It then minimises all seven segments of the decoder.

```python run
from itertools import combinations

def combine(t1, t2):
    """Merge two terms that differ in exactly one fixed position: '0110' + '0100' -> '01-0'."""
    diff = [i for i in range(len(t1)) if t1[i] != t2[i]]
    if len(diff) == 1 and "-" not in (t1[diff[0]], t2[diff[0]]):
        i = diff[0]
        return t1[:i] + "-" + t1[i + 1:]
    return None

def prime_implicants(ones, dont_cares, n):
    """Quine-McCluskey, step 1: merge terms until no more merges are possible."""
    terms = {format(m, f"0{n}b") for m in ones + dont_cares}
    primes = set()
    while terms:
        merged, used = set(), set()
        for t1, t2 in combinations(sorted(terms), 2):
            t = combine(t1, t2)
            if t:
                merged.add(t)
                used |= {t1, t2}
        primes |= terms - used          # a term that merged with nothing is prime
        terms = merged
    return sorted(primes)

def covers(term, m):
    bits = format(m, f"0{len(term)}b")
    return all(t in ("-", b) for t, b in zip(term, bits))

def literals(term):
    return len(term) - term.count("-")

def minimum_cover(primes, ones):
    """Step 2: the fewest primes covering every 1 (ties: fewest literals). Exhaustive, fine for small n."""
    for k in range(1, len(primes) + 1):
        options = [c for c in combinations(primes, k) if all(any(covers(p, m) for p in c) for m in ones)]
        if options:
            return min(options, key=lambda c: sum(literals(p) for p in c))
    return ()

def expression(cover, names="ABCD"):
    terms = ["".join(v + ("'" if b == "0" else "") for v, b in zip(names, t) if b != "-") or "1" for t in cover]
    return " + ".join(terms) or "0"

# A BCD to seven-segment decoder: inputs ABCD (A most significant) hold a digit 0-9;
# the codes 10-15 never occur, so they are don't-cares.
DIGITS = {  # segments lit:  a b c d e f g
    0: "1111110", 1: "0110000", 2: "1101101", 3: "1111001", 4: "0110011",
    5: "1011011", 6: "1011111", 7: "1110000", 8: "1111111", 9: "1111011"}
DONT_CARE = list(range(10, 16))

ones_e = [d for d in range(10) if DIGITS[d][4] == "1"]
primes_e = prime_implicants(ones_e, DONT_CARE, 4)
print("segment e is lit for digits", ones_e)
print("its prime implicants:", primes_e, "=", expression(primes_e).replace(" + ", ", "))
print("minimum cover: e =", expression(minimum_cover(primes_e, ones_e)))
print()
total = 0
for k, seg in enumerate("abcdefg"):
    ones = [d for d in range(10) if DIGITS[d][k] == "1"]
    best = minimum_cover(prime_implicants(ones, DONT_CARE, 4), ones)
    total += sum(literals(t) for t in best)
    print(f"{seg} = {expression(best)}")
print("literals in all seven:", total)
```

Segment e has five prime implicants, $C\overline{D}$, $\overline{B}\,\overline{D}$, $A\overline{D}$, $AC$ and $AB$, but the last three cover only don't-cares and one already-covered 1, so the minimum cover is the pair we found by hand. The whole decoder needs 42 literals across its seven outputs. A real implementation would also *share* terms between outputs: $C\overline{D}$ appears in d, e and g, and $\overline{B}\,\overline{D}$ in a, d and e, so one gate can serve several outputs. Multi-output minimisation is part of what synthesis tools do.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Step 3 is the set-cover problem, which is NP-hard in general, and a function of $n$ inputs can have on the order of $3^n / n$ prime implicants. The exhaustive search here is exponential and only safe for small examples. Practical minimisers such as Espresso (from Berkeley in the 1980s) give up the guarantee of a true minimum and use heuristics that are excellent in practice.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="hazards" kind="examples" title="Glitches and hazards">

Boolean algebra describes what a circuit settles to. It says nothing about the moments in between, when signals travel through gates of different delays. Take the function from the consensus example of the previous lesson, $F = AB + \overline{A}C$, built directly as two AND gates and an OR, with an inverter making $\overline{A}$. Hold $B = C = 1$. Then $F = A + \overline{A} = 1$ whatever $A$ does. But when $A$ falls from 1 to 0, the term $AB$ turns off one gate delay later, while $\overline{A}C$ turns on only after *two* gate delays (inverter, then AND). For one gate delay neither term is 1, and $F$ dips to 0. That is a **static-1 hazard**: an output that should stay 1 briefly glitches to 0.

The cure is the consensus term $BC$, logically redundant but physically useful: while $B = C = 1$ it holds the output at 1 regardless of the race between the other two terms. In K-map language, a static-1 hazard can occur wherever two adjacent 1s are covered by *different* groups; adding a group that spans the boundary removes it. A Verilog simulation with gate delays shows both versions. Each gate below is given a delay of 1 ns with `#1`; the testbench counts every change of each output.

```verilog check
`timescale 1ns/1ps
// F = AB + A'C, built from gates that each take 1 ns, with and without the consensus term BC.
module mux_logic (input a, b, c, output f, f_fixed);
  wire na, t1, t2, t3;
  not #1 g0 (na, a);
  and #1 g1 (t1, a, b);
  and #1 g2 (t2, na, c);
  or  #1 g3 (f, t1, t2);
  and #1 g4 (t3, b, c);              // the consensus term BC, logically redundant
  or  #1 g5 (f_fixed, t1, t2, t3);
endmodule

module tb;
  logic a, b, c;
  wire f, f_fixed;
  int changes = 0, changes_fixed = 0;
  mux_logic dut (.a(a), .b(b), .c(c), .f(f), .f_fixed(f_fixed));

  always @(f)       if ($time > 5) begin changes++;       $display("t = %0d ns: f = %b", $time, f); end
  always @(f_fixed) if ($time > 5) begin changes_fixed++; $display("t = %0d ns: f_fixed = %b", $time, f_fixed); end

  initial begin
    a = 1; b = 1; c = 1;
    #10 $display("t = 10 ns: a falls from 1 to 0 (f should stay 1)");
    a = 0;
    #10;
    if (changes != 2 || f !== 1)    $fatal(1, "expected a 1-0-1 glitch on f");
    if (changes_fixed != 0)         $fatal(1, "f_fixed should not move");
    $display("t = 20 ns: a rises from 0 to 1");
    a = 1;
    #10;
    if (changes != 2)               $fatal(1, "no glitch expected when a rises");
    $display("glitches: f %0d, f_fixed %0d", changes / 2, changes_fixed / 2);
    $finish(0);
  end
endmodule
```

It prints:

```text
t = 10 ns: a falls from 1 to 0 (f should stay 1)
t = 12 ns: f = 0
t = 13 ns: f = 1
t = 20 ns: a rises from 0 to 1
glitches: f 1, f_fixed 0
```

The glitch appears only when $A$ falls; when $A$ rises, $AB$ turns on before $\overline{A}C$ turns off. Hazards are not limited to this case: a **static-0 hazard** is a brief 1 on an output that should stay 0, and a **dynamic hazard** is a multiple bounce (0, 1, 0, 1) on an output that should change once, typical of multi-level logic with several paths of different lengths.

How much should you worry? In a **synchronous** design, the flip-flops of <LessonLink slug="sequential-logic-and-state-machines">the next lesson</LessonLink> sample each combinational output only once per clock cycle, after it has settled, so glitches are harmless to correctness and synthesis tools happily remove consensus terms. They still waste power (every glitch charges and discharges capacitance), and they are dangerous wherever a signal is used *without* a clock: an asynchronous reset, a clock-enable built from gates, a signal leaving the chip to another system. Rule: never drive a clock or an asynchronous input from combinational logic.

</Section>

<Section id="blocks" kind="definitions" title="Building blocks: multiplexers, decoders, encoders, comparators">

Designers rarely minimise individual functions. They assemble a handful of standard blocks, each of which you could now design from gates.

| Block | Inputs → outputs | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Multiplexer** (mux), $2^k$:1 | $2^k$ data inputs, $k$ select lines → 1 | passes the data input whose number is on the select lines |
| **Decoder**, $k$-to-$2^k$ | $k$ inputs (often with an enable) → $2^k$ | exactly one output is 1, the one numbered by the input: each output is one minterm |
| **Encoder**, $2^k$-to-$k$ | $2^k$ inputs, at most one active → $k$ | outputs the number of the active input |
| **Priority encoder** | $2^k$ inputs → $k$ + a "valid" bit | outputs the number of the highest-priority active input, and whether any is active |
| **Equality comparator** | two $n$-bit words → 1 | 1 when $A = B$: an AND of $n$ XNORs |
| **Magnitude comparator** | two $n$-bit words → $A < B$ | usually computed by subtraction: the borrow out of $A - B$ |

Two of these are more powerful than they look. A **decoder** generates every minterm of its inputs, so a decoder plus OR gates implements any set of functions; memories use decoders to select one word out of many. A **multiplexer** with $k$ select lines can implement *any* function of $k$ inputs: wire the select lines to the inputs and tie each data input to 0 or 1, the function's value on that row. That is Shannon expansion applied $k$ times, and it is exactly what an FPGA's look-up table is, as <LessonLink slug="verilog-and-fpgas">Verilog and FPGAs</LessonLink> will show. Large muxes are built as trees of 2:1 muxes, so an $N$:1 mux has depth $\log_2 N$.

A priority encoder appears wherever several requests compete: interrupt controllers, arbiters for a shared bus, the logic that finds the leading 1 of a floating-point mantissa. With inputs $I_3 I_2 I_1 I_0$ and $I_3$ highest, its outputs are $y_1 = I_3 + I_2$, $y_0 = I_3 + \overline{I_2}I_1$ and $\text{valid} = I_3 + I_2 + I_1 + I_0$; the term $\overline{I_2}$ is where the "priority" lives.

</Section>

<Section id="adders" kind="computation" title="Adders and their delay">

The ripple-carry adder of Lumenary CS's [From logic gates to a processor](/en/cs/logic-to-processor/) chains full adders, each waiting for the carry of the one below. Its worst case is the carry that travels the whole way, as in $0111\cdots1 + 1$, so its delay grows linearly with the width $n$. For a 64-bit processor that is too slow. The fix starts with two signals per bit position:

$$g_i = a_i b_i \quad \text{(generate: this position makes a carry by itself)}, \qquad p_i = a_i \oplus b_i \quad \text{(propagate: it passes an incoming carry on)}.$$

Then $c_{i+1} = g_i + p_i c_i$, and expanding the recurrence gives every carry directly from the inputs. For four bits,

$$c_4 = g_3 + p_3 g_2 + p_3 p_2 g_1 + p_3 p_2 p_1 g_0 + p_3 p_2 p_1 p_0 c_0.$$

This is a **carry-lookahead** adder: two levels of logic after $g$ and $p$, whatever the width, but the terms grow quickly, so in practice lookahead is applied to groups of four bits, and then again to the groups. Each group computes a **group generate** and **group propagate**:

$$G_{3:0} = g_3 + p_3 g_2 + p_3 p_2 g_1 + p_3 p_2 p_1 g_0, \qquad P_{3:0} = p_3 p_2 p_1 p_0.$$

The key observation is that combining two adjacent groups, high $(G_h, P_h)$ and low $(G_l, P_l)$, is always the same operation, $(G, P) = (G_h + P_h G_l,\ P_h P_l)$, and that it is *associative*. Any associative operation over $n$ items can be computed as a tree of depth $\log_2 n$, the **parallel prefix** problem. Different trees give the classic adders: **Kogge–Stone** (1973) combines every position with the one $1, 2, 4, \ldots$ places below, giving minimum depth and low fan-out at the price of many gates and long wires; **Sklansky** (1960) uses a divide-and-conquer tree with fewer gates but high fan-out; **Brent–Kung** (1982) uses fewer gates still, at nearly twice the depth.

The program models each adder as a netlist of 2-input gates, each taking one unit of delay, checks every design against Python's `+`, and then measures the latest-arriving sum bit and the number of gates.

```python run
import random

class Circuit:
    """Each signal is (value, arrival time). Every 2-input gate adds one unit of delay."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.gates = 0
    def gate(self, op, x, y):
        self.gates += 1
        return op(x[0], y[0]), max(x[1], y[1]) + 1

AND = lambda u, v: u & v
OR = lambda u, v: u | v
XOR = lambda u, v: u ^ v

def ripple(c, a, b):
    carry, s = (0, 0), []
    for ai, bi in zip(a, b):
        p, g = c.gate(XOR, ai, bi), c.gate(AND, ai, bi)
        s.append(c.gate(XOR, p, carry))
        carry = c.gate(OR, g, c.gate(AND, p, carry))     # c[i+1] = g[i] + p[i] c[i]
    return s

def prefix_adder(c, a, b, style):
    """Parallel-prefix adder: combine (G, P) pairs of ever larger groups in log2(n) levels."""
    n = len(a)
    p = [c.gate(XOR, a[i], b[i]) for i in range(n)]
    G = [c.gate(AND, a[i], b[i]) for i in range(n)]
    P = p[:]
    d = 1
    while d < n:
        if style == "Kogge-Stone":    # every bit combines with the group d places below it
            pairs = [(i, i - d) for i in range(d, n)]
        else:                         # Sklansky: upper half of each 2d-block uses the lower half's top
            pairs = [(i, (i & ~(d - 1)) - 1) for i in range(n) if i & d]
        newG, newP = G[:], P[:]
        for i, j in pairs:
            newG[i] = c.gate(OR, G[i], c.gate(AND, P[i], G[j]))
            newP[i] = c.gate(AND, P[i], P[j])
        G, P = newG, newP
        d *= 2
    return [p[0]] + [c.gate(XOR, p[i], G[i - 1]) for i in range(1, n)]   # carry into bit i is G[0..i-1]

def add(design, x, y, n):
    c = Circuit()
    a = [((x >> i) & 1, 0) for i in range(n)]
    b = [((y >> i) & 1, 0) for i in range(n)]
    s = design(c, a, b)
    return sum(v << i for i, (v, t) in enumerate(s)), max(t for v, t in s), c.gates

designs = {"ripple-carry": ripple,
           "Kogge-Stone": lambda c, a, b: prefix_adder(c, a, b, "Kogge-Stone"),
           "Sklansky": lambda c, a, b: prefix_adder(c, a, b, "Sklansky")}

rng = random.Random(1)
for name, design in designs.items():          # check every design against Python's +
    for _ in range(300):
        n = rng.choice([8, 16, 32])
        x, y = rng.getrandbits(n), rng.getrandbits(n)
        assert add(design, x, y, n)[0] == (x + y) % 2**n, name
print("all three adders agree with + on 900 random sums\n")

print(" bits | gate delays: ripple  Kogge-Stone  Sklansky | gates: ripple  Kogge-Stone  Sklansky")
for n in (8, 16, 32, 64):
    d = {k: add(f, 2**n - 1, 1, n)[1:] for k, f in designs.items()}   # all ones + 1: the longest carry
    print(f"{n:5} | {d['ripple-carry'][0]:19} {d['Kogge-Stone'][0]:12} {d['Sklansky'][0]:9} |"
          f" {d['ripple-carry'][1]:13} {d['Kogge-Stone'][1]:12} {d['Sklansky'][1]:9}")
```

The ripple adder's delay is exactly $2n$ gate delays; the prefix adders' grows by two per doubling of the width. At 64 bits Kogge–Stone is ten times faster than ripple-carry in this model, and needs 3.6 times the gates; Sklansky is one gate delay slower, with a third fewer gates. Real designs pick a point on this curve to meet their clock period with the least area and power, and often mix styles.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Put numbers on it. If one gate delay is about 15 ps in a modern process, a 64-bit ripple adder needs about $128 \times 15 = 1920$ ps, nearly twice the whole 1000 ps period of a 1 GHz clock. Kogge–Stone's 13 gate delays are about 200 ps, which leaves room in the same cycle for the multiplexers and wires around the adder.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="alu" kind="computation" title="An ALU in Verilog">

An **arithmetic logic unit** computes one of several operations on two words, chosen by an operation code. Its central trick is that one adder serves for addition, subtraction and comparison: $a - b = a + \overline{b} + 1$ in two's complement, so subtraction inverts $b$ and sets the adder's carry-in to 1. Comparison is then a subtraction whose result is thrown away:

- **unsigned** $a < b$ exactly when the subtraction *borrows*, that is, when the carry out of $a + \overline{b} + 1$ is 0;
- **signed** $a < b$ exactly when the sign bit of $a - b$ is 1, *unless* the subtraction overflowed, in which case the sign bit is wrong and must be flipped.

Signed **overflow** happens when two operands of the same sign (after inverting $b$ for subtraction) give a result of the other sign, as in $\texttt{7fffffff} + 1$, the largest 32-bit signed number plus one.

The ALU below has the ten operations a RISC-V processor's integer instructions need (the processor in <LessonLink slug="designing-a-processor">Designing a processor</LessonLink> uses one like it). Its testbench follows the professional pattern: a **reference model**, written separately with Verilog's own operators, predicts every result, and the testbench compares the design against it on hand-picked corner cases and on 2,000 pseudo-random vectors per operation. `$random(seed)` with a fixed seed makes the run repeatable.

```verilog check
// A 32-bit ALU: one adder does addition, subtraction and both comparisons.
module alu (
  input  logic [31:0] a, b,
  input  logic [3:0]  op,
  output logic [31:0] y,
  output logic        zero, overflow
);
  localparam ADD = 4'd0, SUB = 4'd1, AND = 4'd2, OR = 4'd3, XOR = 4'd4,
             SLT = 4'd5, SLTU = 4'd6, SLL = 4'd7, SRL = 4'd8, SRA = 4'd9;

  logic        subtract, carry, less_signed;
  logic [31:0] b_in, sum;
  logic [4:0]  shamt;
  assign subtract    = (op == SUB) || (op == SLT) || (op == SLTU);
  assign b_in        = subtract ? ~b : b;                       // a - b = a + ~b + 1
  assign {carry, sum} = {1'b0, a} + {1'b0, b_in} + {32'd0, subtract};
  // Signed overflow: both operands have the same sign and the result's sign differs.
  assign overflow    = (a[31] == b_in[31]) && (sum[31] != a[31]);
  assign less_signed = sum[31] ^ overflow;   // sign of a - b, corrected for overflow
  assign shamt       = b[4:0];               // shifts use the low 5 bits of b

  always_comb begin
    case (op)
      ADD, SUB: y = sum;
      AND:      y = a & b;
      OR:       y = a | b;
      XOR:      y = a ^ b;
      SLT:      y = {31'd0, less_signed};          // a < b as signed numbers
      SLTU:     y = {31'd0, ~carry};               // a < b unsigned: the subtraction borrowed
      SLL:      y = a << shamt;
      SRL:      y = a >> shamt;
      SRA:      y = $signed(a) >>> shamt;
      default:  y = 32'd0;
    endcase
  end
  assign zero = (y == 32'd0);
endmodule

module tb;
  logic [31:0] a, b, y, expected;
  logic [3:0]  op;
  logic        zero, overflow;
  int          seed = 2026, tests = 0;
  alu dut (.a(a), .b(b), .op(op), .y(y), .zero(zero), .overflow(overflow));

  // The reference model uses Verilog's own operators, written independently of the design.
  function automatic logic [31:0] model(input logic [31:0] x, z, input logic [3:0] o);
    case (o)
      0: return x + z;            1: return x - z;
      2: return x & z;            3: return x | z;
      4: return x ^ z;            5: return {31'd0, $signed(x) < $signed(z)};
      6: return {31'd0, x < z};   7: return x << z[4:0];
      8: return x >> z[4:0];      9: return $signed(x) >>> z[4:0];
      default: return 0;
    endcase
  endfunction

  task automatic check(input logic [31:0] x, z, input logic [3:0] o);
    a = x; b = z; op = o; #1;
    expected = model(x, z, o);
    if (y !== expected || zero !== (expected == 0))
      $fatal(1, "op %0d: a=%h b=%h gave %h, expected %h", o, x, z, y, expected);
    tests++;
  endtask

  initial begin
    check(32'h7FFF_FFFF, 32'd1, 0);
    $display("7fffffff + 1        = %h  overflow = %b", y, overflow);
    if (overflow !== 1) $fatal(1, "missed signed overflow");
    check(32'h8000_0000, 32'd1, 1);
    $display("80000000 - 1        = %h  overflow = %b", y, overflow);
    if (overflow !== 1) $fatal(1, "missed signed overflow");
    check(32'hFFFF_FFFB, 32'd3, 5);
    $display("fffffffb < 3 signed = %0d   (-5 < 3)", y);
    check(32'hFFFF_FFFB, 32'd3, 6);
    $display("fffffffb < 3 unsigned = %0d (4294967291 < 3)", y);
    check(32'h8000_0000, 32'd4, 9);
    $display("80000000 >>> 4      = %h", y);
    check(32'd42, 32'd42, 1);
    $display("42 - 42             = %0d  zero = %b", y, zero);
    for (int o = 0; o <= 9; o++)
      repeat (2000) check($random(seed), $random(seed), o[3:0]);
    $display("%0d vectors match the reference model", tests);
    $finish(0);
  end
endmodule
```

It prints:

```text
7fffffff + 1        = 80000000  overflow = 1
80000000 - 1        = 7fffffff  overflow = 1
fffffffb < 3 signed = 1   (-5 < 3)
fffffffb < 3 unsigned = 0 (4294967291 < 3)
80000000 >>> 4      = f8000000
42 - 42             = 0  zero = 1
20006 vectors match the reference model
```

`always_comb` is SystemVerilog's way of saying "this block is combinational logic": the simulator re-evaluates it whenever an input changes, and synthesis tools warn if it would infer memory by accident. The shift amount uses only the low five bits of `b`, as RISC-V specifies for 32-bit registers, and the arithmetic right shift `>>>` copies the sign bit in from the left, so $\texttt{80000000} \gg\!\gg 4 = \texttt{f8000000}$.

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="How combinational logic is designed in practice">

Nobody draws K-maps for a processor. A designer writes behaviour (`assign y = a + b;`) or a structure of blocks in a hardware description language, and a **logic synthesis** tool turns it into gates. Modern synthesis represents logic as an **and-inverter graph**, a network of 2-input AND gates and inverters, rewrites it to reduce size and depth, and then **maps** it onto the cells available: a standard-cell library of NAND, NOR, AOI, flip-flop and other cells for a chip, or look-up tables and carry chains for an FPGA. Berkeley's open-source [ABC](https://github.com/berkeley-abc/abc) does this inside [Yosys](https://github.com/YosysHQ/yosys); the commercial tools of Synopsys, Cadence and Siemens do the same at much larger scale. For arithmetic, tools keep libraries of adder and multiplier architectures and choose among ripple, prefix and hybrid designs according to the timing constraint.

Two consequences matter to anyone writing hardware. First, after optimisation the gates no longer match the source, so teams run **formal equivalence checking** (SAT-based and BDD-based tools) to prove that the netlist computes the same function as the description. Second, the best structure depends on the target. On an FPGA, an adder is usually *fastest* as a ripple-carry chain, because FPGAs have dedicated carry logic running vertically through each column, far faster than general routing; a hand-built Kogge–Stone adder in LUTs can be slower. On a custom chip the prefix adder wins.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A minimal sum of products is the smallest circuit."** It is the smallest *two-level* AND–OR circuit. Factoring ($\overline{D}(\overline{B} + C)$ instead of $C\overline{D} + \overline{B}\,\overline{D}$), sharing terms between outputs, and XOR gates often give much smaller multi-level circuits. Parity of 16 bits needs $2^{15}$ terms as a sum of products and 15 XOR gates as a tree.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Redundant terms are always waste."** The consensus term that removes a hazard is logically redundant and physically necessary in circuits that are not sampled by a clock. Synthesis tools remove such terms, which is why asynchronous logic is written and constrained with special care.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Don't-cares mean the output is 0 for those inputs."** They mean the designer may choose either value, row by row. Treating them all as 0 throws away the freedom that made segment e two small terms. (Conversely, if those inputs *can* occur after all, for example at power-up, the chosen values had better be harmless.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The fastest adder is always the best."** A Kogge–Stone adder costs several times the area and power of a ripple adder and has long wires. If the adder is not on the critical path, the smallest design that meets timing is the right one.

</Misconception>
