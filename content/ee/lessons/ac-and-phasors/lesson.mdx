<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why sinusoids?">

Almost all the electricity you use arrives as a sinusoid. The voltage at a wall socket swings smoothly up and down 50 times a second in most of the world and 60 times a second in most of the Americas. Radio, Wi-Fi and mobile phones send their information on sinusoidal carriers at millions or billions of cycles a second. And through Fourier analysis (<LessonLink slug="fourier-analysis">Fourier analysis</LessonLink>), any repeating signal, a square clock wave or a musical note, can be written as a sum of sinusoids.

Sinusoids have one property that makes them the natural language of circuit analysis. In a **linear** circuit (resistors, capacitors, inductors and sources, with nothing that switches or saturates), a sinusoidal source produces, once the transients have died away, voltages and currents that are all sinusoids **of the same frequency**. Only two things change from place to place: how big each sinusoid is, and how far it is shifted in time. You saw this at the end of <LessonLink slug="rlc-circuits">RLC circuits and resonance</LessonLink>, where a complex exponential turned the RLC differential equation into one line of algebra.

This lesson turns that trick into a method. Each sinusoid becomes a complex number, a **phasor**, that records its size and shift. Each component becomes a complex **impedance**. And then every technique you already know for resistive circuits, from Ohm's law to nodal analysis, works unchanged, with complex numbers in place of real ones.

</Section>

<Section id="sinusoids" kind="definitions" title="Describing a sinusoid">

<Definition term="Sinusoid, amplitude, frequency, phase">

A **sinusoid** is a signal of the form

$$
v(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \phi).
$$

$V_m > 0$ is its **amplitude** (peak value). $\omega$ is its **angular frequency** in radians per second; its **frequency** is $f = \omega/2\pi$ in hertz (cycles per second) and its **period** is $T = 1/f$. $\phi$ is its **phase**, in radians or degrees. Of two sinusoids at the same frequency, the one with the larger phase **leads**: it reaches each peak earlier, by $\Delta t = \Delta\phi/\omega$. The other **lags**.

</Definition>

A sine is a cosine that lags by 90°, $\sin\omega t = \cos(\omega t - 90°)$, so we write everything as cosines and lose nothing.

### RMS values, and why mains is quoted in RMS

A sinusoid averages to zero over a cycle, so its average says nothing about how much it can heat a kettle. What matters for heating is power, and the power in a resistor is $v^2/R$, which is never negative. The **root-mean-square** (RMS) value is defined so that it gives the same average power in a resistor as a steady DC value would:

$$
V_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T v(t)^2\,dt}.
$$

For a sinusoid, the average of $\cos^2$ over a cycle is $\tfrac12$, so $V_\text{rms} = V_m/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707\,V_m$. A 2 kW kettle heats exactly as fast on 230 V RMS AC as it would on 230 V DC.

That is why mains voltages are quoted as RMS values. The "230 V" (or 220 V) supply of most of Europe, Africa, Asia and Oceania, the "120 V" of North America and the "100 V" of Japan are all RMS. Their peaks are $\sqrt2$ times higher: 325 V, 170 V and 141 V. Insulation, and the safety rules you will meet throughout this course, are designed around those peaks. (This is also one of many reasons to leave mains wiring to qualified electricians and to do your own experiments with small batteries, signal generators and simulation.)

The factor $\sqrt2$ belongs to sinusoids alone. Many cheap meters measure the average of $|v|$ and multiply by 1.111, the ratio that makes a sinusoid read correctly; a **true-RMS** meter computes the real thing. The program below compares them on four waveforms, including the current that a simple rectifier power supply draws, which flows only in short pulses near each peak of the mains voltage.

```python run
import numpy as np

f = 50.0
t = np.linspace(0, 1 / f, 200000, endpoint=False)   # one cycle, finely sampled
phase = f * t                                        # runs from 0 to 1 over the cycle
s = np.sin(2 * np.pi * phase)

waves = {
    "sine":             s,
    "square":           np.where(phase < 0.5, 1.0, -1.0),
    "triangle":         2 * np.abs(2 * phase - 1) - 1,
    # current drawn by a rectifier feeding a capacitor: short pulses near each voltage peak
    "rectifier pulses": np.sign(s) * np.clip((np.abs(s) - 0.9) / 0.1, 0, None),
}

print(f"{'waveform':17} {'peak':>5} {'true RMS':>9} {'average-responding':>19} {'crest factor':>13}")
for name, x in waves.items():
    peak = np.abs(x).max()
    rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(x**2))                       # root of the mean of the square
    avg_meter = np.mean(np.abs(x)) * np.pi / (2 * np.sqrt(2))   # calibrated so a sine reads right
    print(f"{name:17} {peak:5.2f} {rms:9.4f} {avg_meter:19.4f} {peak / rms:13.2f}")

print()
for volts in (230, 120, 100):
    print(f"{volts} V RMS mains: peak {volts * np.sqrt(2):.0f} V, peak to peak {2 * volts * np.sqrt(2):.0f} V")
```

For the sine both meters agree at 0.7071 of the peak. For the square wave the average-responding meter reads 11% high (1.1107 instead of 1); for the triangle, 4% low. For the rectifier pulses it reads 0.2119 where the true RMS is 0.3904, almost 46% low, because the pulses are tall and narrow: their **crest factor** (peak divided by RMS) is 2.56 against a sine's 1.41. Since so much modern equipment draws pulsed current like this, power engineers measure with true-RMS instruments.

<Section id="phasors" kind="intuition" title="Phasors: sinusoids as complex numbers">

As in the previous lesson, we write $j$ for $\sqrt{-1}$, because $i$ means current. Euler's formula, $e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$ (see Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.number.complex-numbers">Complex numbers</TopicLink>), lets us write any sinusoid as the real part of a rotating complex number:

$$
V_m\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \operatorname{Re}\!\left(V_m e^{j\phi}\, e^{j\omega t}\right).
$$

The factor $e^{j\omega t}$ is the same for every voltage and current in the circuit, so we can leave it out and keep only the complex number in front of it.

<Definition term="Phasor">

The **phasor** of the sinusoid $V_m\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ is the complex number $V = V_m e^{j\phi}$, often written in **polar form** as $V_m\angle\phi$. Its magnitude is the amplitude and its angle is the phase. To return to the time domain, multiply by $e^{j\omega t}$ and take the real part. We write phasors as capital letters and time functions as lower-case ones.

</Definition>

Picture the phasor as an arrow in the complex plane, spinning anticlockwise at $\omega$ radians per second; the signal is its shadow on the real axis. Two sinusoids at the same frequency spin together, so the angle between their arrows, their phase difference, never changes. That frozen picture is the **phasor diagram**.

<Theorem name="Circuit laws for phasors">

In a linear circuit whose sources all have angular frequency $\omega$, in sinusoidal steady state:

1. Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws hold for phasors: the phasor currents into a node sum to zero, and the phasor voltages round a loop sum to zero.
2. Each component obeys $V = Z I$, where $V$ and $I$ are the phasors of its voltage and current, and $Z$ is its **impedance**: $Z_R = R$, $Z_L = j\omega L$ and $Z_C = \dfrac{1}{j\omega C} = \dfrac{-j}{\omega C}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

(1) If $\sum_k \operatorname{Re}(V_k e^{j\omega t}) = 0$ for all $t$, then $\operatorname{Re}(W e^{j\omega t}) = 0$ for all $t$, where $W = \sum_k V_k$. At $t = 0$ this says $\operatorname{Re} W = 0$; at $\omega t = \pi/2$ it says $\operatorname{Re}(jW) = -\operatorname{Im} W = 0$. So $W = 0$. (2) Differentiating, $\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Re}(I e^{j\omega t}) = \operatorname{Re}(j\omega I e^{j\omega t})$: **differentiation in time is multiplication of the phasor by $j\omega$**. So the inductor law $v = L\,di/dt$ becomes $V = j\omega L\,I$, and the capacitor law $i = C\,dv/dt$ becomes $I = j\omega C\,V$, that is, $V = I/(j\omega C)$. The resistor law $v = Ri$ is unchanged.

</Proof>

<Example title="Adding two voltages">

In a series circuit, the voltage across a resistor is $3\cos\omega t$ V and the voltage across an inductor is $4\cos(\omega t + 90°)$ V. What is the total? In phasors, $3\angle 0° + 4\angle 90° = 3 + 4j$, whose magnitude is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ and whose angle is $\arctan(4/3) = 53.1°$. So the total is $5\cos(\omega t + 53.1°)$ V: **5 V, not 7 V**, because the two peaks happen at different times. Adding the cosines directly with trigonometric identities gives the same answer, with far more work.

</Example>

<Narrator who="chloe">

The deep reason phasors work is that $e^{st}$ is an **eigenfunction** of differentiation: $\frac{d}{dt}e^{st} = s\,e^{st}$. A linear circuit can only scale it and shift its phase, never change its shape. Phasors are the case $s = j\omega$; the <LessonLink slug="laplace-and-stability">Laplace transform</LessonLink> uses every complex $s$, and so handles transients too.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="impedance" kind="definitions" title="Impedance">

<Definition term="Impedance, reactance, admittance">

The **impedance** of a two-terminal element is $Z = V/I$, the ratio of the phasors of its voltage and current, in ohms. It is complex: $Z = R + jX$, where $R$ is the **resistance** and $X$ the **reactance**. Its reciprocal $Y = 1/Z = G + jB$ is the **admittance**, in siemens, made of **conductance** $G$ and **susceptance** $B$.

| Element | Impedance | Magnitude | Current compared with voltage | At DC ($\omega = 0$) | At very high $\omega$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor | $R$ | $R$ | in phase | $R$ | $R$ |
| Inductor | $j\omega L$ | $\omega L$ | lags by 90° | short circuit | open circuit |
| Capacitor | $1/(j\omega C)$ | $1/(\omega C)$ | leads by 90° | open circuit | short circuit |

</Definition>

Impedances combine exactly like resistances: in series they add, $Z = Z_1 + Z_2$; in parallel the admittances add, $Z = Z_1 Z_2/(Z_1 + Z_2)$. Voltage dividers, Thévenin equivalents (<LessonLink slug="network-theorems">Network theorems</LessonLink>) and nodal analysis all carry over.

<Example title="A motor's impedance at 50 Hz">

At one operating point, a small single-phase motor can be modelled as a 20 Ω resistance (which stands for both the real work done and the losses) in series with a 50 mH inductance (its windings). At 50 Hz, $\omega = 2\pi \times 50 = 314.2$ rad/s, so

$$
Z = 20 + j\,(314.2)(0.05) = 20 + j15.71\ \Omega = 25.43\angle 38.1°\ \Omega.
$$

On a 230 V RMS supply the current has RMS value $230/25.43 = 9.04$ A, and it lags the voltage by 38.1°. A pure resistor of 25.43 Ω would draw the same current in step with the voltage; we will see in a moment that the two are very different loads for the power company.

</Example>

The series RLC circuit of the previous lesson is now one line: $Z = R + j\left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)$. The reactance vanishes at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, where $Z = R$, and the current amplitude $V_m/|Z|$ is the resonance formula we derived there.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Impedance is defined only for sinusoidal steady state at one frequency. "The impedance of this inductor is 15.71 Ω" is meaningless without "at 50 Hz", and it says nothing about what happens in the first milliseconds after switching on. For that, go back to the differential equations, or forward to the Laplace transform.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="nodal" kind="computation" title="AC nodal analysis with NumPy">

<LessonLink slug="nodal-and-mesh-analysis">Nodal analysis</LessonLink> writes Kirchhoff's current law at every node as a linear system $YV = I$, where $Y$ is the nodal admittance matrix. Nothing changes for AC circuits except that $Y$, $V$ and $I$ are complex, and Gaussian elimination (Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">Linear algebra</TopicLink>) works over the complex numbers exactly as over the reals, so NumPy solves complex systems as happily as real ones.

Here is a small filter driven at 1 kHz: a 10 V source with 100 Ω of internal resistance, a capacitor to ground, an inductor in series, and a load of 50 Ω in parallel with another capacitor.

The program uses a **source transformation**: a voltage source $V_s$ in series with $R_1$ behaves, as far as the rest of the circuit can tell, like a current source $V_s/R_1$ in parallel with $R_1$. Then it builds $Y$ the way SPICE does, by "stamping" each branch's admittance into the matrix, and solves.

```python run
import numpy as np

f = 1000.0                        # every source in the circuit runs at 1 kHz
w = 2 * np.pi * f
Vs = 10.0 + 0j                    # source phasor: amplitude 10 V, phase 0

def Z_R(R): return complex(R)
def Z_L(L): return 1j * w * L
def Z_C(C): return 1 / (1j * w * C)

# (name, node a, node b, impedance); node 0 is ground. The source and its 100-ohm
# resistor become a current source Vs/R1 into node 1, with R1 from node 1 to ground.
branches = [
    ("R1", 1, 0, Z_R(100)),
    ("C1", 1, 0, Z_C(1e-6)),
    ("L1", 1, 2, Z_L(10e-3)),
    ("R2", 2, 0, Z_R(50)),
    ("C2", 2, 0, Z_C(2.2e-6)),
]
n = 2
Y = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=complex)        # the nodal admittance matrix
for name, a, b, Z in branches:
    y = 1 / Z
    if a: Y[a - 1, a - 1] += y
    if b: Y[b - 1, b - 1] += y
    if a and b:
        Y[a - 1, b - 1] -= y
        Y[b - 1, a - 1] -= y
I = np.array([Vs / 100, 0])                # current injected into each node
V = np.linalg.solve(Y, I)

def polar(z, unit):
    return f"{abs(z):7.4f} {unit} at {np.degrees(np.angle(z)):7.2f} deg"

for name, a, b, Z in branches:
    print(f"Z_{name} = {Z.real:6.2f} {Z.imag:+8.2f}j ohm")
print()
for k, v in enumerate(V, start=1):
    print(f"V{k} = {v.real:7.4f} {v.imag:+7.4f}j V = {polar(v, 'V')}")

# Branch currents, and Kirchhoff's current law checked at both nodes.
i_src = (Vs - V[0]) / 100                  # out of the real source, through R1
i_C1, i_L1 = V[0] / Z_C(1e-6), (V[0] - V[1]) / Z_L(10e-3)
i_R2, i_C2 = V[1] / Z_R(50), V[1] / Z_C(2.2e-6)
print(f"\nsource current = {polar(i_src * 1000, 'mA')}")
print(f"KCL residuals: node 1 {abs(i_src - i_C1 - i_L1):.1e} A, node 2 {abs(i_L1 - i_R2 - i_C2):.1e} A")

t = 0.1e-3                                 # back to the time domain, at t = 0.1 ms
print(f"v2(0.1 ms) = Re(V2 e^(j w t)) = {(V[1] * np.exp(1j * w * t)).real:.4f} V")
```

The output voltage is $V_2 = 3.2956\angle{-63.75°}$ V: at 1 kHz this network passes about a third of the source amplitude and delays it by almost a fifth of a cycle. Node n1 sits at $4.1639\angle 20.27°$ V, which *leads* the source, something no resistive circuit could do. Kirchhoff's current law holds at both nodes up to floating-point rounding: the residuals are of the order of $10^{-17}$ A or zero.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Check one number by hand before trusting the rest. At node n2, the load is $R_2 \parallel C_2$, and the current through it should equal the inductor current. The program gives $|V_2| = 3.2956$ V; $|Z_{R_2 \parallel C_2}| = 50 \times 72.34/\sqrt{50^2 + 72.34^2} = 41.1\ \Omega$, so the inductor carries about 80 mA, and its voltage, $62.83\ \Omega \times 80$ mA $\approx 5.0$ V, is larger than either node voltage. Phasors allow that; magnitudes alone would not.

</Narrator>

### Checking with SPICE

SPICE's `.ac` analysis does exactly this calculation: it linearises the circuit, gives each source a phasor (here an amplitude of 10 V at phase 0), and solves the complex nodal equations at each requested frequency. `vm` and `vp` are a node's magnitude and phase (in radians, which the script converts to degrees).

```spice check
* AC nodal analysis check: 10 V at 1 kHz into a C-L-C network
V1 s 0 DC 0 AC 10
R1 s n1 100
C1 n1 0 1u
L1 n1 n2 10m
R2 n2 0 50
C2 n2 0 2.2u
.control
ac lin 1 1k 1k
let ph1 = 180/pi*vp(n1)
let ph2 = 180/pi*vp(n2)
print vm(n1) ph1 vm(n2) ph2
.endc
.end
```

<Section id="power" kind="examples" title="Power in AC circuits">

In AC circuits, power has more than one meaning, and the differences matter to anyone who pays for electricity or designs the cables that carry it. Let a load have RMS voltage $V$ and RMS current $I$, with the current lagging the voltage by the angle $\theta$, which is the angle of the load's impedance.

<Theorem name="Average power in sinusoidal steady state">

If $v(t) = \sqrt2\,V\cos\omega t$ and $i(t) = \sqrt2\,I\cos(\omega t - \theta)$, the instantaneous power is

$$
p(t) = v(t)\,i(t) = VI\cos\theta + VI\cos(2\omega t - \theta),
$$

and its average over a cycle is $P = VI\cos\theta$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Use $\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12[\cos(a - b) + \cos(a + b)]$ with $a = \omega t$ and $b = \omega t - \theta$: $p(t) = 2VI \cdot \tfrac12[\cos\theta + \cos(2\omega t - \theta)]$. The second term oscillates at $2\omega$ and averages to zero over a cycle, leaving $VI\cos\theta$.

</Proof>

So the power flowing into a load has a steady part and a part that surges back and forth at twice the supply frequency. That splits naturally into named quantities.

<Definition term="Real, reactive and apparent power; power factor">

With RMS phasors $V$ and $I$, the **complex power** is $S = V I^{*} = P + jQ$, where $I^*$ is the complex conjugate. Its parts are:

- **Real (active) power** $P = VI\cos\theta$, in watts (W): the average rate at which energy is delivered and turned into heat, light or work.
- **Reactive power** $Q = VI\sin\theta$, in volt-amperes reactive (var): the amplitude of the energy that flows into the load's inductances and capacitances and back again each cycle, doing no net work. By convention $Q > 0$ for inductive loads (current lagging) and $Q < 0$ for capacitive ones.
- **Apparent power** $|S| = VI$, in volt-amperes (VA): the product of the RMS voltage and current that the supply must actually provide.
- **Power factor** $\text{pf} = P/|S|$, equal to $\cos\theta$ for sinusoids, described as **lagging** or **leading** according to the current.

</Definition>

The program computes all of this for the motor example, then checks it against the time domain by multiplying $v(t)$ and $i(t)$ sample by sample.

```python run
import numpy as np

f, V_rms = 50.0, 230.0
w = 2 * np.pi * f
R, L = 20.0, 50e-3                     # a motor at one operating point: R in series with L
Z = R + 1j * w * L
V = complex(V_rms)                     # an RMS phasor, as power engineers write them
I = V / Z
S = V * np.conj(I)                     # complex power in volt-amperes
P, Q = S.real, S.imag
print(f"Z = {Z.real:.2f} + {Z.imag:.2f}j ohm = {abs(Z):.2f} ohm at {np.degrees(np.angle(Z)):.1f} deg")
print(f"I = {abs(I):.3f} A RMS, lagging the voltage by {-np.degrees(np.angle(I)):.1f} deg")
print(f"P = {P:.0f} W   Q = {Q:.0f} var   |S| = {abs(S):.0f} VA   power factor = {P / abs(S):.3f}")

# The same numbers from the time domain: average v(t) i(t) over one cycle.
t = np.linspace(0, 1 / f, 100000, endpoint=False)
def wave(phasor):                      # an RMS phasor back to a waveform
    return np.sqrt(2) * abs(phasor) * np.cos(w * t + np.angle(phasor))
v, i = wave(V), wave(I)
v_R, v_L = wave(R * I), wave(1j * w * L * I)
p = v * i
print(f"\nmean of v*i over a cycle   = {p.mean():.1f} W")
print(f"p(t) swings between {p.min():.0f} W and {p.max():.0f} W")
print(f"mean power into R          = {np.mean(v_R * i):.1f} W")
print(f"mean power into L          = {abs(np.mean(v_L * i)):.3f} W (it swings by +/- {np.abs(v_L * i).max():.0f} W)")
```

The phasor calculation and the brute-force average agree: 1636 W. The instantaneous power swings between −444 W and 3716 W, which is $P \mp |S|$: for a short part of every cycle the motor pushes energy *back* into the supply. All the real power goes into the resistance. The inductance takes in and returns 1285 W at the peak of each surge, exactly $Q$, and on average absorbs nothing.

### Why the power factor matters

The supply must deliver 9.04 A for this motor's 1636 W. A load with power factor 1 would need only $1636/230 = 7.11$ A for the same power. Every cable, transformer and generator between the power station and the motor must carry the full 9.04 A and heats in proportion to the square of the current, so the losses in the wiring are $(9.04/7.11)^2 \approx 1.62$ times larger: 62% more, for the same useful power. That is why transformers and supplies are rated in VA rather than watts, and why many utilities require large industrial customers to keep their power factor above a minimum or pay extra.

The remedy, **power-factor correction**, is to supply the reactive power locally. A capacitor connected across an inductive load draws a leading current that cancels much of the load's lagging current, so the line carries less. Modern power supplies go further and shape their input current electronically. Both are subjects of the Power level, beginning with <LessonLink slug="three-phase-power">Three-phase power</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="power-electronics">Power electronics</LessonLink>.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Watch the conventions. With RMS phasors, $S = VI^*$; with amplitude phasors, as in the nodal example and in SPICE's `.ac`, the same power is $S = \tfrac12 VI^*$. Power engineers use RMS phasors, many electronics texts use amplitudes, and mixing them gives answers that are wrong by exactly a factor of 2.

</Narrator>

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="The professional view">

**Measuring impedance.** Engineers measure impedance with an **LCR meter**, which drives a component with a small sinusoid at a chosen frequency (100 Hz, 1 kHz and 100 kHz are common choices) and computes $Z = V/I$ from the measured phasors; an **impedance analyser** or **vector network analyser** does the same across a wide band of frequencies. Real components are not ideal. A capacitor has a little series resistance and a little series inductance, so above its **self-resonant frequency** it behaves like an inductor; manufacturers such as Murata, TDK, KEMET and Samsung Electro-Mechanics publish measured impedance-versus-frequency curves for their parts, and designers choose decoupling capacitors from those curves rather than from the nominal capacitance alone.

**Phasors on the grid.** Power engineers think in phasors all day: load-flow studies of national grids are AC nodal analysis with thousands of nodes, solved with sparse complex matrices. Since the 1990s, **phasor measurement units** (PMUs) have measured the voltage and current phasors at substations many times a second, time-stamped to within about a microsecond by satellite timing, so that phase angles across a whole continent can be compared directly. The measurement is standardised jointly by the IEEE and the IEC, and grid operators in North America, Europe, China, India, Brazil and elsewhere use it to watch for the oscillations and angle separations that precede blackouts (more in <LessonLink slug="the-power-grid">The power grid</LessonLink>).

**Beyond one sinusoid.** Phasors describe a single frequency in steady state. Waveforms that are not sinusoidal, like the rectifier pulses above, are analysed by splitting them into harmonics (<LessonLink slug="fourier-analysis">Fourier analysis</LessonLink>) and handling each frequency with its own phasors; then the power factor has two parts, one from the phase shift and one from the distortion. Transients after switching need the differential equations of <LessonLink slug="rlc-circuits">the previous lesson</LessonLink> or the Laplace transform. And SPICE's `.ac` analysis of a circuit with transistors is a *small-signal* analysis: SPICE linearises each device around its operating point first, so the results hold only for small signals.

**A short history.** In 1886 Oliver Heaviside coined the word *impedance*. The late 1880s brought the contest between Thomas Edison's direct current and the alternating current of George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla, which AC won largely because transformers, which work only with AC, can step voltages up for efficient long-distance transmission (<LessonLink slug="transformers-and-machines">Transformers and electric machines</LessonLink>). But analysing AC circuits with trigonometry was laborious, and in 1893 the German-American engineer Charles Proteus Steinmetz and the British-American engineer Arthur Kennelly independently showed how complex numbers turn it into algebra. Within a generation, $Z = R + jX$ was standard engineering practice.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A 230 V supply never exceeds 230 V."** 230 V is the RMS value. The voltage peaks at $230\sqrt2 = 325$ V twice every cycle, and swings 651 V from peak to peak.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Voltages in series add up."** Their *phasors* add up. A resistor with 3 V across it in series with an inductor with 4 V across it has 5 V across the pair, not 7 V, because the two voltages peak at different moments. In a series RLC circuit near resonance, the inductor and capacitor voltages can each be far larger than the supply, because they nearly cancel.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Impedance is just resistance that depends on frequency."** Resistance dissipates energy; reactance stores it and gives it back. A load with a large impedance but a small resistive part takes plenty of current from the supply while absorbing very little power.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Reactive power is wasted power."** Reactive power is not consumed: it flows back and forth, averaging to zero. What it costs is current, and that current causes real losses in the wires and occupies the capacity of cables and transformers.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Power factor is the same thing as efficiency."** An electric heater has power factor 1 and turns every watt into heat; an efficient motor may still have a power factor of 0.8. Efficiency compares useful output with real power in; power factor compares real power with apparent power.

</Misconception>
