<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="From a need to a drawing someone can make">

In <LessonLink slug="how-things-are-made">How things are made</LessonLink> you met the design cycle: understand the problem, sketch ideas, prototype, test, improve. That loop never goes away, but a professional team building a gearbox, an infusion pump or a farm machine needs more: a precise statement of what "good enough" means, a fair way to choose between very different ideas, a model precise enough to make thousands of identical parts from, and a way of saying how *im*precise each part may be.

This lesson follows a human-powered irrigation pump for smallholder farms, and a gearbox shaft, through **requirements**, **concepts**, **embodiment**, **detail**, and the CAD and tolerancing that carry a design to the factory. You will compute a weighted decision matrix and test how fragile its answer is, work out the clearance in a standard fit, and add up a chain of tolerances three ways.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Every stage of this lesson answers the same question at a finer scale: what is this for, and how will it do it? A specification answers it for the whole product, a concept for its working principle, a layout for each part, and a tolerance for each surface.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="requirements" kind="definitions" title="Requirements and specifications">

A **need** is what a person wants, in their words: "I want to water my vegetables in the dry season without paying for fuel." Engineers translate needs into **requirements** that a design can be checked against, and collect them in a **specification** (in industry often called a product requirements document or, in the German tradition, a *Lastenheft* and *Pflichtenheft*).

<Definition term="requirement">

A statement of something the product **shall** do or be, written so that it can be **verified** by test, measurement, inspection or analysis. A good requirement is necessary, unambiguous, verifiable, and free of any particular solution. It has a metric, a target value and, often, a limit beyond which the design is unacceptable.

</Definition>

Compare "the pump should be easy to use" with "a 60 kg adult shall deliver at least 0.7 L/s against a 4 m head for 30 minutes at a mechanical power input of no more than 75 W." The second can be tested on a rig with a stopwatch and a bucket; the first cannot. Here is a fragment of a specification for our pump, written with a farming cooperative's members after field visits:

| ID | Requirement | Metric | Target | Limit | Verified by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R1 | Delivers water from a shallow well | flow at 4 m head, 75 W input | 1.0 L/s | ≥ 0.7 L/s | rig test |
| R2 | Affordable for one household | factory cost | USD 60 | ≤ USD 90 | cost model |
| R3 | Repairable in the village | special tools needed | none | none | inspection |
| R4 | Survives a season's use | hours to first failure | 1500 h | ≥ 800 h | endurance test |
| R5 | Carried by one person | mass | 15 kg | ≤ 25 kg | weighing |

Requirements may be **functional** (what it does), **performance** (how well), **interfaces** (a 40 mm hose, a standard bicycle chain), **constraints** (regulations, standards) or about the whole life cycle: manufacture, maintenance, recycling. Gerhard Pahl and Wolfgang Beitz's German school divides them into *demands*, which must be met, and *wishes*, which are traded against each other, much like the "limit" and "target" columns above.

**Quality function deployment**, developed in Japan from the late 1960s by Yoji Akao and others, links each customer need to the engineering metrics that serve it in a matrix (the "house of quality"), and **benchmarking** measures existing products against the same metrics, showing whether a target is ambitious, ordinary or impossible.

</Section>

The bottom arrow is the reason for all the discipline: changing a requirement on day three costs a meeting; changing it after the moulds are cut costs the moulds. Organisations formalise this with **stage gates** (reviews a project must pass before more money is spent) and, in aerospace and medical devices, the **V-model** of systems engineering, which pairs each requirement with the test that will verify it. **Verification** asks "did we build the product right?", against the specification; **validation** asks "did we build the right product?", with real users in the real setting.

<Section id="concepts" kind="examples" title="Generating concepts and choosing one">

A **concept** is a working principle and a rough form: *how* the product will do its job. The commonest mistake at this stage is falling in love with the first idea. Good teams generate many on purpose.

- **Function decomposition.** Break the overall function ("lift water 4 m using leg power") into sub-functions: accept human power, convert it to a pumping motion, move water, prevent backflow, connect to a hose.
- **Morphological chart.** List several ways of achieving each sub-function in a table, then combine one from each row. Four ways to accept power, three pumping principles, three kinds of valve and two frames already give $4 \times 3 \times 3 \times 2 = 72$ combinations, most of which nobody would have thought of one at a time.
- **Analogy and nature.** See how other industries and living things solve the same sub-problem, like the kingfisher-nosed Shinkansen of the Start level.
- **TRIZ**, developed in the Soviet Union from the 1940s by Genrich Altshuller from a study of patents, treats inventive problems as contradictions (stronger *and* lighter) with general principles for resolving them.

Then the concepts must be compared. Stuart Pugh, at the University of Strathclyde, popularised a quick first filter: pick one concept as the *datum* and mark every other one better, same or worse on each criterion. For the final choice, teams use a **weighted decision matrix**.

<Definition term="weighted decision matrix">

Choose criteria $j = 1, \dots, m$ from the specification and give them weights $w_j \ge 0$ with $\sum_j w_j = 1$. Score each concept $i$ on each criterion, $s_{ij}$, on a common scale (here 1 to 5). The concept's weighted score is

$$S_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\, s_{ij},$$

a matrix–vector product $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{s}\,\mathbf{w}$. The highest score is the preferred concept, *if* the margin is larger than the uncertainty in the weights and scores.

</Definition>

Our cooperative's team considered four concepts: a **treadle pump** (two pistons worked by stepping, long used on small farms in Bangladesh and East Africa), a **rope-and-washer pump** (washers on a loop of rope drawn up through a pipe), a **lever-operated diaphragm pump**, and a small **solar-powered pump** as a benchmark. After building rough prototypes, they scored each concept against six criteria drawn from the specification.

</Section>

<Section id="computation-matrix" kind="computation" title="Computing it: the decision matrix and its sensitivity">

The program computes the weighted scores, then asks the question too many reports skip: if the weights had been a little different, would the answer change? It redraws the weights 20 000 times from a Dirichlet distribution centred on the agreed ones (a convenient way to generate random weights that are positive and sum to 1), and finally scans the weight on purchase cost.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Concept selection for a human-powered irrigation pump for small farms.
criteria = ["flow at 4 m head", "purchase cost", "local repair", "user effort", "durability", "portability"]
weights = np.array([0.25, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.05])
concepts = ["treadle pump", "rope-and-washer", "lever diaphragm", "small solar pump"]

# Scores from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent), agreed by the team after prototype tests.
scores = np.array([
    # flow cost repair effort durab. portab.
    [4,    4,   4,     3,     3,     3],   # treadle pump
    [3,    5,   5,     3,     2,     2],   # rope-and-washer
    [2,    3,   2,     4,     4,     5],   # lever diaphragm
    [5,    1,   1,     5,     4,     3],   # small solar pump
])

assert abs(weights.sum() - 1) < 1e-12
totals = scores @ weights                      # weighted sum, out of 5
order = np.argsort(-totals)
print("concept              weighted score   rank")
for rank, i in enumerate(order, start=1):
    print(f"{concepts[i]:18s}   {totals[i]:6.2f} / 5       {rank}")

# How fragile is the ranking? Re-draw the weights many times near the agreed ones.
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
trials = 20000
w = rng.dirichlet(weights * 40, size=trials)   # random weights, mean = agreed weights
winners = np.argmax(w @ scores.T, axis=1)
print("\nshare of 20 000 perturbed weightings won by each concept:")
for i, name in enumerate(concepts):
    print(f"  {name:18s} {np.mean(winners == i):6.1%}")

# What weight on purchase cost would change the winner?
print()
for cost_w in [0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.05, 0.0]:
    ww = weights.copy()
    ww[1] = cost_w
    ww = ww / ww.sum()                         # keep the weights summing to 1
    best = concepts[int(np.argmax(scores @ ww))]
    print(f"cost weight {cost_w:4.2f} (renormalised {ww[1]:.3f}): best = {best}")
```

The rope-and-washer pump scores 3.75 and the treadle pump 3.70, with the solar pump at 3.00 and the diaphragm pump at 2.90. A difference of 0.05 on a five-point scale is far smaller than the uncertainty in anyone's scores, and the sensitivity test says so plainly: the rope pump wins about 64% of the perturbed weightings and the treadle pump about 35%. Lower the weight on purchase cost from 0.25 to 0.20 and the treadle pump takes the lead; set it to zero and the solar pump wins.

<Narrator who="margaret">

When two concepts finish within a few per cent of each other, the matrix has not chosen; it has told you which two to test properly. Here the next step is a field trial of both pumps for a season, measuring flow, breakdowns and how tired the users are, so the scores on "flow" and "durability" become measurements instead of opinions.

</Narrator>

Notice also what a matrix cannot do. It cannot rescue a concept that fails a hard limit (the solar pump's cost of several times the limit in R2 should exclude it before scoring), and it cannot correct criteria that overlap: "purchase cost" and "local repair" both reward simple construction, so simplicity is counted twice.

</Section>

<Section id="embodiment" kind="human" title="Embodiment and detail design">

With a concept chosen, **embodiment design** gives it a definite form. The team decides the overall layout and the main dimensions, chooses materials and manufacturing processes, sizes the parts that carry load (with the stress analysis of <LessonLink slug="axial-torsion-and-bending">axial load, torsion and bending</LessonLink> and the checks of <LessonLink slug="failure-and-fatigue">failure and fatigue</LessonLink>), and selects standard parts such as bearings, seals and fasteners, the subject of the <LessonLink slug="machine-elements">next lesson</LessonLink>. Several principles guide this stage:

- **Clarity, simplicity, safety.** Each function should have one clear carrier; fewer parts mean fewer failures; a failure should be safe and visible.
- **Design for manufacture and assembly (DFM/DFA).** Parts easy to make with the chosen process; assembly with parts that cannot go in the wrong way, screws from one direction, and as few parts as the functions allow.
- **Failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA).** For each part, list how it could fail, the effect, its likelihood and detectability, and fix the worst risks in the design rather than in the instructions.

**Detail design** then fixes every dimension, tolerance, surface finish, material grade, heat treatment and coating, and the **bill of materials** (BOM), and writes the test plan that will verify each requirement. Design reviews at each stage bring in people who did not do the design, because the designer is the person least able to see what was assumed.

</Section>

<Section id="cad" kind="human" title="CAD: parametric modelling, assemblies and drawings">

Gaspard Monge's descriptive geometry, in France in the 1790s, gave the projections still used on every drawing; Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad at MIT in 1963 brought drawing to the computer; and the **parametric, feature-based solid model** that defines modern mechanical CAD reached the market in the late 1980s.

### Sketches, constraints and features

A part begins as a 2-D **sketch**: lines, arcs and circles tied by **geometric constraints** (horizontal, tangent, coincident, concentric, symmetric) and **dimensional constraints** (lengths, radii, angles). Each free point has two degrees of freedom and each constraint removes some; a **fully constrained** sketch has none left, so it changes only when you change a dimension. A constraint solver handles the resulting nonlinear equations, by the kind of Newton iteration you will use in <LessonLink slug="mechanisms-and-linkages">mechanisms and linkages</LessonLink>.

A **feature** turns a sketch into solid (a pad, pocket, revolve, sweep or loft) or dresses it (a fillet, chamfer, shell or draft). Features are stored in order in a **feature tree**, and the model is rebuilt from it whenever anything changes.

The skill of parametric modelling is capturing **design intent**: dimensioning a hole from the datum face it must line up with, not from whichever edge was handy; making the plate width depend on the hole size if that is the real rule; driving a family of parts from a table of parameters. A well-built model can be stretched to a new size in seconds; a badly built one collapses when the first early feature changes, because later features referred to edges that no longer exist (the "topological naming" problem, which every CAD kernel has to manage and which FreeCAD's 1.0 release in 2024 tackled for its own kernel).

Underneath, the solid is a **boundary representation** (B-rep): exact surfaces (planes, cylinders, NURBS) and the edges and vertices where they meet, maintained by a geometry **kernel** such as Parasolid (Siemens), ACIS and CGM (Dassault Systèmes) or the open-source Open CASCADE that FreeCAD uses. Models travel between programs as **STEP** files (ISO 10303), and as STL or 3MF meshes for printing.

### Assemblies and drawings

An **assembly** places parts with **mates** or **joints** (coincident faces, concentric axes, revolute or slider joints), which remove relative degrees of freedom as kinematic pairs do in a mechanism; the software then checks interference, measures clearances, computes mass properties and animates motion. Large products are built **top-down** from a skeleton model, so that moving a mounting point updates everything attached to it.

From the model the software projects 2-D **drawings** (views, sections, dimensions, tolerances, title block). Increasingly, the tolerances and notes are attached to the 3-D model itself as **product and manufacturing information** (PMI), called **model-based definition**, and product lifecycle management (PLM) systems control versions, approvals and changes.

### The tools

[FreeCAD](https://www.freecad.org/) is free and open source (LGPL), and covers the whole workflow above in its Sketcher, PartDesign, Assembly and TechDraw workbenches, with finite element and CAM workbenches besides; it is scriptable in Python. Among commercial packages, Dassault Systèmes in France makes SOLIDWORKS and CATIA (dominant in aerospace and car making); Siemens makes NX and Solid Edge; PTC in the United States makes Creo, descended from the first commercial parametric modeller, and the browser-based Onshape; Autodesk makes Inventor and Fusion; and ZWSOFT in China makes ZW3D. They share the concepts in this section and differ in price, licensing, specialist tools (sheet metal, surfacing, moulds, piping) and handling of large assemblies; most offer free or educational licences to students, on terms that change, so check each maker's.

CAD can also be written as code. Here is a mounting plate in CadQuery, a Python library built on Open CASCADE. It is shown for reading and is **not run here**, because CadQuery is not available in the browser:

```python
# CadQuery (not run here: needs the cadquery package and its geometry kernel)
import cadquery as cq

d = 12.0                     # hole diameter, mm
plate = (cq.Workplane("XY")
         .box(2 * d + 20 + 60, 2 * d + 20, 6)        # length, width, thickness
         .edges("|Z").fillet(4)
         .faces(">Z").workplane()
         .pushPoints([(-30, 0), (30, 0)]).hole(d))
cq.exporters.export(plate, "plate.step")
```

Code-based models are easy to version-control and to generate in families; interactive CAD remains faster for shaping and checking most parts.

<Section id="tolerances" kind="definitions" title="Tolerances, fits and GD&T">

No part is made exactly to size. A **tolerance** is the permitted variation, and choosing it is a design decision with consequences both ways: too loose and parts do not work together; too tight and every part costs more to make and inspect, because the process must be slower, the machine better, or the parts sorted.

<Definition term="limits, fits and the ISO system">

A size tolerance sets an upper and a lower limit, for example $\varnothing 25^{+0.021}_{0}$. A **fit** is the relationship between a hole and the shaft in it: a **clearance fit** always leaves a gap, an **interference fit** always overlaps (the shaft must be pressed or shrunk in), and a **transition fit** may do either. In the ISO system (ISO 286) a fit is written like $\varnothing 25\ \text{H7/g6}$: the letter gives the position of the tolerance zone relative to the nominal size (capital for holes, lower case for shafts; H puts the hole's lower limit exactly at nominal), and the number gives the **international tolerance grade**, IT6, IT7 and so on, how wide the zone is.

</Definition>

The grades are not arbitrary. The standard defines a tolerance unit that grows with the cube root of the size, $i = 0.45\sqrt[3]{D} + 0.001 D$ micrometres for $D$ in millimetres, and each grade is a fixed multiple of it (IT6 is $10i$, IT7 is $16i$). A larger part is harder to make to the same absolute accuracy, so it is allowed more. The program computes the grades from the formula and the limits of two common fits on a 25 mm bearing seat.

```python run
import numpy as np

# How big is a tolerance grade? The ISO system grows tolerances with the cube root of size.
def tolerance_unit_um(D):
    """Standard tolerance factor i in micrometres for a nominal size D in mm (sizes up to 500 mm)."""
    return 0.45 * D ** (1 / 3) + 0.001 * D

GRADE = {"IT6": 10, "IT7": 16, "IT8": 25, "IT9": 40, "IT11": 100}   # multiples of i

print("size (mm)   i (um)   " + "   ".join(f"{g:>5s}" for g in GRADE))
for D in [3, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500]:
    i = tolerance_unit_um(D)
    print(f"{D:8d}   {i:6.2f}   " + "   ".join(f"{m * i:5.0f}" for m in GRADE.values()))

# A 25 mm bearing seat. The standard groups sizes into ranges; 25 mm lies in 18-30 mm,
# and the formula is evaluated at the range's geometric mean.
D = np.sqrt(18 * 30)
i = tolerance_unit_um(D)
IT6, IT7 = 10 * i, 16 * i
print(f"\n25 mm (range 18-30 mm): IT6 = {IT6:.1f} um, IT7 = {IT7:.1f} um")

# Hole H7: lower deviation 0, upper +IT7.  Shaft g6: upper deviation -2.5 D^0.34, lower = upper - IT6.
hole = (0.0, round(IT7))
es = -round(2.5 * D ** 0.34)
shaft = (es - round(IT6), es)
print(f"hole  25 H7: +{hole[0]:.0f} to +{hole[1]:.0f} um")
print(f"shaft 25 g6: {shaft[0]:.0f} to {shaft[1]:.0f} um")
print(f"clearance: min {hole[0] - shaft[1]:.0f} um, max {hole[1] - shaft[0]:.0f} um  -> a sliding (clearance) fit")

# Shaft k6 for comparison: lower deviation +0.6 D^(1/3)
ei = round(0.6 * D ** (1 / 3))
shaft_k = (ei, ei + round(IT6))
print(f"shaft 25 k6: +{shaft_k[0]:.0f} to +{shaft_k[1]:.0f} um")
print(f"with H7: from {hole[0] - shaft_k[1]:.0f} um (interference) to {hole[1] - shaft_k[0]:.0f} um (clearance) -> a transition fit")
```

At 25 mm, IT7 is about 21 µm and IT6 about 13 µm: a quarter of a human hair. An H7 hole with a g6 shaft always slides, with 7 to 41 µm of clearance, as for a gear that must slide along a shaft. An H7 hole with a k6 shaft ranges from 15 µm of interference to 19 µm of clearance, a transition fit that locates a part accurately yet assembles with a light press. The standard rounds and tabulates these values by size range; for a real drawing, take the limits from ISO 286 or your CAD system's fit tables, and follow bearing makers' advice for bearing seats.

### Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing

Size limits say nothing about **form**, **orientation** or **location**. A shaft can be within its diameter limits and still be bent like a banana; a hole can be the right size in the wrong place. **Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing** (GD&T) adds a language for these. It is specified by ASME Y14.5 in the United States and by the ISO geometrical product specification (GPS) standards, chiefly ISO 1101, elsewhere; the two are similar but not identical.

The essentials:

- **Datums** are real features of the part (a flat face, an edge, a bore) that define a reference frame, chosen to match how the part is located when assembled. The order matters: the primary datum takes three degrees of freedom, the secondary two, the tertiary one, just as a part rests on a table, slides against a fence and stops against a pin.
- **Basic dimensions**, in boxes, give the exact ideal geometry; the tolerance lives in the feature control frame, not on the dimension.
- **Tolerance zones** have a shape: a cylinder around a hole's ideal axis for **position**, two parallel planes for **flatness**. The round zone matches function better than "±0.1 in x and y": that square allows 0.141 mm of error along a diagonal, and a hole that works there works 0.141 mm off in any direction, so the functional zone is the circle through the square's corners, ⌀0.283 mm, with $\pi/2 \approx 1.57$ times the square's area.
- **Material condition modifiers.** The circled M (maximum material condition) lets the zone grow by however much the hole exceeds its smallest size, since a bigger hole leaves more room for a bolt; this **bonus tolerance** suits quick checks with functional gauges.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The ASME and ISO systems differ in a default that catches people out. Under ASME Y14.5, a size tolerance also limits form (Rule #1, the envelope principle: a shaft at its largest size must be perfectly straight). Under ISO 8015 the default is independence: size and form are separate unless the drawing adds the envelope symbol Ⓔ. The same drawing can mean two different parts, so check which standard the title block invokes.

Write the gap as a sum of signed dimensions, $G = A - B_1 - S - H - B_2 - C$. Then there are three ways to find its spread, given each dimension's tolerance $\pm t_k$:

- **Worst case**: $G$ varies by $\pm \sum_k t_k$. Every part could be at its worst limit at once, so this guarantees 100% assembly, at the price of tight (expensive) part tolerances.
- **Statistical, root-sum-square (RSS)**: $\pm\sqrt{\sum_k t_k^2}$. If the dimensions vary independently and each tolerance is three standard deviations of a centred normal distribution, the variances add, and the result covers 99.73% of assemblies. With many parts this is much narrower than the worst case.
- **Monte Carlo**: build a large number of virtual assemblies from randomly drawn parts with whatever distributions the processes really produce, and count the failures.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Axial stack in a small gearbox: the end float G between the circlip and the outer bearing.
# sign +1: making this part bigger makes the gap bigger; -1: makes it smaller.
#            name               nominal   +/- tol   sign
stack = [("housing bore depth",  62.00,   0.10,   +1),
         ("bearing 1 width",     12.00,   0.06,   -1),
         ("spacer",              21.70,   0.05,   -1),
         ("gear hub",            14.00,   0.05,   -1),
         ("bearing 2 width",     12.00,   0.06,   -1),
         ("circlip thickness",    1.75,   0.03,   -1)]
G_min, G_max = 0.20, 0.80             # end float the bearings need (mm)

nominal = sum(s * n for _, n, _, s in stack)
wc = sum(t for _, _, t, _ in stack)
rss = np.sqrt(sum(t ** 2 for _, _, t, _ in stack))
print(f"nominal gap        {nominal:.2f} mm")
print(f"worst case         {nominal - wc:.2f} to {nominal + wc:.2f} mm  (+/- {wc:.2f})")
print(f"statistical (RSS)  {nominal - rss:.2f} to {nominal + rss:.2f} mm  (+/- {rss:.3f})")
print(f"requirement        {G_min:.2f} to {G_max:.2f} mm")

# Monte Carlo: build 200 000 gearboxes from parts drawn at random.
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 200_000
for shape in ["normal, tol = 3 sigma", "uniform over the tolerance"]:
    G = np.zeros(N)
    for _, n, t, s in stack:
        if shape.startswith("normal"):
            x = rng.normal(n, t / 3, N)
        else:
            x = rng.uniform(n - t, n + t, N)
        G += s * x
    bad = np.mean((G < G_min) | (G > G_max))
    print(f"\n{shape}: gap mean {G.mean():.3f}, std {G.std():.4f} mm")
    print(f"  0.1th-99.9th percentile {np.percentile(G, 0.1):.3f} to {np.percentile(G, 99.9):.3f} mm")
    print(f"  out of specification: {bad * 1e6:.0f} per million")

# Which tolerance matters most? Contribution to the variance of the gap.
print("\ncontribution to the variance of the gap:")
for name, _, t, _ in stack:
    print(f"  {name:20s} {t ** 2 / rss ** 2:6.1%}")
```

The nominal gap is 0.55 mm. The worst case runs from 0.20 to 0.90 mm and breaks the 0.80 mm limit, so a worst-case designer must tighten tolerances or adjust nominals. The RSS estimate, 0.40 to 0.70 mm, sits comfortably inside the requirement, and the Monte Carlo run with normal, centred parts agrees: no failures in 200 000 assemblies. But if each part is equally likely to be anywhere in its tolerance band (a uniform distribution, typical of a process that drifts between tool changes), the spread grows by 74% and about 800 assemblies per million fail. The variance breakdown shows where to spend money: the housing bore depth alone contributes 43% of the variance, so tightening that one tolerance does more than tightening the other five together.

<Narrator who="gideon">

RSS rests on three assumptions: the dimensions are independent, each process is centred on its nominal, and there are enough contributors for the sum to look normal. With two or three parts, with processes that drift, or for anything safety-critical, use the worst case or a Monte Carlo run with measured distributions. Some companies split the difference and inflate the RSS result by a factor such as 1.5.

</Narrator>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How it is done in industry">

In a company these stages run inside a quality system. ISO 9001 requires documented **design and development controls** (planned reviews, verification and validation records, controlled changes); medical-device and aerospace makers add stricter rules on risk management and traceability; and a change after release goes through an **engineering change** process, so that factory, suppliers and service manuals move together.

Tolerances are increasingly analysed in 3-D tools that run stack-ups on the CAD model's GD&T directly, and set against the **process capability** data the factory measures, which you will compute in <LessonLink slug="manufacturing-processes">manufacturing processes</LessonLink>. Simulation moves earlier every year, and topology optimisation and generative design now propose shapes from loads and constraints, the subject of <LessonLink slug="simulation-driven-design">simulation-driven design</LessonLink>. None of this replaces the judgement this lesson is about: deciding what the product is for, writing it down so it can be checked, and choosing honestly between alternatives.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The highest score in the decision matrix is the answer."** A matrix organises judgement; it does not replace it. If the top two scores are within the uncertainty of the weights, the matrix has not decided, and a sensitivity check or a test should.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Tighter tolerances make a better product."** They make a more expensive product. The right tolerance is the loosest one that still guarantees the function, found from the stack-up, not from habit.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"RSS is always fine because worst cases never happen."** RSS is fine when its assumptions hold. A process that drifts, a supplier who ships parts all near one limit, or a stack of only two parts can all make the worst case real.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Once the 3-D model is done, the design is done."** A model with no tolerances, materials, finishes or verification plan cannot be bought, made or inspected. Detail design is where most of a product's cost and quality are fixed.

</Misconception>
