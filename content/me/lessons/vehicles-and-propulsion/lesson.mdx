<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What it takes to move">

A bicycle, a car, a freight train, a container ship, an airliner and a rocket all solve the same problem: keep a mass moving against whatever resists it, carrying the energy to do so. What resists them differs enormously. The car fights the air and the deformation of its tyres; the train, almost only the air; the ship, the waves it makes; the airliner must also hold itself up; the rocket must carry everything it will ever push against. How they carry their energy differs too: food, petrol, batteries, kerosene, heavy fuel oil, liquid oxygen and hydrogen.

This lesson follows the mechanical engineer's method across all of them: draw the forces, compute the power and energy, and follow the energy back to where it came from. It uses the dynamics of <LessonLink slug="particle-dynamics">particles</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="rigid-body-dynamics">rigid bodies</LessonLink>, the engine cycles of <LessonLink slug="power-and-refrigeration-cycles">Power and refrigeration cycles</LessonLink>, the drag of <LessonLink slug="viscous-flow-and-pipes">Viscous flow and pipes</LessonLink> and the motors of <LessonLink slug="mechatronics-and-actuators">Mechatronics and actuators</LessonLink>. The numbers it produces explain a great deal about the world: why motorway speed matters so much, why electric cars do best in towns, why airliners have huge fans, and why rockets come in stages.

</Section>

Along the road, Newton's second law for the car (with its spinning wheels and drivetrain counted as a few per cent of extra mass) says that the force the road exerts on the driven tyres must supply four things.

<Definition term="road load and tractive force">

The **tractive force** needed to drive a vehicle of mass $m$ at speed $v$ and acceleration $a$ up a gradient of angle $\theta$ is
$$
F_t = \underbrace{C_{rr}\,mg\cos\theta}_{\text{rolling}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\rho\,C_dA\,v^2}_{\text{aerodynamic drag}} + \underbrace{mg\sin\theta}_{\text{gradient}} + \underbrace{m_\text{eff}\,a}_{\text{acceleration}},
$$
where $C_{rr}$ is the **rolling-resistance coefficient** (about 0.007–0.012 for car tyres on asphalt), $\rho$ the air density, $C_d$ the **drag coefficient** and $A$ the frontal area (their product $C_dA$ is what matters), and $m_\text{eff}$ the mass plus the equivalent mass of rotating parts. The first three terms are the **road load**. The power at the wheels is $P = F_tv$.

</Definition>

Rolling resistance comes from the tyre flexing as it rolls, and losing some of the energy as heat in the rubber, not from friction with the road; <LessonLink slug="rigid-body-dynamics">Rigid-body dynamics</LessonLink> showed that static friction at a rolling contact does no work. Aerodynamic drag grows with the square of speed, so the power to overcome it grows with the cube. Gradients are expressed as a percentage, $100\tan\theta$: a 6 per cent motorway climb is $\theta = 3.4°$.

</Section>

<Section id="road-computation" kind="computation" title="A car and an e-bike, computed">

The program computes the road load of a mid-sized car (1500 kg with its driver, $C_d = 0.28$ on 2.3 m² of frontal area, $C_{rr} = 0.010$) at several speeds, and of an electrically assisted bicycle with its rider (100 kg, an upright rider with $C_dA \approx 0.55$ m², $C_{rr} = 0.006$). In the European Union and many other countries such **pedelecs** may assist only while the rider pedals, up to 25 km/h, with a continuous motor rating of 250 W.

```python run
import numpy as np

g, rho = 9.81, 1.20                          # m/s^2; air density near sea level, kg/m^3

def road_load(m, Crr, CdA, v, grade=0.0):
    """Tractive force (N) to hold speed v (m/s) up a gradient: rolling + aerodynamic + grade."""
    th = np.arctan(grade)
    return Crr * m * g * np.cos(th) + 0.5 * rho * CdA * v**2 + m * g * np.sin(th)

# A mid-sized car with its driver, and an electric-assist bicycle with its rider.
car = dict(m=1500.0, Crr=0.010, CdA=0.28 * 2.3)     # drag coefficient 0.28 on 2.3 m^2 of frontal area
bike = dict(m=100.0, Crr=0.006, CdA=0.55)           # rider sitting upright

print("car on a level road")
print(" speed    rolling   drag    force   power at   energy at the wheels")
print("(km/h)      (N)     (N)     (N)    wheels(kW)   (kWh per 100 km)")
for kmh in (30, 50, 90, 120, 130):
    v = kmh / 3.6
    roll = road_load(car["m"], car["Crr"], 0.0, v)
    F = road_load(**car, v=v)
    print(f"{kmh:5d}   {roll:7.0f} {F - roll:7.0f} {F:7.0f}   {F * v / 1e3:7.1f}       {F * 1e5 / 3.6e6:6.1f}")

v = 25 / 3.6
F = road_load(**bike, v=v)
print(f"\ne-bike at 25 km/h on the level: {F:.1f} N, {F * v:.0f} W, {F * 1e5 / 3.6e6:.2f} kWh per 100 km")
# Up a 6 % hill, rider 100 W plus the motor's 250 W: what speed can they hold?
lo, hi = 0.1, 15.0
for _ in range(60):
    mid = (lo + hi) / 2
    lo, hi = (mid, hi) if road_load(**bike, v=mid, grade=0.06) * mid < 350.0 else (lo, mid)
print(f"e-bike up 6 %, 350 W in total: {3.6 * lo:.1f} km/h")
battery = 500.0                              # Wh
for share in (1.0, 0.5):
    print(f"range on the level at 25 km/h with a 500 Wh battery, motor supplying {100 * share:.0f} % "
          f"(85 % efficient): {battery * 0.85 / (share * F * v) * 25:.0f} km")

# Rolling resistance per tonne-kilometre: rubber tyres on asphalt against steel wheels on rails.
for name, Crr in (("car tyres", 0.010), ("e-bike tyres", 0.006), ("rail wheels", 0.0015)):
    print(f"{name:13s}: rolling resistance costs {Crr * 1000 * g * 1000 / 3.6e6 * 1000:.1f} Wh per tonne-km")
```

At town speeds the car's rolling resistance dominates; by 90 km/h drag has overtaken it, and at 130 km/h drag is more than three-quarters of the load. The power needed at the wheels rises from 3.1 kW at 50 km/h to 23.5 kW at 130 km/h, and the energy per kilometre nearly triples. The e-bike needs about 150 W at 25 km/h, which a fit rider can supply alone for a while; it uses 0.61 kWh per 100 km, about an eighteenth of what the car needs at 90 km/h, carrying the same person. On a 6 per cent climb, rider and motor together (350 W) hold 17 km/h, and a 500 Wh battery that supplies half the effort lasts about 140 km on the level.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The table gives a tidy rule for drivers. Going from 130 to 120 km/h cuts the energy per kilometre from 18.1 to 16.0 kWh per 100 km, 12 per cent, for a trip that takes 8 per cent longer. Power goes as speed cubed and energy per distance as speed squared (once drag dominates), so the last few km/h are the most expensive ones, for every kind of vehicle.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="powertrains" kind="computation" title="Combustion and electric powertrains compared">

A **powertrain** turns stored energy into tractive force. In a car with an internal-combustion engine, the engine follows (roughly) the Otto or Diesel cycle of <LessonLink slug="power-and-refrigeration-cycles">Power and refrigeration cycles</LessonLink>; the best car petrol engines reach a peak brake efficiency of about 40 per cent at their best operating point, and the largest marine diesels exceed 50 per cent. But a car engine rarely runs at its best point. At light load its friction and pumping losses are a large fraction of its output; it idles at traffic lights; and every stop throws the car's kinetic energy away as heat in the brakes. Gearboxes with many ratios, start–stop systems and hybrids exist to fight exactly these losses.

An electric powertrain has a battery, an inverter and a motor, usually with a single fixed gear, because an electric motor delivers high torque from rest and runs efficiently over a wide speed range. From battery to wheels it is typically 85 to 90 per cent efficient across most of its operating range; charging loses perhaps another 10 per cent. And it can run backwards: **regenerative braking** turns the motor into a generator and returns part of the braking energy to the battery.

The program compares the two over a home-made test cycle (not an official one): stop-start town driving, a country road at 80 km/h with one slowdown, and a motorway run at 120 km/h. The petrol car is modelled with a **Willans line**, a classic engine model in which fuel power is a fixed loss whenever the engine turns plus the output divided by a marginal efficiency. The electric car is 250 kg heavier, for its battery, with the same body.

```python run
import numpy as np

g, rho, dt = 9.81, 1.20, 0.1

def cycle(legs, accel=1.0, decel=1.0):
    """Speed trace (m/s) from (speed in km/h, seconds held) legs, ramping at the given rates."""
    v, out = 0.0, []
    for kmh, hold in legs:
        target = kmh / 3.6
        while abs(v - target) > 1e-9:
            v = min(target, v + accel * dt) if target > v else max(target, v - decel * dt)
            out.append(v)
        out += [v] * int(round(hold / dt))
    return np.array(out)

# A home-made test cycle (not an official one): stop-start town driving, a country road, a motorway.
parts = {
    "town":     cycle([(50, 30), (0, 30)] * 6 + [(30, 20), (0, 30)] * 4),
    "country":  cycle([(80, 300), (50, 40), (80, 300), (0, 10)], accel=0.8),
    "motorway": cycle([(120, 600), (0, 10)], accel=0.6),
}

def wheel_power(v, m, Crr, CdA):
    a = np.gradient(v, dt)
    F = Crr * m * g * (v > 0) + 0.5 * rho * CdA * v**2 + 1.05 * m * a   # 5 % extra for spinning parts
    return F * v                                                         # W; negative when braking

PETROL_KWH_PER_L = 8.9                       # lower heating value of petrol, about 32 MJ per litre

def petrol_car(v):
    """Fuel power (W): a Willans-line engine (6 kW of losses whenever it runs, 35 % marginal
    efficiency), 92 % efficient gearbox, 4 kW of fuel while idling, fuel cut off when slowing."""
    P = wheel_power(v, 1500.0, 0.010, 0.644)
    fuel = np.where(P > 0, 6e3 + P / (0.92 * 0.35), 0.0)
    fuel = np.where(v == 0, 4e3, fuel)
    return P, fuel

def electric_car(v):
    """Battery power (W): 88 % from battery to wheels and back, regeneration up to 50 kW,
    0.3 kW for electronics; a heavier car (battery) with the same body."""
    P = wheel_power(v, 1750.0, 0.010, 0.644)
    batt = np.where(P > 0, P / 0.88, -0.88 * np.minimum(-P, 50e3)) + 300.0
    return P, batt

print("           km   at the wheels    petrol car                       electric car")
print("                 used   braked    L/100km  kWh/100km  wheels/fuel    kWh/100km at plug")
for name, v in list(parts.items()) + [("all", np.concatenate(list(parts.values())))]:
    per100 = 100 / (v.sum() * dt / 1000)                 # converts kWh to kWh per 100 km
    kwh = lambda p: p.sum() * dt / 3.6e6 * per100
    P, fuel = petrol_car(v)
    _, batt = electric_car(v)
    print(f"{name:9s}{100 / per100:5.1f}  {kwh(P.clip(min=0)):5.1f}  {kwh(-P.clip(max=0)):5.1f}"
          f"      {kwh(fuel) / PETROL_KWH_PER_L:5.1f}    {kwh(fuel):5.1f}      {100 * kwh(P.clip(min=0)) / kwh(fuel):3.0f} %"
          f"          {kwh(batt) / 0.90:5.1f}")
```

In town the petrol car turns only 20 per cent of its fuel into work at the wheels, and half of that work, 6.0 of 11.6 kWh per 100 km, is then thrown away in the brakes. On the country road it does best, 26 per cent, and 29 per cent on the motorway, where the engine works hardest and closest to its efficient region. Over the whole cycle it uses 5.6 L/100 km, 50 kWh of fuel energy. The electric car uses 17.2 kWh per 100 km from the plug, about a third as much, and its advantage is greatest in town (11.5 against 57 kWh), where regeneration recovers braking energy and there is no idling, and smallest on the motorway (21 against 56 kWh), where drag dominates and both powertrains are working near their best.

<Narrator who="gideon">

That comparison stops at the plug and the pump, which flatters neither side fairly. Petrol must be extracted, refined and transported, and electricity generated and transmitted; a kilowatt-hour from a gas-fired plant at 50 per cent efficiency carries twice its energy in fuel, while one from wind, water, sunlight or nuclear does not burn fuel at all. Manufacturing the battery has its own energy and emissions cost. Honest comparisons follow the energy and emissions over the whole life of the vehicle and state the electricity mix they assume, as the analyses linked from the [IEA](https://www.iea.org/) do.

</Narrator>

<Section id="aircraft" kind="computation" title="Aircraft: thrust and propulsive efficiency">

An aircraft engine produces thrust by throwing air backwards. If air enters at the flight speed $v_0$ and leaves at the jet speed $v_j$, with mass flow $\dot m$ (and the exit pressure equal to the ambient), momentum gives the thrust
$$
T = \dot m\,(v_j - v_0).
$$
The useful power is $Tv_0$, but the engine also leaves kinetic energy in the wake, $\tfrac12\dot m(v_j - v_0)^2$ per second, so the **propulsive efficiency** is
$$
\eta_p = \frac{Tv_0}{Tv_0 + \tfrac12\dot m(v_j - v_0)^2} = \frac{2}{1 + v_j/v_0} .
$$
The same thrust can come from a little air accelerated a lot or a lot of air accelerated a little, and the second is far more efficient. That single fact shaped the modern airliner.

How efficiently an engine uses fuel is measured by its **thrust-specific fuel consumption** (TSFC), the fuel mass flow per unit thrust; a modern large turbofan in cruise burns roughly 15 g of kerosene per second for each kilonewton of thrust. An aircraft's range then follows from the **Breguet range equation**, which integrates the fuel burnt as the aircraft gets lighter:
$$
R = \frac{v_0}{g\,\text{TSFC}}\,\frac{L}{D}\,\ln\frac{m_\text{start}}{m_\text{end}},
$$
with $L/D$ the aircraft's lift-to-drag ratio in cruise. Three disciplines appear as three factors: propulsion (speed over TSFC), aerodynamics ($L/D$) and structures (how much of the take-off mass can be fuel).

```python run
import numpy as np

g = 9.81
v0 = 240.0                                   # cruise speed, m/s (about 860 km/h, Mach 0.8 up high)
T = 60e3                                     # thrust needed from one engine at cruise, N

print("exhaust   air through   propulsive   kinetic energy left")
print("speed     the engine    efficiency   in the wake")
for vj in (600.0, 450.0, 330.0, 290.0):
    mdot = T / (vj - v0)                     # thrust = mass flow x speed increase (exit pressure = ambient)
    eta_p = 2 / (1 + vj / v0)                # useful power T v0, divided by T v0 + wake kinetic power
    wake = 0.5 * mdot * (vj - v0) ** 2
    print(f"{vj:5.0f} m/s  {mdot:6.0f} kg/s     {eta_p:5.2f}        {wake / 1e6:5.1f} MW")

# Range: the Breguet equation for a jet airliner, and the same aircraft carrying batteries instead.
TSFC = 15e-6                                 # thrust-specific fuel consumption: 15 g per kN per second
LD = 18.0                                    # lift-to-drag ratio in cruise
LHV = 43.2e6                                 # kerosene, J/kg
eta_0 = v0 / (TSFC * LHV)                    # overall efficiency: thrust power / fuel power
fuel_fraction = 0.30                         # share of take-off mass that is fuel burnt in cruise
R_jet = v0 / (g * TSFC) * LD * np.log(1 / (1 - fuel_fraction))
print(f"\noverall efficiency of the turbofan, fuel to thrust power: {eta_0:.2f}")
print(f"jet range burning {100 * fuel_fraction:.0f} % of take-off mass: {R_jet / 1e3:,.0f} km")
# Batteries do not get lighter as they discharge, so range = eta * e * (L/D) * (battery fraction) / g.
e_batt = 0.25 * 3.6e6                        # 0.25 kWh per kg of battery pack, in J/kg
eta_e = 0.75                                 # battery to thrust power: motor, inverter, propeller
R_batt = eta_e * e_batt * LD * fuel_fraction / g
print(f"battery-electric range with {100 * fuel_fraction:.0f} % of take-off mass as batteries: {R_batt / 1e3:,.0f} km")
print(f"energy per kg: kerosene {LHV / 3.6e6:.1f} kWh, battery pack {e_batt / 3.6e6:.2f} kWh "
      f"(ratio {LHV / e_batt:.0f})")
```

A pure turbojet with a 600 m/s jet is 57 per cent propulsively efficient and leaves 10.8 MW of kinetic energy in its wake to make 60 kN; a turbofan whose mixed exhaust leaves at 290 m/s makes the same thrust at 91 per cent, by moving seven times as much air. That is why airliner engines have grown from slim turbojets in the 1950s to fans over three metres across, why bypass ratios have risen past ten, and why designers study open rotors and ever larger fans; the limits are the weight and drag of the nacelle and fitting it under the wing. The turbofan's overall efficiency, from fuel energy to thrust power, is 0.37, and the Breguet equation gives a range of about 10 500 km when 30 per cent of the take-off mass is fuel.

The same aircraft carrying batteries instead, of 0.25 kWh/kg (an optimistic pack-level figure as of 2026), would fly about 370 km, even though its electric propulsion is twice as efficient, because kerosene stores 48 times as much energy per kilogram and batteries do not get lighter as they discharge. Battery-electric flight therefore makes sense first for small aircraft on short routes, while long-haul aviation looks to **sustainable aviation fuels**, made from wastes, crops or captured carbon and hydrogen, and, further off, to hydrogen itself.

</Section>

<Section id="rockets" kind="definitions" title="Rockets and the rocket equation">

A rocket carries its propellant and throws it backwards; nothing outside is pushed against, which is why rockets work in a vacuum. As the propellant burns the rocket gets lighter, and that changes everything.

<Theorem name="The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation">

A rocket ejecting propellant at a constant speed $v_e$ relative to itself, with no external forces, changes its speed by
$$
\Delta v = v_e\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f},
$$
where $m_0$ is its mass before the burn and $m_f$ after. Engine makers quote $v_e$ as the **specific impulse** $I_{sp} = v_e/g_0$ in seconds, with $g_0 = 9.806\,65$ m/s².

</Theorem>

<Proof>

In a short time $\mathrm dt$ the rocket, of mass $m$ and speed $v$, ejects a mass $\mathrm dm_p$ at speed $v - v_e$ and keeps mass $m - \mathrm dm_p$ moving at $v + \mathrm dv$. With no external force, momentum is conserved: $mv = (m - \mathrm dm_p)(v + \mathrm dv) + \mathrm dm_p(v - v_e)$. Expanding and dropping the product of two small quantities leaves $m\,\mathrm dv = v_e\,\mathrm dm_p$. The rocket's mass falls by $\mathrm dm_p$, so $\mathrm dm = -\mathrm dm_p$ and $\mathrm dv = -v_e\,\mathrm dm/m$. Integrating from $m_0$ to $m_f$ gives $\Delta v = v_e\ln(m_0/m_f)$.

</Proof>

The logarithm is the whole story of rocketry. Every extra unit of $\Delta v$ multiplies the required **mass ratio** $m_0/m_f$ by the same factor, so a rocket that must reach low Earth orbit (about 7.8 km/s of orbital speed, plus roughly 1.5 to 2 km/s lost to gravity and drag on the way up, minus a little help from the Earth's rotation, about 9.4 km/s in all) must be mostly propellant. The escape is **staging**: drop the empty tanks and engines of the first stage once they have done their work, so that the rest of the flight does not have to accelerate them.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So what is a first stage for? Not to reach orbital speed, which it never does, but to lift the second stage out of the thick lower atmosphere and give it a head start of a few kilometres per second; then it falls away, taking its now-useless mass with it. A rocket is a series of ever-smaller rockets, each carried to its starting point by the one below.

</Narrator>

```python run
import numpy as np

g0 = 9.80665                                 # standard gravity, used to define specific impulse
dv_orbit = 9400.0                            # m/s to reach low Earth orbit, including gravity and drag losses

def payload_fraction(dvs, isps, eps):
    """Payload / lift-off mass for stages flown in order. dvs: delta-v of each stage (m/s);
    isps: specific impulses (s); eps: structural fraction of each stage, structure/(structure+propellant)."""
    frac = 1.0
    for dv, isp, e in zip(dvs, isps, eps):
        R = np.exp(dv / (isp * g0))          # Tsiolkovsky: mass before / mass after the burn
        f = (1 / R - e) / (1 - e)            # what this stage hands to the next, as a share of its own start
        if f <= 0:
            return 0.0
        frac *= f
    return frac

print(f"mass ratio needed for {dv_orbit / 1e3} km/s in one stage:")
for isp in (300, 350, 450):
    print(f"   Isp {isp} s (exhaust {isp * g0 / 1e3:.2f} km/s): {np.exp(dv_orbit / (isp * g0)):5.1f}, "
          f"so {100 * (1 - np.exp(-dv_orbit / (isp * g0))):.1f} % of lift-off mass must be propellant")

print("\npayload as a share of lift-off mass (stage structure 6 % of stage mass, upper 10 %)")
for isp in (300, 350):
    print(f"   one stage, Isp {isp} s: {100 * payload_fraction([dv_orbit], [isp], [0.06]):.2f} %")
for name, isp2 in (("kerosene upper stage, Isp 345 s", 345), ("hydrogen upper stage, Isp 450 s", 450)):
    splits = np.linspace(0.2, 0.8, 601)
    fr = [payload_fraction([s * dv_orbit, (1 - s) * dv_orbit], [300, isp2], [0.06, 0.10]) for s in splits]
    i = int(np.argmax(fr))
    print(f"   two stages, {name}: {100 * fr[i]:.2f} %, with the first stage giving {100 * splits[i]:.0f} % of the delta-v")

# Where the lift-off mass goes in the best hydrogen-upper-stage design (per cent).
s = splits[i]
R1, R2 = np.exp(s * dv_orbit / (300 * g0)), np.exp((1 - s) * dv_orbit / (450 * g0))
pi1 = (1 / R1 - 0.06) / 0.94                 # upper stage + payload, as a share of lift-off mass
pi2 = (1 / R2 - 0.10) / 0.90                 # payload, as a share of the upper stage's start mass
parts = {"stage 1 propellant": 0.94 * (1 - pi1), "stage 1 structure": 0.06 * (1 - pi1),
         "stage 2 propellant": pi1 * 0.90 * (1 - pi2), "stage 2 structure": pi1 * 0.10 * (1 - pi2),
         "payload": pi1 * pi2}
print("\n" + ", ".join(f"{k} {100 * v:.1f} %" for k, v in parts.items()))
```

With kerosene and liquid oxygen ($I_{sp} \approx 300$ s), a single stage would have to be 95.9 per cent propellant. If its tanks, engines and structure weigh 6 per cent of the stage, which is already excellent, nothing is left for payload. With a better engine ($I_{sp}$ = 350 s) a single stage delivers 0.5 per cent, too thin a margin to survive the first kilogram of extra structure. Two stages deliver 2.5 per cent with a kerosene upper stage and 4.8 per cent with a hydrogen one, whose higher exhaust speed makes it worth giving the upper stage more of the work: the optimum first stage then supplies only 31 per cent of the $\Delta v$.

Since the 2010s, **reusable first stages** that fly back and land, first flown in routine service by SpaceX's Falcon 9 and now pursued by companies and agencies in the USA, China, Europe and elsewhere, have changed the economics without changing the equation: the propellant needed to return costs payload, which the reuse repays in hardware.

Rocket propellants are among the most dangerous substances engineers handle: they are designed to release enormous energy quickly, and liquid oxygen, hydrogen and the toxic propellants of some engines each bring their own hazards. That is why rocket engines are developed only by trained teams on remote test stands with blast protection and strict procedures. The safe way to explore rocket design is simulation, for example with the open-source [OpenRocket](https://openrocket.info/), and the equations in this lesson.

<Section id="ships-trains" kind="human" title="Ships and trains, in brief">

**Trains** move freight and people with remarkably little energy, for two mechanical reasons. A steel wheel on a steel rail barely deforms, so its rolling-resistance coefficient is about 0.001–0.002, five to ten times less than a tyre's: the program above found 4 Wh per tonne-kilometre for rolling resistance on rails against 27 Wh for car tyres. And a long train hides most of its wagons in the wake of the ones in front, so its drag per tonne is small. Electric trains add regenerative braking, feeding energy back into the overhead line when they slow down, and high-speed lines, from Japan's Shinkansen (1964) to the networks of Europe and China, compete with flying on journeys of a few hundred kilometres.

**Ships** carry most of the world's traded goods. A hull pushing through water loses energy to friction on its wetted surface and, increasingly as speed rises, to the waves it makes; at typical speeds the power needed rises roughly with the cube of speed. So **slow steaming** pays: 10 per cent slower needs about $0.9^3 = 0.73$ of the power, and since the voyage takes $1/0.9 = 1.11$ times as long, about $0.9^2 = 0.81$ of the fuel, a saving of nearly a fifth at the cost of time. The largest ships are driven by slow-speed two-stroke diesel engines turning the propeller directly, which are among the most efficient heat engines ever built. The International Maritime Organization's 2023 strategy aims at net-zero emissions from international shipping by or around 2050, and ships running on methanol, liquefied natural gas and, experimentally, ammonia, together with wind-assist sails and rotors, are being built and tested as of 2026.

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Vehicles and propulsion in professional practice">

**Testing to a cycle.** Cars are certified by driving them on rollers through a standard drive cycle, such as the WLTP adopted at [UNECE](https://unece.org/) and used in the European Union, Japan and a number of other countries, or the cycles of the US Environmental Protection Agency; the lesson's home-made cycle imitates the idea, not any official cycle. Engineers then build **longitudinal vehicle models** like the one above, with measured engine maps, gearbox losses and battery models, to choose gear ratios, size batteries, tune hybrid strategies and predict real-world range, often in tools such as those described in <LessonLink slug="system-modelling">Modelling dynamic systems</LessonLink>.

**Engines and aircraft.** Aircraft engines are certified by national authorities against standards for safety, noise and emissions coordinated through [ICAO](https://www.icao.int/), after years of rig, ground and flight testing. Engine makers in the USA, the UK, France, Germany, Canada, Japan and China and their partners compete on TSFC, weight, durability and noise; the airframe makers on $L/D$ and structural weight.

**Space.** National agencies, among them NASA, ESA, Roscosmos, ISRO, JAXA and CNSA, and a growing number of companies design launch vehicles with exactly the trade-offs computed here, stage by stage, with far more detailed models of gravity and drag losses and trajectories. Lumenary ME returns to them in <LessonLink slug="space-and-fusion-engineering">Space and fusion engineering</LessonLink>, and to the plants that generate the electricity for vehicles in <LessonLink slug="power-plants-and-energy-conversion">Power plants and energy conversion</LessonLink>.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

Richard Trevithick ran a steam locomotive on rails in Wales in 1804. Karl Benz patented his petrol-engined *Motorwagen* in 1886, and in 1888 Bertha Benz drove it over a hundred kilometres to prove it practical; Rudolf Diesel's engine followed in the 1890s, and electric cars, popular around 1900, lost out to petrol for most of the twentieth century before returning with lithium-ion batteries. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published the rocket equation in 1903, and Robert Goddard flew the first liquid-fuelled rocket in 1926. Frank Whittle in Britain and Hans von Ohain in Germany independently invented the jet engine in the 1930s, and von Ohain's engine first flew in 1939. A Soviet R-7 rocket put Sputnik into orbit in 1957, and the first high-bypass turbofans entered airline service around 1970, with the wide-body jets they made possible.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Jet engines and rockets work by pushing against the air behind them."** They push on the gas they throw backwards, and the gas pushes back on them (Newton's third law). Air resistance outside is a nuisance, not a help: a rocket works better in a vacuum, and a jet engine needs air only as the stuff it accelerates and burns its fuel in.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Driving a bit faster costs only a bit more fuel."** Once drag dominates, energy per kilometre grows with the square of speed and power with the cube. From 120 to 130 km/h, 8 per cent faster, the car above needed 13 per cent more energy per kilometre and 22 per cent more power.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Twice the propellant gives a rocket twice the speed."** Because of the logarithm in the rocket equation, each extra kilogram of propellant must also accelerate all the propellant still unburnt. Doubling the mass ratio adds only $v_e\ln 2$, about 0.7 exhaust speeds, to the $\Delta v$, however large the rocket.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Electric vehicles use no energy and cause no emissions."** They use about a third of the energy of a comparable petrol car at the plug, and none of their emissions come from the exhaust pipe, but the electricity must be generated and the battery made. How much cleaner they are depends on the electricity mix and the vehicle's life, which is why careful studies state both.

</Misconception>
