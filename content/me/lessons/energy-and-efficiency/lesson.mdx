<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What is energy, and where does it go?">

Look at the label on a banana or a packet of biscuits and you will see a number in **kilojoules** (kJ). A medium banana gives your body roughly 400 kJ. A full phone battery stores roughly 50 to 70 kJ. So in one sense, a banana holds six or seven times as much energy as a charged phone.

But your phone cannot run on bananas, and you cannot run on a battery. Energy comes in different forms, and machines exist to change it from one form into another: a car engine turns the energy in fuel into motion, a kettle turns electricity into hot water, your muscles turn food into movement. Engineers spend a lot of their lives asking two questions about every machine. **How much energy does it need?** And **how much of that energy does what we actually want?**

<Narrator who="chloe">

The surprising idea at the heart of this lesson is that energy is never made and never destroyed: the total always stays the same. So when we say a machine "wastes" energy, the energy hasn't vanished. Where has it gone? Keep that question in mind.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="stores" kind="intuition" title="Stores and transfers">

Energy is what lets things happen: things move, heat up, light up, make sounds. It is useful to think of energy sitting in **stores** and moving between them along **transfers**.

| Store | Where you find it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kinetic | anything moving | a rolling football, a spinning wind turbine |
| Gravitational potential | anything raised up | water behind a dam, a roller coaster at the top of a hill |
| Elastic | anything stretched or squashed | a wound-up clockwork spring, a bent bow, a compressed car spring |
| Thermal | anything warm: its particles jiggle faster | a cup of tea, hot exhaust gas |
| Chemical | food, fuels, batteries | rice, petrol, wood, a phone battery |
| Nuclear | the centres of atoms | the fuel of a nuclear power station, and the Sun |

Energy moves between stores in four main ways: when a **force** moves something (this is called doing **work**), by **heating**, by an **electric current**, and by **light and sound**. A cyclist pedalling uphill moves energy from the chemical store in her muscles, by work, into the kinetic store of the bike and the gravitational store of being higher up, and some of it, by heating, into the thermal store of her body and the air.

All energy is measured in **joules** (J). One joule is small: lifting an apple of about 100 grams up by 1 metre takes about 1 J. That is why food labels use kilojoules (1 kJ = 1000 J) and electricity bills use **kilowatt-hours** (1 kWh = 3 600 000 J).

</Section>

<Section id="formulas" kind="definitions" title="Measuring energy">

### Work

You met work in <LessonLink slug="simple-machines">Simple machines</LessonLink>: when a force moves something, the energy transferred is

$$
\text{work} = \text{force} \times \text{distance}.
$$

### Kinetic energy

<Definition term="kinetic energy">

An object of mass $m$ (kg) moving at speed $v$ (m/s) has **kinetic energy**

$$
E_k = \tfrac12 \times m \times v^2 \quad \text{(joules)}.
$$

</Definition>

Look at that $v^2$. A cyclist and bike of 60 kg at 5 m/s have ½ × 60 × 5 × 5 = 750 J of kinetic energy. At 10 m/s, twice the speed, they have ½ × 60 × 10 × 10 = 3000 J: **four times** as much. Brakes must turn all of that energy into heat, so stopping from twice the speed takes about four times the distance. This is why speed limits near schools are low, and why a small rise in speed makes crashes much worse.

### Gravitational potential energy

<Definition term="gravitational potential energy">

Raising an object of mass $m$ (kg) by a height $h$ (m) stores

$$
E_p = m \times g \times h \quad \text{(joules)}
$$

in its gravitational potential store, where $g = 9.8$ N/kg on Earth.

</Definition>

This is just work again: the force needed to lift the object is its weight, $m \times g$, and it moves through the height $h$. Lifting a 2 kg bag onto a shelf 1.5 m up stores 2 × 9.8 × 1.5 = 29.4 J.

### Conservation of energy

<Theorem name="Conservation of energy">

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be moved from one store to another. In any closed system, the total amount of energy stays exactly the same.

</Theorem>

This is one of the most thoroughly tested laws in all of science. Nobody has ever found an exception, and engineers use it as a strict accounting rule: every joule that goes in must come out somewhere.

<Example title="How fast is the swing?">

Lena pulls a swing back until its seat is 0.8 m above its lowest point, and lets go. If no energy is lost to the air or the chains, all of the potential energy at the top becomes kinetic energy at the bottom:

$$
m \times g \times h = \tfrac12 \times m \times v^2.
$$

The mass $m$ is on both sides, so it cancels: $v^2 = 2 \times g \times h = 2 \times 9.8 \times 0.8 = 15.68$, and $v$ is the square root of that, about **4.0 m/s**. A heavy adult and a small child pulled back to the same height reach the same speed at the bottom.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="coaster" kind="computation" title="A roller coaster, computed">

A roller coaster is a giant conservation-of-energy machine. A chain hauls the car to the top of the first hill, filling its gravitational store. From then on there is no engine at all: the car trades height for speed going down, and speed for height going up.

The program adds up the car's energy at the top of the first hill, then, at each point, subtracts the potential energy there to find what is left as kinetic energy, and turns that into a speed. It does this twice: once for a perfect track, and once with friction and air resistance acting like a steady 150 N force holding the car back, so that 150 J of energy turns into heat for every metre travelled.

```python run
# Energy bookkeeping for a roller-coaster car rolling along a track.
g = 9.8              # N/kg
mass = 600           # kg: the car and its riders
start_speed = 2.0    # m/s as the car tips over the top of the first hill
drag = 150           # N: friction and air resistance together, always against the motion

# (name, height in metres, distance along the track from the top of the first hill in metres)
track = [
    ("A  top of the first hill", 40, 0),
    ("B  bottom of the first drop", 2, 60),
    ("C  second hill", 30, 110),
    ("D  valley", 5, 160),
    ("E  third hill", 35, 220),
    ("F  brakes", 3, 300),
]

total = mass * g * track[0][1] + 0.5 * mass * start_speed ** 2   # potential + kinetic energy at A
print(f"Total energy at the start: {total:.0f} J")
print()
print("point                         height  speed with no losses  speed with drag")
for name, height, along in track:
    potential = mass * g * height
    ideal_kinetic = total - potential                  # nothing lost: energy only changes form
    real_kinetic = total - potential - drag * along    # drag has turned some energy into heat
    ideal = (2 * ideal_kinetic / mass) ** 0.5
    if real_kinetic < 0:
        print(f"{name:28} {height:5} m {ideal:12.1f} m/s       cannot get here: short by {-real_kinetic:.0f} J")
        break
    real = (2 * real_kinetic / mass) ** 0.5
    print(f"{name:28} {height:5} m {ideal:12.1f} m/s {real:12.1f} m/s")
```

On a perfect track, the car could climb hill E easily, still doing 10.1 m/s at the top, because E (35 m) is lower than A (40 m). But with drag, by the time the car has travelled 220 m it has turned 150 × 220 = 33 000 J into heat. It arrives 2400 J short of what it needs to reach the top of E, so it would slow to a stop on the slope and roll backwards.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Change the height of hill E from `35` to `30` and run it again: now the car crosses E at 9.5 m/s and reaches the brakes at 24.1 m/s. Then try `drag = 0` and see the two speed columns agree exactly. Real designers do this kind of energy budget first, and only then build detailed computer models of every curve.

</Narrator>

This is why, on almost every roller coaster, the first hill is the tallest and each later hill is lower than the one before: the car must keep enough energy in hand to cover all the losses still to come. At the end, the brakes at F use friction (or magnets) to turn the remaining kinetic energy into heat, so the car can stop gently at the station.

<Narrator who="gideon">

A designer cannot rely on "just enough" energy: on a cold, windy day, or with a lighter or heavier car, the losses change. So real coasters are designed with a generous margin, and the lift hill has a ratchet (the clack-clack-clack you hear on the way up) that stops the car from ever rolling backwards down it.

</Narrator>

<Section id="power-efficiency" kind="examples" title="Power and efficiency">

### Power

Two people can do the same work, one quickly and one slowly. **Power** measures how fast energy is transferred.

<Definition term="power">

$$
\text{power} = \frac{\text{energy transferred}}{\text{time taken}}
$$

measured in **watts** (W): 1 W is 1 joule per second. A kilowatt (kW) is 1000 W.

</Definition>

<Example title="Running up the stairs">

Siddharth, 50 kg, runs up a flight of stairs 3 m high in 5 seconds. He gains 50 × 9.8 × 3 = 1470 J of potential energy, so his useful power is 1470 ÷ 5 = **294 W**. If he walks up in 15 seconds, the energy is exactly the same, but the power is a third as much: 98 W.

</Example>

Some powers to compare: a phone charger delivers somewhere between about 5 and 60 W, a fit cyclist can keep up about 150 to 250 W for an hour, a kettle draws about 2000 to 3000 W, a family car's engine can deliver tens of thousands of watts or more, and a large wind turbine several million. The old unit **horsepower**, chosen by the Scottish engineer James Watt in the 1780s to sell steam engines to mine owners who used horses, is about 746 W.

### Efficiency

<Definition term="efficiency">

The **efficiency** of a machine is the fraction of the energy put in that ends up doing what we want:

$$
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy out}}{\text{total energy in}} \times 100\%.
$$

</Definition>

<Example title="How efficient is a kettle?">

Warming 1 kg of water by 1 °C takes about 4200 J. So heating 1 litre (1 kg) of water from 20 °C to 100 °C needs 4200 × 80 = 336 000 J. Suppose a 2000 W kettle takes 210 seconds to do it. It drew 2000 × 210 = 420 000 J from the socket. Its efficiency is 336 000 ÷ 420 000 = 0.8, or **80%**. The other 84 000 J warmed the kettle itself and the air around it.

</Example>

No real machine is 100% efficient. The "wasted" energy has not disappeared: it has nearly always ended up as **thermal energy**, spread thinly through the surroundings by friction, by electrical resistance and by sound. Spread-out heat like this is almost impossible to collect and use again, which is why we call it wasted.

A **heat engine**, such as the petrol or diesel engine in a car, burns fuel to make hot gas, and lets the hot gas push on pistons. A typical car engine, as it is actually driven, turns only about a fifth to a third of its fuel's energy into motion; the best car engines reach about 40% at their most efficient, and the giant diesel engines of container ships a little over 50%. The rest leaves as heat. This is not just poor design: there is a deep law of physics that stops any heat engine from turning all of its heat into work, which you will meet in <LessonLink slug="second-law-and-entropy">The second law and entropy</LessonLink>.

An **electric motor** uses magnetic forces instead of heat, and has no such limit: small motors are often 70 to 90% efficient, and large industrial motors above 95%. Motors are also quiet and make no exhaust where they are used.

<Narrator who="gideon">

A fair comparison follows the energy all the way back to its source. The electricity for a motor had to be made somewhere: in a power station burning gas (often 40 to 60% efficient), or from wind, sunlight, water or nuclear fuel, and some is lost in the wires and while charging a battery. And petrol stores roughly 30 to 50 times as much energy per kilogram as today's lithium-ion batteries, which is why electric cars carry heavy battery packs. The motor itself is far more efficient; the honest answer to "which is better?" depends on the whole chain.

</Narrator>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="How energy was discovered">

The idea of energy took a long time to settle. In the 1740s the French scientist and writer Émilie du Châtelet argued, using experiments by the Dutch scientist Willem 's Gravesande in which brass balls pressed dents into soft clay, that the "force" of a moving body grows with the *square* of its speed, an early form of the kinetic energy formula. In the 1840s the English brewer and scientist James Prescott Joule let falling weights turn a paddle wheel inside a tank of water, and measured the tiny rise in the water's temperature. He showed that a given amount of work always produces the same amount of heat, so heat and work are two forms of the same thing. At about the same time, Julius Robert Mayer in Germany and Hermann von Helmholtz stated the law of conservation of energy in general form. The unit of energy is named after Joule, and the unit of power after James Watt.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Energy gets used up."** Energy is never destroyed. When we "use" energy, we move it from a concentrated, useful store (fuel, a battery, water behind a dam) into spread-out thermal energy in the surroundings, which is very hard to use again. What gets used up is the *usefulness*, not the energy.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A clever enough machine could run for ever, or give out more energy than it takes in."** Such "perpetual motion machines" have been proposed for centuries, and every one breaks conservation of energy or ignores friction. No machine can give out more energy than goes in, and every real machine loses some as heat, so without a supply of energy every machine eventually stops.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Heavier things go faster down a slope."** Without friction, the speed gained from a drop does not depend on mass at all: in $m g h = \tfrac12 m v^2$ the mass cancels. A heavier car has more energy, but it also needs more energy for each metre per second of speed.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Power and energy are the same thing."** Energy (joules, or kilowatt-hours) is an amount; power (watts) is how fast that amount is moved. A 2000 W kettle switched on for half an hour uses 1 kWh of energy; a 10 W lamp would need 100 hours to use the same.

</Misconception>
