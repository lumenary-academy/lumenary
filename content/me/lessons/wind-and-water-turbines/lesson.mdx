<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Harvesting moving fluids">

Water wheels and windmills were humanity's first engines. Vertical-axis windmills ground grain in Sistan, on today's border between Iran and Afghanistan, more than a thousand years ago; water wheels powered mills across the Roman world, China and medieval Europe; Dutch windmills drained a country. Their descendants are some of the most refined machines engineers build. In 2025, according to Ember, hydropower generated about 14% of the world's electricity and wind about 8.5%, with wind and solar together at 17.3%. A modern wind turbine's blades are longer than a football pitch, and the largest hydro turbines, at China's Baihetan dam, each deliver 1,000 MW.

Lumenary EE's [renewables and storage](/en/ee/renewables-and-storage/) derives the Betz limit and follows wind and hydro power into the grid. This lesson takes the mechanical engineer's view: the forces on a rotor and its tower, how a blade is shaped and why, how the shape determines the power curve and the energy a site will yield, and how hydro turbines are chosen for very different rivers and mountains.

A wind turbine and a hydro turbine do the same job, taking energy out of a moving fluid, but from opposite ends of a scale. Wind is a thin fluid at low pressure spread over a huge area, so a wind turbine must be enormous and light; water is dense and can be gathered behind a dam at high pressure, so a hydro turbine can be compact and immensely strong. Most of the differences between them follow from that.

</Section>

<Section id="momentum" kind="definitions" title="Momentum theory: power, thrust and the Betz limit">

Model the rotor as an **actuator disc**: a thin, permeable disc of area $A$ that extracts energy uniformly from a steady, incompressible stream of speed $V$. The air slowing down ahead of the disc must spread out, so the flow through the disc is a **stream tube** that widens downstream. Define the **axial induction factor** $a$ by the speed at the disc, $V(1 - a)$.

<Theorem name="Momentum theory of an ideal rotor">

For an actuator disc in a steady, incompressible, frictionless stream, the speed at the disc is the average of the far upstream and far downstream speeds, so the wake speed is $V(1 - 2a)$. The thrust and power are
$$T = \tfrac12\rho A V^2 \cdot C_T, \quad C_T = 4a(1 - a); \qquad P = \tfrac12\rho A V^3 \cdot C_P, \quad C_P = 4a(1 - a)^2.$$
$C_P$ is largest, $16/27 \approx 0.593$ (the **Betz limit**), at $a = 1/3$, where $C_T = 8/9$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Apply the momentum equation to the stream tube: with mass flow $\dot m = \rho A V(1 - a)$ and wake speed $V_w$, the thrust is $T = \dot m(V - V_w)$. Apply Bernoulli's equation separately upstream and downstream of the disc (not across it, where energy is removed): the pressure drop across the disc is $\Delta p = \tfrac12\rho(V^2 - V_w^2)$, and $T = A\,\Delta p$. Equating the two expressions for $T$ gives $V(1 - a) = \tfrac12(V + V_w)$, so $V_w = V(1 - 2a)$ and $T = 2\rho A V^2 a(1 - a)$. The power is the thrust times the speed at the disc, $P = TV(1 - a) = 2\rho A V^3 a(1 - a)^2$. Setting $dC_P/da = 4(1 - a)(1 - 3a) = 0$ gives $a = 1/3$. Lumenary EE's [renewables and storage](/en/ee/renewables-and-storage/) gives the same derivation through the kinetic energy of the stream.

</Proof>

The thrust is the mechanical engineer's first concern, because it acts at the top of a tower. The program tabulates the two coefficients and computes the thrust and overturning moment for a 120 m rotor on a 100 m tower.

```python run
import numpy as np

# One-dimensional momentum theory of an ideal rotor (an "actuator disc").
# a is the axial induction factor: the wind slows from V far upstream to V(1 - a) at the disc.
print("   a     Cp = 4a(1-a)^2   CT = 4a(1-a)   wake speed / V")
for a in (0.1, 0.2, 1 / 3, 0.4, 0.5):
    print(f"{a:6.3f}{4 * a * (1 - a) ** 2:13.3f}{4 * a * (1 - a):16.3f}{1 - 2 * a:15.2f}")

# What the rotor does to the tower: thrust T = CT * 1/2 rho A V^2 acts at hub height.
rho, R, hub = 1.225, 60.0, 100.0        # kg/m^3, rotor radius (m), hub height (m)
A = np.pi * R**2
for V, CT in ((10.6, 0.8), (25.0, 0.1)):  # near rated wind; at cut-out with the blades pitched
    T = CT * 0.5 * rho * A * V**2
    print(f"V = {V:4.1f} m/s, CT = {CT}: thrust {T / 1e3:5.0f} kN, "
          f"overturning moment at the base {T * hub / 1e6:5.1f} MN m")
```

Beyond $a = 1/3$ the rotor extracts less power but pushes ever harder on the air; at $a = 0.5$ the wake would stop completely, which is impossible, and above about $a = 0.4$ real rotors enter a **turbulent wake state** that momentum theory cannot describe. Engineers then switch to empirical corrections first proposed by Hermann Glauert in the 1920s. Near rated wind, the 120 m rotor pushes on its tower with about 623 kN, a moment of 62.3 MN m at the base, which the tower, its foundation and every bolt between them must carry, millions of times over the turbine's life as the wind gusts.

Those two thrust cases are only the start of a load analysis. The IEC 61400-1 standard defines dozens of design load cases: normal turbulence, extreme gusts and changes of wind direction, faults during operation, emergency stops, and a parked rotor in the strongest gust expected in 50 years, which for a parked turbine can produce larger tower loads than anything in operation. Fatigue, the accumulation of 10⁸ or more load cycles, often sizes the blades and tower more than any single extreme load.

<Section id="blade-elements" kind="intuition" title="Rotation and blade elements">

A real rotor turns. Its **tip-speed ratio**
$$\lambda = \frac{\Omega R}{V}$$
compares the speed of the blade tips, $\Omega R$, with the wind; large three-bladed turbines work best at $\lambda \approx 7$ to 9, so in a 10 m/s wind their tips move at 70 to 90 m/s. Turning the rotor costs something: by Newton's third law, the torque the air exerts on the blades is matched by a torque the blades exert on the air, so the wake **rotates**, and the kinetic energy of that rotation is lost. The faster the rotor turns for a given power, the smaller its torque and the smaller this loss, which is one reason modern rotors are fast and slender rather than slow and many-bladed like an old farm windpump.

To design a blade we cut it into short **blade elements** at radius $r$ and treat each as a two-dimensional aerofoil. The element meets a **relative wind** $W$ made of the axial wind at the rotor, $V(1 - a)$, and the speed at which the blade moves through the air, $\Omega r(1 + a')$, where $a'$ is a small **tangential induction factor** from the wake's rotation. The relative wind arrives at the **inflow angle** $\phi$ to the rotor plane, with $\tan\phi = V(1 - a)/[\Omega r(1 + a')]$. The blade's chord is set at the **twist** (plus pitch) angle $\theta$, so the aerofoil sees an **angle of attack** $\alpha = \phi - \theta$.

Lift $L$ acts perpendicular to $W$ and drag $D$ along it. Resolving them gives a force along the rotor plane, which drives the rotor, and a force along the axis, which is thrust:
$$C_t = C_l\sin\phi - C_d\cos\phi, \qquad C_n = C_l\cos\phi + C_d\sin\phi.$$
Near the tip $\Omega r$ is large and $\phi$ small, so the blade is set nearly flat to the rotor plane; near the root $\Omega r$ is small, $\phi$ is large, and the blade must be twisted steeply to keep the angle of attack near its best. That is why every wind-turbine blade is twisted, typically by 20° or more from root to tip, and why its chord shrinks towards the tip: the outer blade moves fastest and needs less area to produce the same force.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The design idea is to make every element of the blade work at the angle of attack where its aerofoil has the best ratio of lift to drag, and to choose the chord so that each annulus of the rotor slows the wind by the Betz-optimal amount. Twist serves the first aim, chord the second. The blade's odd shape, wide and steeply twisted at the root and slender and flat at the tip, is the picture of those two rules.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="bem" kind="computation" title="A blade element momentum solver">

**Blade element momentum (BEM) theory** joins the two pictures. For each annulus of the rotor, momentum theory gives the thrust and torque in terms of $a$ and $a'$, and blade-element theory gives them in terms of the aerofoil's lift and drag at the angle of attack, which itself depends on $a$ and $a'$. Equating the two gives a pair of equations for each annulus, solved by iteration:
$$a = \frac{1}{\dfrac{4F\sin^2\phi}{\sigma C_n} + 1}, \qquad a' = \frac{1}{\dfrac{4F\sin\phi\cos\phi}{\sigma C_t} - 1},$$
where $\sigma = Bc/(2\pi r)$ is the local **solidity** ($B$ blades of chord $c$), and $F$ is Ludwig Prandtl's **tip-loss factor**, which accounts for air leaking round the tips of a rotor with a finite number of blades.

The program designs a 120 m, three-bladed rotor with the classic optimum chord and twist for a tip-speed ratio of 8 (capping the chord at 4.5 m, as road transport demands), gives it a simple aerofoil with a lift slope of $2\pi$ per radian and a lift-to-drag ratio of about 100, and computes $C_P$ and $C_T$ across a range of tip-speed ratios.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Blade element momentum (BEM) theory for a three-bladed rotor, 120 m in diameter.
B, R, r_hub = 3, 60.0, 6.0               # blades, tip radius and root radius (m)
lam_design, cl_design, alpha_design = 8.0, 1.0, np.radians(6.0)

def polar(alpha):
    """A simple aerofoil: lift slope 2*pi, zero lift at -3.5 deg, stall above 12 deg."""
    a_stall = np.radians(12.0)
    cl_max = 2 * np.pi * (a_stall + np.radians(3.5))
    cl = np.where(alpha < a_stall, 2 * np.pi * (alpha + np.radians(3.5)),
                  cl_max - 1.5 * (alpha - a_stall))                  # lift falls after stall
    cd = 0.010 + 0.01 * alpha**2 + 0.6 * np.maximum(alpha - a_stall, 0.0)
    return cl, cd

# The blade: the classic optimum shape for the design tip-speed ratio, 40 elements.
dr = (R - r_hub) / 40
r = r_hub + dr * (np.arange(40) + 0.5)          # mid-radius of each element
lam_r = lam_design * r / R
phi_d = (2 / 3) * np.arctan(1 / lam_r)          # design inflow angle, with wake rotation
chord = np.minimum(8 * np.pi * r * (1 - np.cos(phi_d)) / (B * cl_design), 4.5)  # capped for transport
twist = phi_d - alpha_design

def bem(lam, pitch=0.0, V=1.0, rho=1.225):
    """Power and thrust coefficients (Cp, CT) at tip-speed ratio lam and blade pitch (rad)."""
    omega = lam * V / R
    sigma = B * chord / (2 * np.pi * r)                       # local solidity
    a, ap = np.full_like(r, 0.3), np.zeros_like(r)            # axial and tangential induction
    for _ in range(500):
        phi = np.arctan2((1 - a) * V, (1 + ap) * omega * r)   # inflow angle
        cl, cd = polar(phi - twist - pitch)                   # angle of attack = phi - twist - pitch
        cn = cl * np.cos(phi) + cd * np.sin(phi)              # force coefficients normal and
        ct = cl * np.sin(phi) - cd * np.cos(phi)              # tangential to the rotor plane
        F = 2 / np.pi * np.arccos(np.exp(-B * (R - r) / (2 * r * np.sin(phi))))   # Prandtl tip loss
        K = 4 * F * np.sin(phi) ** 2 / (sigma * cn)
        a_new = 1 / (K + 1)
        ac = 0.4                                              # above a = 0.4 momentum theory fails;
        heavy = 0.5 * (2 + K * (1 - 2 * ac) - np.sqrt(np.maximum((K * (1 - 2 * ac) + 2) ** 2 + 4 * (K * ac**2 - 1), 0.0)))
        a_new = np.where(a_new > ac, heavy, a_new)            # use a straight-line (Glauert-type) fit
        ap_new = 1 / (4 * F * np.sin(phi) * np.cos(phi) / (sigma * ct) - 1)
        done = np.max(np.abs(a_new - a)) + np.max(np.abs(ap_new - ap)) < 1e-9
        a, ap = 0.5 * (a + a_new), 0.5 * (ap + ap_new)        # relax for stability
        if done:
            break
    else:
        raise RuntimeError("BEM iteration did not converge")
    W2 = ((1 - a) * V) ** 2 + ((1 + ap) * omega * r) ** 2     # relative wind speed squared
    dT = B * 0.5 * rho * W2 * chord * cn * dr                 # thrust of each element
    dQ = B * 0.5 * rho * W2 * chord * ct * r * dr             # torque of each element
    q = 0.5 * rho * np.pi * R**2 * V**2
    return omega * dQ.sum() / (q * V), dT.sum() / q

print("r/R   chord (m)  twist (deg)")
for k in (0, 10, 20, 30, 39):
    print(f"{r[k] / R:4.2f} {chord[k]:9.2f} {np.degrees(twist[k]):10.1f}")
print("\ntip-speed ratio    Cp      CT")
for lam in range(3, 13):
    cp, ct = bem(lam)
    print(f"{lam:10d}      {cp:6.3f}  {ct:6.3f}")
```

The blade comes out as expected: twisted by 27° from root to tip, its chord falling from the 4.5 m cap to 1.75 m. The rotor's power coefficient peaks at 0.490 at a tip-speed ratio of 7, about 83% of the Betz limit, and falls away on either side: at low $\lambda$ the inner blade stalls, and at high $\lambda$ drag and over-induction take over. The best $\lambda$ is below the design value of 8 because the simple design formula ignores tip losses and drag, which the solver includes. Real large rotors achieve power coefficients of about 0.45 to 0.50.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Try `bem(7, pitch=np.radians(5))`: pitching the blades by 5° drops $C_P$ to about 0.40 and $C_T$ from 0.78 to 0.54. That is exactly how a turbine sheds power in strong winds: it turns its blades to reduce the angle of attack, spilling the energy it cannot use and lightening the load on the tower at the same time.

</Narrator>

BEM is fast, which is why it sits inside the design tools of every manufacturer and inside simulation codes such as [OpenFAST](https://github.com/OpenFAST/openfast) and [QBlade](https://qblade.org/), with corrections for yawed flow, dynamic stall and the time the wake takes to respond. When BEM's assumptions fail, for very large flexible blades, wakes interacting across a wind farm or a rotor in yaw, engineers use vortex methods or the <LessonLink slug="computational-fluid-dynamics">computational fluid dynamics</LessonLink> of a previous lesson.

</Section>

<Section id="power-curve" kind="computation" title="Power curves and capacity factors">

A modern turbine runs at **variable speed**. Below rated wind it adjusts its rotor speed to hold the best tip-speed ratio and so the best $C_P$ (region 2); if the tips reach their speed limit, set by noise and by erosion of the leading edges by rain, it holds that speed and accepts a lower $C_P$ (region 2.5); above rated wind it pitches its blades to hold rated power (region 3); and at the **cut-out** speed, typically about 25 m/s, it feathers its blades and stops. The program builds the power curve of a 4.0 MW turbine with the rotor computed above, then averages it over a **Weibull distribution** of wind speeds,
$$f(v) = \frac{k}{c}\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^{k-1}e^{-(v/c)^k},$$
whose shape parameter $k$ describes how steady the wind is (higher $k$, less spread) and whose scale $c$ sets the mean speed, $\bar v = c\,\Gamma(1 + 1/k)$. Lumenary EE uses the Rayleigh distribution, the special case $k = 2$.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

# Power curve of a variable-speed, pitch-controlled turbine with the rotor computed above:
# 120 m diameter, rated 4.0 MW (electrical), tip speed limited to 80 m/s.
lam_table = np.arange(3, 13)
cp_table = np.array([0.135, 0.261, 0.398, 0.467, 0.490, 0.484, 0.451, 0.399, 0.335, 0.257])
R, P_rated, eta_elec, v_tip_max = 60.0, 4.0e6, 0.94, 80.0
v_in, v_out = 3.0, 25.0
A = np.pi * R**2
lam_best = lam_table[np.argmax(cp_table)]

def power(v, rho=1.225):
    """Electrical power (W) at hub-height wind speed v (m/s)."""
    if v < v_in or v >= v_out:
        return 0.0
    omega = min(lam_best * v / R, v_tip_max / R)       # track the best tip-speed ratio, up to the speed limit
    cp = np.interp(omega * R / v, lam_table, cp_table)
    return min(eta_elec * 0.5 * rho * A * cp * v**3, P_rated)   # above rated, pitch the blades

print("wind (m/s)  power (kW)")
for v in (3, 5, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15, 25):
    print(f"{v:8}   {power(v) / 1e3:8.0f}")

# Annual energy from a Weibull distribution of hub-height wind speeds.
v = np.linspace(0, 30, 3001)
dv = v[1] - v[0]
P = np.array([power(x) for x in v])

def capacity_factor(v_mean, k):
    c = v_mean / math.gamma(1 + 1 / k)                 # Weibull scale from the mean speed
    pdf = (k / c) * (v / c) ** (k - 1) * np.exp(-(v / c) ** k)
    return np.sum(P * pdf) * dv / P_rated

print("\nmean wind   capacity factor (gross) for Weibull shape k")
print("  (m/s)       k = 1.6    k = 2.0    k = 2.6")
for vm in (6.0, 7.5, 9.0):
    print(f"{vm:6.1f}   " + "".join(f"{capacity_factor(vm, k):10.1%} " for k in (1.6, 2.0, 2.6)))
cf = capacity_factor(7.5, 2.0)
net = cf * 0.97 * 0.90 * 0.98                         # availability, wake losses, electrical losses
print(f"\nat 7.5 m/s, k = 2: gross {cf:.1%}, net {net:.1%}, {net * P_rated * 8760 / 1e9:.1f} GWh a year")

# Two site effects: wind shear (the power law) and thin air at altitude.
for alpha in (0.14, 0.20):
    print(f"mean 5.5 m/s at 10 m becomes {5.5 * (100 / 10) ** alpha:.2f} m/s at 100 m if alpha = {alpha}")
print(f"at 2000 m altitude (air 1.01 kg/m^3), output at 9 m/s falls from "
      f"{power(9.0) / 1e3:.0f} to {power(9.0, rho=1.01) / 1e3:.0f} kW")
```

The table makes several engineering points. The turbine reaches rated power at about 10.8 m/s, before its tips reach 80 m/s, so for this machine region 2.5 never occurs. Its gross capacity factor rises from about 27% at a mean of 6 m/s to 53% at 9 m/s (for $k = 2$). The shape of the distribution matters too: at a low-wind site a gustier wind ($k = 1.6$) is slightly *better*, because more of the energy arrives in strong winds that the turbine can use, while at a windy site a steadier wind ($k = 2.6$) is better, because less time is wasted above rated power. After realistic losses for availability, the wakes of neighbouring turbines and electrical losses, the 7.5 m/s site's 41.3% becomes 35.4%, or 12.4 GWh a year.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The weakest link in an energy estimate is usually the wind data, not the turbine. Extrapolating a mean of 5.5 m/s measured at 10 m to the 100 m hub gives 7.59 or 8.72 m/s depending on whether the shear exponent is 0.14 or 0.20, a difference worth about a fifth of the annual energy. That is why developers measure for a year or more at hub height with masts or ground-based lidar, correlate with long-term records, and report energy yields as probabilities (a "P90" yield is one exceeded in nine years out of ten) rather than single numbers. Air density matters too: at 2,000 m the same turbine makes 1,918 kW at 9 m/s instead of 2,326.

</Narrator>

Real power curves are measured under IEC 61400-12-1, with a mast or lidar upwind of the turbine. Manufacturers have been adding rotor area faster than generator rating, lowering the **specific power** (rated watts per square metre of rotor) so that turbines reach rated power in lighter winds and run at higher capacity factors; our turbine has 354 W/m². In 2026 onshore turbines of 5 to 7 MW with rotors of 150 to 175 m are common, and offshore turbines of 15 MW with rotors of about 240 m are being installed, with prototypes of 20 MW and more tested in China. Blades are built from glass- and carbon-fibre composites (Lumenary Materials' [composites](/en/materials/composites/)), and recycling them at the end of their 25 to 30-year life is an active engineering problem.

<Section id="hydro" kind="examples" title="Hydro turbines: Pelton, Francis and Kaplan">

Hydropower converts the potential energy of water falling through a **head** $H$:
$$P = \eta\,\rho g Q H,$$
for a flow $Q$ (m³/s) and an overall efficiency $\eta$, which for large modern turbine-generator sets exceeds 90%. All hydro turbines rest on **Euler's turbine equation**: the torque on a runner equals the rate at which it reduces the angular momentum of the water, $\tau = \dot m\,(r_1 v_{\theta 1} - r_2 v_{\theta 2})$, where $v_\theta$ is the swirl velocity at the inlet (1) and outlet (2). They differ in how they apply it.

- A **Pelton wheel** is an **impulse turbine** for high heads, from a few hundred to almost 1,900 m. Nozzles turn the whole head into high-speed jets, which strike split buckets on the rim of a wheel turning in air at atmospheric pressure. The buckets reverse the jet, and the wheel works best when they move at a little under half the jet speed. Its relatives, the Turgo and the crossflow (Banki–Michell) turbines, serve small hydro schemes.
- A **Francis turbine** is a **reaction turbine** for medium heads, from about 20 to 700 m. Water fills a spiral casing, passes adjustable **guide vanes** that give it swirl, and flows inwards through a closed runner, turning from radial to axial and leaving through a **draft tube** that recovers kinetic energy. The world's largest hydro turbines, of 700 MW at Itaipu (Brazil and Paraguay) and Three Gorges (China) and 1,000 MW at Baihetan, are Francis turbines.
- A **Kaplan turbine** is an axial-flow reaction turbine, a propeller in a pipe, for low heads and large flows. Both its guide vanes and its runner blades are adjustable, which keeps its efficiency high as the flow varies. **Bulb** turbines, with the generator in a capsule in the flow, serve the lowest heads and tidal barrages.

The **specific speed** tells which kind suits a site. It combines the rotational speed $n$ (rpm), flow and head in the group $n_q = n\sqrt{Q}/H^{3/4}$, which is the same for geometrically similar turbines whatever their size: low values mean high head and little flow (Pelton), high values low head and much flow (Kaplan). The rotational speed itself is not free, because a generator connected directly to a 50 Hz grid must turn at $3000/p$ rpm, where $p$ is its number of pole pairs.

```python run
import math

rho, g = 1000.0, 9.81

def specific_speed(n_rpm, Q, H):
    """n_q = n sqrt(Q) / H^(3/4), with n in rpm, Q in m^3/s and H in m."""
    return n_rpm * math.sqrt(Q) / H**0.75

def turbine_type(nq):
    """A rough guide; near the boundaries, cost, cavitation and part-load behaviour decide."""
    if nq < 25:
        return "Pelton (impulse)"
    if nq < 110:
        return "Francis"
    return "Kaplan or bulb"

# Illustrative sites: head H (m), flow Q (m^3/s), a synchronous speed for a 50 Hz grid (rpm),
# and the overall efficiency of turbine and generator.
sites = [
    ("Alpine high-head plant", 1000.0, 8.0, 500.0, 0.90),
    ("mountain storage plant", 300.0, 50.0, 375.0, 0.92),
    ("large river dam", 110.0, 650.0, 90.9, 0.93),
    ("lowland river barrage", 12.0, 400.0, 75.0, 0.90),
    ("village micro-hydro", 60.0, 0.12, 1000.0, 0.70),
]
print(f"{'site':24}{'power':>11}{'n_q':>7}   type")
for name, H, Q, n, eta in sites:
    P = rho * g * Q * H * eta
    power = f"{P / 1e6:.0f} MW" if P >= 1e6 else f"{P / 1e3:.0f} kW"
    nq = specific_speed(n, Q, H)
    print(f"{name:24}{power:>11}{nq:7.0f}   {turbine_type(nq)}")

# A Pelton wheel: how efficiency depends on bucket speed u relative to jet speed V.
H = 1000.0
V = 0.98 * math.sqrt(2 * g * H)                 # jet speed after a nozzle losing 4% of the head
print(f"\nPelton jet at {H:.0f} m of head: {V:.1f} m/s")
k, beta = 0.90, math.radians(165)               # friction in the bucket, deflection angle
for x in (0.3, 0.4, 0.47, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7):
    eta = 2 * x * (1 - x) * (1 - k * math.cos(beta))
    print(f"  u/V = {x:4.2f}: bucket efficiency {eta:.3f}")
D = 60 * 0.47 * V / (math.pi * 500)
print(f"at 500 rpm, u/V = 0.47 needs a runner {D:.2f} m across (pitch diameter)")

# Tidal stream, wind and waves: power per square metre of flow, and per metre of wave crest.
print("\ntidal stream at 2.5 m/s: %.1f kW/m^2" % (0.5 * 1025 * 2.5**3 / 1e3))
print("wind at 12 m/s:          %.1f kW/m^2" % (0.5 * 1.225 * 12**3 / 1e3))
for Hs, Te in ((2.0, 8.0), (3.0, 10.0)):
    print(f"waves Hs = {Hs} m, Te = {Te} s: {1025 * g**2 * Hs**2 * Te / (64 * math.pi) / 1e3:.0f} kW per metre of crest")
```

In this simple model the buckets do best at exactly half the jet speed, with 93.5% of the jet's energy; real wheels run at about 0.46 to 0.48 of jet speed because of windage and the changing angle as each bucket sweeps through the jet.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Every reaction turbine is limited by **cavitation**: where the local pressure on a runner blade falls below the water's vapour pressure, vapour bubbles form and collapse violently against the metal, pitting it and costing efficiency. The margin is set by how far below the tailwater the runner is placed, judged with the Thoma cavitation number, and fast, high-specific-speed runners need deeper settings. That is why large Kaplan and Francis units sit well below the river level, and why plants are tested on scale models at the IEC's model-test conditions before the full-size runner is cast.

</Narrator>

<Section id="ocean" kind="human" title="Tidal and wave energy in outline">

The oceans offer two more resources. **Tides** are as predictable as the Moon's orbit. A **tidal range** scheme traps water behind a barrage at high tide and releases it through low-head bulb turbines: France's La Rance barrage (240 MW) has run since 1966, and South Korea's Sihwa Lake plant (254 MW) since 2011. They are reliable but rare, because suitable estuaries are few and barrages change the ecology of the estuaries behind them. **Tidal stream** turbines work like underwater wind turbines in fast currents. Seawater is about 840 times denser than air, so a 2.5 m/s current carries 8.0 kW per square metre of flow, more than seven times as much as a 12 m/s wind (1.1 kW/m²); rotors can be small, but they must survive salt water, enormous thrust and the difficulty of maintenance underwater. Arrays of a few megawatts operate in Scotland's Pentland Firth (MeyGen), in Orkney and Shetland, and in China's Zhoushan archipelago, with larger projects planned in France and elsewhere.

**Waves** carry wind energy gathered over thousands of kilometres of ocean. In deep water the power per metre of wave crest is about $\rho g^2 H_s^2 T_e/(64\pi)$, where $H_s$ is the significant wave height and $T_e$ the energy period: 16 kW/m for 2 m waves of 8 s, and 44 kW/m for 3 m waves of 10 s, more on exposed Atlantic and Southern Ocean coasts. Devices include oscillating water columns, where waves pump air through a turbine (Spain's breakwater plant at Mutriku has run since 2011), floating point absorbers and hinged attenuators. As of 2026 wave energy remains at the demonstration stage: the engineering problem is a machine that captures energy efficiently from ordinary waves yet survives the largest storms, which carry a hundred times more power, at an affordable cost. Test centres such as [EMEC](https://www.emec.org.uk/) in Orkney exist to let developers learn this at sea.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A turbine with more blades captures more energy."** More blades add drag, weight and cost, and slow the optimum rotor speed, which increases wake-rotation losses. Three blades are a compromise that gives nearly the maximum power coefficient with smooth dynamics; two-bladed rotors are cheaper but noisier and harder on their hubs, and many-bladed windpumps are designed for high starting torque, not efficiency.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A turbine rated at 4 MW produces 4 MW."** It produces 4 MW only above its rated wind speed, about 10.8 m/s for the turbine in this lesson; at 7 m/s it produced 1,094 kW. Over a year its output averages a third to a half of its rating at good sites, which is what the capacity factor measures.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The Betz limit applies to hydro turbines too."** Betz bounds a rotor in an open stream, which the flow can go around. A hydro turbine in a closed penstock takes the water's pressure energy directly, and every kilogram of water must pass through it, so efficiencies above 90% are possible. Tidal stream turbines in open currents, by contrast, are bounded by Betz.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Doubling the wind speed doubles the power."** Below rated speed, power grows as the cube of the wind speed, so doubling the speed gives about eight times the power. This is why a site's mean wind speed, and the shear profile up to hub height, matter so much.

</Misconception>
