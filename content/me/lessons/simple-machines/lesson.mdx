<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Moving the immovable">

How did people without engines raise the stones of the pyramids of Giza, lift water from the Nile, or haul a ship's anchor up from the seabed? How can a ten-year-old balance her father on a seesaw, or a mechanic lift a whole car with one hand?

The answer is **simple machines**: levers, pulleys, wheels and axles, ramps, wedges and screws. Each one lets a small force do the job of a big one. Every complicated machine, from a sewing machine to a crane, is made of these simple parts working together.

But there is a catch, and it is one of the most important ideas in all of engineering. A machine can make a job *easier*, but it can never make it *smaller*. In this lesson you will see exactly what you pay for that help.

</Section>

<Section id="levers" kind="intuition" title="Levers">

A **lever** is a stiff bar that turns about a fixed point called the **fulcrum** (or pivot). You push or pull on one part of the bar with an **effort**, and the bar moves a **load** somewhere else.

Try it with a ruler laid across a pencil, with a coin or two on each side. Move the coins around and you will find a rule: a small weight far from the pivot can balance a big weight close to it.

<Theorem name="The law of the lever">

A lever balances when

$$
\text{effort} \times \text{effort's distance from the fulcrum} = \text{load} \times \text{load's distance from the fulcrum}.
$$

Each side of this equation measures the **turning effect** of a force, which engineers call its **moment**.

</Theorem>

The Greek mathematician Archimedes of Syracuse wrote this law down in the third century BCE. He is said to have boasted that, given a place to stand and a long enough lever, he could move the whole Earth.

<Example title="Balancing a seesaw">

Daniel has a mass of 30 kg and sits 2 m from the middle of a seesaw. His father Joseph has a mass of 75 kg. How far from the middle must Joseph sit to balance him?

Turning effect on Daniel's side: 30 × 2 = 60. Joseph's side must match it: 75 × distance = 60, so distance = 60 ÷ 75 = **0.8 m**. (We used masses instead of weights; that is fine here, because both weights are their mass times the same $g$, which cancels out.)

</Example>

- **Class 1: fulcrum in the middle.** A seesaw, a crowbar levering up a paving stone, a claw hammer pulling a nail, and a pair of scissors (two class 1 levers joined at the pivot). Effort and load move in opposite directions.
- **Class 2: load in the middle.** A wheelbarrow (the wheel's axle is the fulcrum), a nutcracker, a bottle opener. The effort is always further from the fulcrum than the load, so these always make the force smaller.
- **Class 3: effort in the middle.** Tweezers, a fishing rod, a broom, and your own forearm: your elbow is the fulcrum and your biceps muscle pulls on the bone just below it. Here the effort is *bigger* than the load! What you gain is speed and reach: the far end moves much further and faster than the point you push.

<Example title="A wheelbarrow and a forearm">

A wheelbarrow carries 600 N of soil, 0.4 m from the wheel's axle. You lift the handles 1.2 m from the axle. The law of the lever gives effort × 1.2 = 600 × 0.4, so the effort is 240 ÷ 1.2 = **200 N**. The wheelbarrow divides the force by 3.

Now your forearm, holding a 20 N bag of shopping about 32 cm from the elbow. The biceps attaches only about 4 cm from the elbow, so muscle force × 4 = 20 × 32, and the muscle must pull with **160 N**, eight times the load. In return, your hand moves eight times as far, and eight times as fast, as the end of the muscle. That is how you can flick a ball or play the piano with quick, wide movements.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="advantage" kind="definitions" title="Mechanical advantage, and why work is never free">

<Definition term="mechanical advantage">

The **mechanical advantage** (MA) of a machine is how many times it multiplies your force:

$$
\text{MA} = \frac{\text{load}}{\text{effort}}.
$$

The wheelbarrow above has MA = 600 ÷ 200 = 3. The forearm has MA = 20 ÷ 160 = 1/8.

</Definition>

Now watch the distances. Lift the wheelbarrow's handles by 30 cm and the soil, three times closer to the axle, rises by only 10 cm. The force went down by 3, and the distance went up by 3. That is not a coincidence. Engineers multiply a force by the distance it moves through and call the result **work**, measured in **joules** (J).

<Definition term="work">

When a force moves something, the **work** done is

$$
\text{work} = \text{force} \times \text{distance moved in the direction of the force}.
$$

One joule is the work done when a force of 1 N moves through 1 m.

</Definition>

For the wheelbarrow: your work is 200 N × 0.3 m = 60 J, and the work done lifting the soil is 600 N × 0.1 m = 60 J. Exactly the same.

<Theorem name="No work for free">

The work you put into a machine is never less than the work it does on the load. In a perfect machine with no friction they are equal; in every real machine, some of your work is lost (mostly as heat in rubbing parts), so you always put in a little more than you get out.

</Theorem>

So a machine can turn a big force over a short distance into a small force over a long distance, or the other way round, but it cannot shrink the work. For a perfect machine, the mechanical advantage is the distance your effort moves divided by the distance the load moves.

<Narrator who="chloe">

This is the key idea of the whole lesson: force times distance stays the same, so whatever a machine saves in force it must cost in distance. Engineers trusted it so completely that they used it to *find* the mechanical advantage of complicated machines without ever measuring a force: just move the effort, and measure how far the load goes.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="other-machines" kind="examples" title="Pulleys, wheels, ramps, wedges and screws">

### Pulleys

A **pulley** is a wheel with a groove for a rope. A single pulley fixed to a beam does not reduce the force at all (MA = 1), but it changes the direction: you can pull *down* to lift a flag or a bucket *up*, using your own weight to help. A pulley that hangs from the rope and moves with the load is different: the load hangs from two lengths of rope, each carrying half of it, so MA = 2.

Combine fixed and movable pulleys into a **block and tackle** and the rule is simple: the mechanical advantage equals the number of rope segments holding up the moving block. With four segments, a 400 N sack needs an effort of only 100 N, but you must pull 4 m of rope through your hands to lift it 1 m. Cranes on building sites, sailing ships and lifts all use pulleys, though anything that lifts people or heavy loads at work must be designed, tested and inspected to strict rules, because a failure could kill.

### Wheel and axle

A **wheel and axle** is a big wheel (or handle) fixed to a thin rod, so they turn together: a door knob, a screwdriver, a steering wheel, the crank of a well. It is a lever that goes round and round: MA = radius of the wheel ÷ radius of the axle. A well's crank handle 30 cm from the centre turning an axle of radius 6 cm has MA = 5, so a 150 N bucket of water needs only 30 N on the handle.

### Inclined plane

A ramp, or **inclined plane**, lets you raise something by pushing it a long way along a gentle slope instead of lifting it straight up. Pushing a wheelchair up a ramp 6 m long that rises 0.5 m, a perfect ramp with no friction would need a push of only 900 N × 0.5 ÷ 6 = **75 N** to raise a 900 N person and chair, instead of lifting 900 N straight up. In many countries building rules ask for wheelchair ramps no steeper than about 1 in 12, like this one, for exactly this reason. Mountain roads zig-zag for the same reason: a longer road with a gentler slope.

### Wedge and screw

A **wedge** is two ramps back to back that move into the material: an axe head, a chisel, a doorstop, even your front teeth. A small push on the back of the wedge becomes large sideways forces that split or hold things apart.

A **screw** is a ramp wrapped round a cylinder. Turn it once and it moves forwards by just the distance between neighbouring threads, the **pitch**. A car jack with a screw of pitch 1.5 mm, turned by a handle 20 cm long, moves your hand round a circle about 1257 mm long for every 1.5 mm the screw moves: a perfect machine would have an MA of more than 800! Screws hold furniture together, lift cars, clamp wood, and, in Archimedes' screw, lift water: a big screw turning inside a tube carries water uphill, a design still used in water treatment works and fish-friendly hydropower today.

<Narrator who="gideon">

A real screw jack is far from perfect: friction in the threads wastes most of the effort. But that loss is exactly what keeps it safe. A screw with enough friction is **self-locking**: let go of the handle and the car stays up instead of turning the screw backwards on its way down. Even so, a jack is only for lifting: anyone working under a car supports it on proper axle stands first.

</Narrator>

<Section id="gears" kind="computation" title="Gears, and the gears of a bicycle">

A **gear** is a wheel with teeth. When two gears mesh, or are joined by a chain, the teeth pass one by one, so in one turn a gear with 40 teeth pushes 40 teeth' worth of the other gear (or chain) along. If the other gear has 20 teeth, that is two whole turns.

<Definition term="gear ratio">

When a gear with $A$ teeth drives a gear with $B$ teeth, the driven gear turns $A \div B$ times for every turn of the driver. A small gear driving a big one makes the output slower but turn with more force; a big gear driving a small one makes it faster but with less force.

</Definition>

A bicycle with gears has one or more **chainrings** at the pedals and a stack of **cogs** of different sizes on the back wheel. Changing gear moves the chain to a different cog. Each turn of the pedals turns the back wheel (chainring teeth ÷ cog teeth) times, and each turn of the back wheel rolls the bike forward by the wheel's circumference.

Here is a bicycle with a 40-tooth chainring, eight cogs and wheels that roll 2.1 m per turn. The program works out, for each gear, how far one turn of the pedals takes you, and how fast you go if you pedal at a comfortable 70 turns a minute:

```python run
# The gears of a bicycle with one chainring at the front and eight cogs at the back.
chainring = 40                               # teeth on the front chainring (turned by the pedals)
cogs = [32, 28, 24, 21, 18, 16, 14, 12]      # teeth on each rear cog, biggest first
wheel_circumference = 2.1                    # metres the bike rolls for one turn of the rear wheel
cadence = 70                                 # pedal turns per minute (a comfortable pace)

print("gear  cog  ratio  metres per   speed at 70 turns")
print("      teeth       pedal turn   per minute (km/h)")
for gear, cog in enumerate(cogs, start=1):
    ratio = chainring / cog                  # rear-wheel turns for each turn of the pedals
    metres = ratio * wheel_circumference     # distance rolled for each turn of the pedals
    speed = metres * cadence * 60 / 1000     # metres per minute -> kilometres per hour
    print(f"{gear:4} {cog:4} {ratio:6.2f} {metres:10.2f} {speed:12.1f}   " + "=" * round(speed))
```

In first gear (the 32-tooth cog) each turn of the pedals rolls the bike only about 2.6 m, and pedalling at 70 turns a minute gives 11 km/h. In eighth gear (the 12-tooth cog) each turn rolls it 7 m, and the same pedalling gives 29.4 km/h.

Why not stay in eighth gear all the time? Because of the rule of the previous section. In eighth gear the bike goes 7 ÷ 2.625 ≈ 2.7 times as far per pedal turn as in first, so to climb the same hill your feet would have to push about 2.7 times as hard. On a steep hill you choose first gear: a small push on the pedals, many turns, slow progress. On the flat, with the wind behind you, you choose a high gear: a firmer push, fewer turns, more speed. Gears do not give you extra energy; they let you match the work your legs can comfortably do to what the road needs.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Try changing the program. A child's single-speed bike might have a 32-tooth chainring, a 16-tooth cog and wheels that roll 1.5 m per turn: set `chainring = 32`, `cogs = [16]` and `wheel_circumference = 1.5`, and you get 3 m per pedal turn. A mountain bike might have a 32-tooth chainring with cogs from 51 down to 10 teeth; check how gentle its lowest gear is.

</Narrator>

Engineers often write a gear ratio the other way round, as the number of input turns per output turn: the gearbox between an electric motor and a robot's wheel might be a "20 : 1 reduction", turning a fast, weak spin into a slow, strong one. Always check which way round a ratio is written.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Simple machines through history">

Simple machines are far older than writing about them. The **shadoof**, a long pole on a pivot with a bucket at one end and a counterweight at the other, was lifting water from rivers in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia four thousand years ago, and is still used in some places today. Historians still debate exactly how the pyramids were built, but ramps and levers were surely part of the answer.

Archimedes worked out the law of the lever in the third century BCE. Around the first century CE, Heron of Alexandria described five "simple powers": the lever, the wheel and axle, the pulley, the wedge and the screw. Thinkers of the European Renaissance added the inclined plane to make the six classical simple machines; around 1586 the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin found the rule for a ramp with a famous thought experiment about a loop of balls draped over a triangle.

Gears appear in many cultures. Chinese engineers built geared machines such as the south-pointing chariot, whose gears kept a pointing figure facing the same direction however the cart turned. In 1206 Ismail al-Jazari, working in what is now south-eastern Turkey, described water-raising machines, clocks and automata full of gears, cams and cranks in his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A machine reduces the work you have to do."** It reduces the *force*, not the work. Work is force times distance, and a perfect machine only trades one for the other. A real machine always needs a little *more* work than the job itself, because of friction.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bigger mechanical advantage is always better."** It depends on what you want. A big MA means a small force but a long, slow movement. Class 3 levers such as tweezers, brooms and your forearm deliberately have an MA less than 1, to give speed, reach or delicate control.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"High gears make a bicycle faster."** Gears don't add any power: that comes only from your legs. A high gear lets you turn your legs at a comfortable speed when the bike is already going fast; on a hill, it just makes pedalling too hard.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A fixed pulley makes lifting easier, because it has a wheel."** A single fixed pulley has MA = 1: you pull just as hard as the load. What it gives you is a more convenient direction, pulling down instead of lifting up.

</Misconception>

</Section>
