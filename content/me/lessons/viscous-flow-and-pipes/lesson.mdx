<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="The push a pipe needs">

In <LessonLink slug="fluid-statics-and-bernoulli">Fluid statics and Bernoulli</LessonLink> an ideal fluid traded pressure, speed and height along a streamline and lost nothing. Apply that to a level pipe of constant bore. Continuity says the mean speed is the same at both ends; the height is the same; so Bernoulli says the pressure is the same too, and water should glide along a kilometre of pipe for ever without being pushed.

Every water utility knows otherwise. The mains under a street in Nairobi need pumping stations; a district-heating loop in Copenhagen has circulating pumps working day and night; the oil in a car engine needs an oil pump to reach the bearings; your heart does real work to push blood through your arteries. Pumps and fans are among the largest users of electricity in industry and buildings, and much of that electricity goes to overcoming one thing: **viscosity**, the internal friction of a real fluid.

This lesson puts viscosity back: why fluid sticks to walls, why one dimensionless number decides between smooth and chaotic flow, how to compute the pressure a pipe and its fittings need, how a pump and a pipe system agree on a flow rate, and, in outline, the boundary layers and drag on everything that moves through air or water. <LessonLink slug="heat-transfer">Heat transfer</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="computational-fluid-dynamics">computational fluid dynamics</LessonLink> build on all of it.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Layers that drag on each other">

Stir a jar of honey and a glass of water with the same spoon. The honey resists: it takes force to make one layer slide over the next. That resistance to *shearing* is viscosity. It is not the same as density: olive oil floats on water, yet it is about eighty times more viscous.

The simplest case is a thin film between two large plates, the lower one fixed and the upper one dragged along at speed $U$. The fluid touching each plate moves with that plate, the speed in between varies linearly from zero to $U$, and the force needed to keep the top plate moving is proportional to $U$ and to the plate's area, and inversely proportional to the gap.

This **no-slip condition** is the heart of viscous flow. Fluid at a wall is at rest relative to it and fluid far away is moving, so there is shearing in between, and shearing a viscous fluid takes work. That work becomes internal energy, warming the fluid very slightly; engineers call it a **loss**, and the pump must make it good.

In 1883 Osborne Reynolds, in Manchester, ran water from a tank through a glass tube with a thin streak of dye at the entrance. At low speeds the streak stayed a clean thread; above a certain speed it wavered and burst into eddies that mixed the dye through the tube. He had made visible the two regimes of pipe flow: **laminar**, moving in smooth layers, and **turbulent**, with unsteady eddies of many sizes tumbling across the flow.

<Analogy>

Laminar flow is like disciplined motorway traffic: every car keeps its lane, and the lanes slide past one another. Turbulent flow is like a busy market crowd, where people weave across the aisle carrying their momentum into slower regions, so speeds even out, but those next to the stalls are jostled far harder. Turbulence in a pipe likewise flattens the velocity profile and multiplies the friction at the wall.

</Analogy>

<Narrator who="chloe">

So what is a pump for? Not to keep water moving against nothing, but to replace the mechanical energy that shearing turns into internal energy. <LessonLink slug="first-law-of-thermodynamics">The first law</LessonLink> says that energy is not destroyed (the water leaving a long pipeline is slightly warmer, by about 0.02 K for every 10 m of head lost); what is lost is its usefulness, an irreversibility in the sense of <LessonLink slug="second-law-and-entropy">the second law</LessonLink>. Every choice in this lesson, from the pipe size to the shape of a bend to the pump itself, is about losing less of it.

</Narrator>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Viscosity and the Reynolds number">

<Definition term="dynamic viscosity (Newton's law of viscosity)">

For a **Newtonian fluid** the shear stress on layers sliding over one another is proportional to the rate of shear, the velocity gradient across the layers:

$$\tau = \mu\,\frac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dy}.$$

The constant $\mu$ is the **dynamic viscosity**, measured in pascal seconds (Pa·s). Water, air, oils and most gases and simple liquids are Newtonian.

</Definition>

<Definition term="kinematic viscosity">

The ratio $\nu = \mu/\rho$ of viscosity to density, in m²/s, is the **kinematic viscosity**. It measures how fast momentum diffuses through the fluid, and it is the combination that appears in most flow formulas.

</Definition>

Representative values near 20 °C and atmospheric pressure, rounded (take design values from property databases such as the [NIST Chemistry WebBook](https://webbook.nist.gov/chemistry/fluid/)):

| Fluid | $\rho$ (kg/m³) | $\mu$ (Pa·s) | $\nu$ (m²/s) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Air | 1.2 | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | $1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| Water | 998 | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| Olive oil | 910 | about 0.08 | about $9 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| Glycerol | 1260 | about 1.4 | about $1.1 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| Honey | about 1400 | 2 to 10 | $10^{-3}$ or more |

Viscosity depends strongly on temperature: water's halves between 20 °C and 50 °C, and engine oil can be a hundred times thicker on a frozen morning than in a hot engine. Gases go the other way: their viscosity *rises* with temperature, because it comes from molecules carrying momentum between layers, and hotter molecules travel faster.

<Note>

Many everyday fluids are **non-Newtonian**: their apparent viscosity depends on how fast they are sheared. Paint, ketchup and blood thin as they are sheared harder (shear-thinning); a thick suspension of cornflour in water stiffens suddenly when struck (shear-thickening); toothpaste does not flow at all until the stress passes a threshold (a yield-stress fluid). Their behaviour is the subject of *rheology*. Everything below assumes a Newtonian fluid.

</Note>

<Definition term="Reynolds number">

For a flow of speed $V$ past or through an object of size $D$,

$$\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho V D}{\mu} = \frac{V D}{\nu}.$$

It compares the momentum the fluid carries (inertia) with the viscous stresses that smooth it out. For flow in a round pipe, with $V$ the mean speed and $D$ the internal diameter, the flow is normally **laminar** below $\mathrm{Re} \approx 2300$ and **turbulent** above about 4000, with an unsteady, intermittent **transitional** range in between.

</Definition>

The Reynolds number spans an astonishing range: about $10^{-5}$ for a swimming bacterium, which stops dead the instant it stops pushing; $10^4$ for water in a garden hose; $10^6$ for air past a car; $10^9$ for a large ship. Two flows with the same geometry and Reynolds number behave alike whatever their size, which is why models can be tested in towing tanks and wind tunnels.

The number 2300 is a convention, not a law. Laminar pipe flow becomes turbulent only when disturbances are large enough: with very smooth inlets and quiet tanks it has been kept laminar beyond $\mathrm{Re} = 10^5$, while below roughly 2000 every turbulent patch eventually decays. In a real plant, with pumps, bends and valves upstream, expect transition between about 2000 and 4000, and in that range design for whichever regime gives the larger pressure drop.

</Section>

<Section id="laminar" kind="definitions" title="Laminar pipe flow, exactly">

Laminar flow in a long straight pipe is one of the few viscous flows that can be solved exactly with a pencil, and the answer is worth knowing by heart.

<Theorem name="Hagen–Poiseuille flow">

In steady, fully developed laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid through a round pipe of radius $R = D/2$, with a pressure drop $\Delta p$ over a length $L$, the velocity at radius $r$ is the paraboloid

$$u(r) = \frac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right),$$

the volume flow rate is

$$Q = \frac{\pi R^4\,\Delta p}{8\mu L} = \frac{\pi D^4\,\Delta p}{128\,\mu L},$$

and the mean velocity $V = Q/(\pi R^2)$ is exactly half the centreline velocity.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take as a free body a cylinder of fluid of radius $r$ and length $L$, coaxial with the pipe. Because the flow is fully developed, the velocity profile is the same at both ends, so momentum flows in and out at the same rate and the net force on the cylinder is zero. The pressure difference pushes on its ends with force $\Delta p\,\pi r^2$; the slower fluid outside drags back on its curved surface with shear stress $\tau$ over area $2\pi r L$. Equilibrium gives $\tau = \Delta p\, r/(2L)$: the shear stress rises linearly from zero on the axis to its maximum at the wall. The velocity falls as $r$ increases, so Newton's law of viscosity reads $\tau = -\mu\,\mathrm du/\mathrm dr$, and

$$\frac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dr} = -\frac{\Delta p}{2\mu L}\,r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u = \frac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right),$$

where the constant of integration was fixed by no slip, $u(R) = 0$. The flow rate is the integral of $u$ over the cross-section, $Q = \int_0^R u(r)\,2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \pi R^4 \Delta p/(8\mu L)$. Dividing by the area $\pi R^2$ gives $V = R^2\Delta p/(8\mu L)$, which is half of $u(0) = R^2 \Delta p /(4\mu L)$.

</Proof>

The fourth power of the radius is the striking part. Halve the diameter of a laminar pipe, at the same pressure difference, and the flow falls by a factor of 16. That is why a small narrowing of a blood vessel matters so much to the heart, and why lubricant films and capillaries are so sensitive to their gap.

Rearranged in terms of the mean velocity, Hagen–Poiseuille reads

$$\Delta p = \frac{64}{\mathrm{Re}}\cdot\frac{L}{D}\cdot\frac{\rho V^2}{2},$$

which is the Darcy–Weisbach equation below with friction factor $f = 64/\mathrm{Re}$. In laminar flow the pressure drop is proportional to the flow rate; that is about to change.

</Section>

<Section id="darcy" kind="definitions" title="Head loss: Darcy–Weisbach, Colebrook and fittings">

Between two points 1 and 2 of a pipe system, the energy equation for steady incompressible flow is Bernoulli's equation with two new terms, written as heights of fluid ("heads", in metres):

$$\frac{p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 + h_\text{pump} = \frac{p_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 + h_L.$$

$h_\text{pump}$ is the head a pump adds; $h_L$ is the **head loss**, the mechanical energy per unit weight of fluid turned into internal energy on the way. (It is a per-unit-weight form of the steady-flow energy equation of <LessonLink slug="first-law-of-thermodynamics">the first law</LessonLink>.) A head of 1 m of water is a pressure of $\rho g \times 1\ \text{m} \approx 9.8$ kPa.

<Definition term="Darcy–Weisbach equation">

The head loss to friction in a straight pipe of length $L$ and internal diameter $D$, with mean velocity $V$, is

$$h_f = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{V^2}{2g}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \Delta p = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{\rho V^2}{2},$$

where the dimensionless **Darcy friction factor** $f$ depends on the Reynolds number and the **relative roughness** $\varepsilon/D$ of the pipe wall.

</Definition>

In laminar flow, $f = 64/\mathrm{Re}$ and roughness does not matter. In turbulent flow there is no exact theory, but there is an excellent fit to experiment, published by Cyril Colebrook in 1939 from his own and Johann Nikuradse's measurements:

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt f} = -2\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt f}\right).$$

In a smooth pipe ($\varepsilon = 0$) only $\mathrm{Re}$ matters, and $f$ falls slowly as it grows: the viscous layer at the wall buries any small roughness. At very high $\mathrm{Re}$ the second term vanishes and the pipe is **fully rough**: the bumps poke through the thin viscous layer and $f$ depends only on $\varepsilon/D$. The equation is *implicit*, with $f$ on both sides and no algebraic solution. Engineers of the 1940s read $f$ from Lewis Moody's chart; today we solve it numerically in microseconds, or use explicit approximations such as Haaland's (1983), within a couple of per cent:

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt f} \approx -1.8\log_{10}\!\left[\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7}\right)^{1.11} + \frac{6.9}{\mathrm{Re}}\right].$$

Typical roughness heights, for orientation (pipe makers publish values for their products, and roughness grows as pipes age and corrode):

| Pipe | $\varepsilon$ (mm) |
| --- | --- |
| Drawn copper or plastic (PVC, PE) | about 0.0015 to 0.007 |
| Commercial steel | about 0.045 |
| Cast iron | about 0.25 |
| Concrete | about 0.3 to 3 |

<Narrator who="gideon">

Check which friction factor a formula or a chart uses before you trust it. The **Darcy** factor used here is four times the **Fanning** factor, $f_\text{F} = \tau_w/(\tfrac12\rho V^2)$, common in chemical engineering and heat transfer: laminar flow is $f = 64/\mathrm{Re}$ in one convention and $f_\text{F} = 16/\mathrm{Re}$ in the other. Mixing them up gives an answer exactly four times wrong, which looks plausible enough to survive a hurried review.

</Narrator>

### Minor losses

Bends, valves, tees, entrances, exits and changes of section disturb the flow and cause extra losses. Each is described by a **loss coefficient** $K$, so that $h_m = K\,V^2/(2g)$, and the system's total loss is

$$h_L = \left(f\frac{L}{D} + \sum K\right)\frac{V^2}{2g}.$$

Indicative values, which vary with size, design and manufacturer: a sharp-edged entrance from a tank, about 0.5; a well-rounded one, below 0.05; any exit into a large tank, 1.0 (all the kinetic energy is dissipated); a 90° elbow, roughly 0.2 to 1; a fully open gate or ball valve, below 0.2; a fully open globe valve, around 5 to 10. In a plant room full of fittings, "minor" losses can be most of the total. One can be derived exactly: a momentum balance on a sudden enlargement from area $A_1$ to $A_2$ gives the **Borda–Carnot** loss $K = (1 - A_1/A_2)^2$ on the upstream velocity, which becomes the exit loss $K = 1$ as $A_2 \to \infty$.

</Section>

<Section id="computation-friction" kind="computation" title="Computing it: Colebrook by iteration">

Write $x = 1/\sqrt f$. Then Colebrook's equation is $x = -2\log_{10}\big(\tfrac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7} + \tfrac{2.51\,x}{\mathrm{Re}}\big)$, which already has the form $x = g(x)$, ready for **fixed-point iteration**: guess $x$, evaluate the right-hand side, repeat. It converges because $|g'(x)|$ is small (the logarithm flattens everything), well under 0.1 for turbulent flow, so each iteration gains at least one correct digit.

The program below solves the worked example (water at 20 °C, 3.0 L/s through 100 m of 50 mm commercial-steel pipe), compares Haaland's approximation, and prints a small Moody chart as a table.

```python run
import numpy as np

def colebrook(Re, rr, tol=1e-12):
    """Darcy friction factor from the Colebrook equation (turbulent flow).
    Re: Reynolds number; rr: relative roughness eps/D."""
    x = 8.0                                   # x = 1/sqrt(f); f = 0.016 is a fair first guess
    for it in range(100):
        x_new = -2.0 * np.log10(rr / 3.7 + 2.51 * x / Re)
        if abs(x_new - x) < tol:
            break
        x = x_new
    return 1.0 / x_new**2, it + 1

def haaland(Re, rr):
    return (-1.8 * np.log10((rr / 3.7) ** 1.11 + 6.9 / Re)) ** -2

# Worked example: water at 20 C, 3.0 L/s through 100 m of 50 mm commercial steel pipe
rho, nu = 998.0, 1.00e-6                      # kg/m^3, m^2/s
Q, D, L, eps = 3.0e-3, 0.050, 100.0, 0.045e-3
V = Q / (np.pi * D**2 / 4)
Re = V * D / nu
f, n_it = colebrook(Re, eps / D)
hf = f * (L / D) * V**2 / (2 * 9.81)
print(f"V = {V:.3f} m/s, Re = {Re:.0f}, eps/D = {eps / D:.4f}")
print(f"Colebrook f = {f:.5f} after {n_it} iterations; Haaland f = {haaland(Re, eps / D):.5f}")
print(f"head loss {hf:.2f} m of water = pressure drop {rho * 9.81 * hf / 1e3:.1f} kPa")

# A text Moody chart: f against Re for several relative roughnesses
rrs = [0.0, 1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2]
print("\n       Re  laminar" + "".join(f"  eps/D={r:<6g}" for r in rrs))
for Re in [1e3, 2e3, 4e3, 1e4, 1e5, 1e6, 1e7, 1e8]:
    lam = f"{64 / Re:8.4f}" if Re < 2300 else "       -"
    turb = "".join(f"  {colebrook(Re, r)[0]:12.4f}" if Re >= 4000 else "             -" for r in rrs)
    print(f" {Re:8.0e} {lam}{turb}")
```

The mean velocity is 1.53 m/s and the Reynolds number about 76 000, well into turbulence. The iteration settles to $f = 0.02248$ in a dozen steps, and Haaland's formula gives 0.02223, about 1% lower. The 100 m of pipe costs 5.35 m of head, a pressure drop of 52 kPa.

The table is the Moody chart in numbers. In a smooth pipe $f$ keeps falling, from 0.040 at $\mathrm{Re} = 4000$ to 0.006 at $10^8$; at $\varepsilon/D = 0.01$ it levels off at 0.038 from about $10^5$ on, where the pipe is fully rough and viscosity has stopped mattering. Notice also the jump at transition: at $\mathrm{Re} = 3000$ a laminar flow would have $f = 64/3000 = 0.021$, while Colebrook's smooth-pipe value there is 0.044, so a flow that trips into turbulence can double its pressure drop with no change in flow rate.

<Narrator who="margaret">

What does that 52 kPa cost? The hydraulic power is $\Delta p\,Q = 52\,400 \times 0.003 \approx 160$ W. Run that for a year (8760 hours) through a pump and motor that are together about 60% efficient, and it is roughly 2300 kWh of electricity for one modest pipe. Choosing the next pipe size up, 65 mm, cuts the friction loss by a factor of about 3.7, which is why pipe sizing is an economic optimisation between the cost of the pipe and the cost of the energy.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="pumps" kind="examples" title="Pumps, system curves and operating points">

A **centrifugal pump** spins an impeller that flings fluid outwards; its casing turns much of the fluid's speed into pressure. At a fixed speed the head it delivers falls as the flow rises, and the maker publishes this **pump curve** $H_\text{pump}(Q)$, measured to a standard such as ISO 9906, with the efficiency and power at each flow.

The pipework has its own curve. To push $Q$ through it, the pump must supply the static lift $\Delta z$ (plus any difference in surface pressures) and the losses, which grow roughly as $Q^2$:

$$H_\text{system}(Q) = \Delta z + \left(f\frac{L}{D} + \sum K\right)\frac{V^2}{2g}, \qquad V = \frac{Q}{\pi D^2/4}.$$

Pump and pipes must agree: the flow settles at the **operating point**, where the curves cross. The shaft power the pump then draws is

$$P = \frac{\rho g Q H}{\eta},$$

where $\eta$ is the pump efficiency at that point.

<Example title="Lifting water to a header tank">

A pump lifts water at 20 °C from a sump to a tank 12 m higher, through 150 m of 80 mm commercial-steel pipe with fittings whose loss coefficients add up to $\sum K = 8$. The pump, at its rated speed, has (as a model fitted to its published curve) $H = 32 - 0.060\,Q^2$, with $H$ in metres and $Q$ in L/s, and its best efficiency, 72%, at 14 L/s. Find the operating point and the power. Then the plant needs only 10 L/s: compare closing a valve (throttling) with slowing the pump.

Because $f$ depends on $Q$, the equation $H_\text{pump}(Q) = H_\text{system}(Q)$ has no closed-form solution, so we solve it by **bisection**: the difference is positive at $Q = 0$ and negative at large $Q$, and halving the bracket repeatedly pins down the root. For the slower pump we use the **affinity laws**: at speed ratio $s$, flow scales with $s$, head with $s^2$ and power with $s^3$ at corresponding points, so the curve becomes $H = 32 s^2 - 0.060\,Q^2$.

</Example>

```python run
import numpy as np

rho, nu, g = 998.0, 1.00e-6, 9.81
D, L, eps = 0.080, 150.0, 0.045e-3            # commercial steel pipe
dz, K_sum = 12.0, 8.0                         # lift (m) and sum of minor-loss coefficients
A = np.pi * D**2 / 4

def friction(Re, rr):
    if Re < 2300:
        return 64.0 / Re
    x = 8.0
    for _ in range(50):
        x = -2.0 * np.log10(rr / 3.7 + 2.51 * x / Re)
    return 1.0 / x**2

def system_head(q):                           # q in L/s -> head the pipework needs (m)
    if q <= 0:
        return dz
    V = q / 1000 / A
    f = friction(V * D / nu, eps / D)
    return dz + (f * L / D + K_sum) * V**2 / (2 * g)

def pump_head(q, s=1.0):                      # a model pump curve; s = speed / rated speed
    return 32.0 * s**2 - 0.060 * q**2

def pump_eff(q, s=1.0):                       # best efficiency 72 % at 14 L/s (rated speed)
    return 0.72 * (1 - ((q / s - 14.0) / 14.0) ** 2)

def bisect(fun, a, b, tol=1e-10):
    fa = fun(a)
    while b - a > tol:
        m = 0.5 * (a + b)
        if (fun(m) > 0) == (fa > 0):
            a, fa = m, fun(m)
        else:
            b = m
    return 0.5 * (a + b)

print("  Q (L/s)  pump H (m)  system H (m)")
for q in range(0, 24, 3):
    print(f"  {q:6.1f}  {pump_head(q):9.2f}  {system_head(q):11.2f}")

q = bisect(lambda q: pump_head(q) - system_head(q), 0.0, 23.0)
H, eta = pump_head(q), pump_eff(q)
P = rho * g * q / 1000 * H / eta
print(f"\noperating point: Q = {q:.2f} L/s, H = {H:.2f} m, efficiency {eta:.1%}, shaft power {P / 1e3:.2f} kW")

# The plant now needs only 10 L/s. Option 1: close a valve (throttle).
qt = 10.0
Ht = pump_head(qt)
Pt = rho * g * qt / 1000 * Ht / pump_eff(qt)
print(f"\nthrottled to 10 L/s: pump head {Ht:.2f} m, of which the valve burns {Ht - system_head(qt):.2f} m;"
      f" power {Pt / 1e3:.2f} kW")
# Option 2: slow the pump down until its curve passes through the system curve at 10 L/s.
s = np.sqrt((system_head(qt) + 0.060 * qt**2) / 32.0)
Ps = rho * g * qt / 1000 * system_head(qt) / pump_eff(qt, s)
print(f"variable speed: {s:.3f} x rated speed, head {system_head(qt):.2f} m, power {Ps / 1e3:.2f} kW"
      f" ({1 - Ps / Pt:.0%} less)")
```

The curves cross at 11.6 L/s and 24.0 m of head, near the pump's best efficiency (70%), and the pump draws 3.9 kW. To get 10 L/s with a valve, the pump must climb back up its curve to 26.0 m, and the valve throws away 5.0 m of it: the power hardly changes (3.85 kW). Slowing the pump to 92% of its rated speed instead delivers the same 10 L/s at the 21.0 m the system actually needs, for 3.0 kW, 22% less. That saving is why so many pumps and fans now run from variable-speed drives, the power electronics of [power electronics](/en/ee/power-electronics/) feeding the induction and permanent-magnet motors of [transformers and machines](/en/ee/transformers-and-machines/).

A pump also needs enough pressure at its inlet. If the pressure in the impeller falls to the liquid's vapour pressure (2.3 kPa for water at 20 °C), vapour bubbles form and collapse violently further on: **cavitation**, which sounds like gravel, wrecks performance and pits the metal. Makers publish the *net positive suction head required* (NPSHr), and the designer makes sure the system provides more, with a margin. Nor should a pump run for long against a closed valve, where all its power heats a small volume of trapped liquid; installations have minimum-flow lines and controls for this reason.

<Section id="drag" kind="human" title="Boundary layers and drag, in outline">

Outside pipes, the same no-slip condition shapes the flow around every object. In 1904 Ludwig Prandtl explained why fluids with tiny viscosity still feel friction: at high Reynolds number the viscous effects are confined to a thin **boundary layer** next to the surface, outside which the flow is nearly ideal and Bernoulli works. On a flat plate the laminar boundary layer grows as the square root of the distance $x$ from the leading edge, with thickness about $5x/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}$, where $\mathrm{Re}_x = Vx/\nu$; for air at 10 m/s, that is about 4 mm at 0.5 m along the plate. Near $\mathrm{Re}_x \approx 5 \times 10^5$ it usually becomes turbulent, thicker and with more friction.

If the outer flow slows down (as it does around the back of a cylinder, where the pressure rises again), the slow fluid deep in the boundary layer can be brought to a halt and pushed backwards. The boundary layer **separates** from the surface, leaving a broad, eddying **wake** at low pressure. The force a body feels in a stream is the **drag**, the sum of skin friction (shear stress on the surface) and pressure or form drag (the low pressure of the wake pulling backwards). It is written with a **drag coefficient**:

$$F_D = C_D\,\tfrac12\rho V^2 A,$$

with $A$ normally the frontal area. Streamlined bodies avoid separation and have $C_D$ below 0.1; a sphere in the usual range has $C_D \approx 0.4$–0.5; most cars of 2026 are between about 0.2 and 0.35; an upright cyclist is around 0.9 to 1.1 on a frontal area of about 0.5 m². At about $\mathrm{Re} = 3 \times 10^5$ a smooth sphere's boundary layer turns turbulent before it separates; the more energetic turbulent layer clings on further round the back, the wake narrows, and $C_D$ falls by a factor of three or more: the **drag crisis**. The dimples on a golf ball trip the boundary layer early, so the ball enjoys this low drag at the speeds it actually flies.

At the other extreme, for $\mathrm{Re} \lt 1$, George Stokes found in 1851 that a sphere of diameter $d$ feels $F_D = 3\pi\mu V d$, that is, $C_D = 24/\mathrm{Re}$: the law of fine dust, mist and silt. Between the regimes engineers use fitted correlations. The program below finds how fast quartz sand settles in still water, balancing weight minus buoyancy against drag with the Schiller–Naumann correlation $C_D = (24/\mathrm{Re})(1 + 0.15\,\mathrm{Re}^{0.687})$ up to $\mathrm{Re} = 1000$ and $C_D \approx 0.44$ beyond: the calculation behind settling tanks in water treatment.

```python run
import numpy as np

g = 9.81
rho, mu = 998.0, 1.00e-3                      # water at 20 C
rho_p = 2650.0                                # quartz sand

def drag_coefficient(Re):
    """Sphere drag: Schiller-Naumann up to Re = 1000, then roughly constant."""
    return 24.0 / Re * (1 + 0.15 * Re**0.687) if Re < 1000 else 0.44

def settling_speed(d):
    V = g * d**2 * (rho_p - rho) / (18 * mu)  # start from Stokes' law
    for _ in range(200):
        Re = rho * V * d / mu
        V_new = np.sqrt(4 * g * d * (rho_p - rho) / (3 * rho * drag_coefficient(Re)))
        if abs(V_new - V) < 1e-12:
            break
        V = 0.5 * (V + V_new)                 # damped fixed-point iteration
    return V_new, rho * V_new * d / mu

sig3 = lambda v: f"{v:#.3g}".rstrip(".")       # three significant figures
print(" d (mm)   Re        V (mm/s)   Stokes V (mm/s)   time to sink 1 m")
for d_mm in [0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0]:
    d = d_mm / 1000
    V, Re = settling_speed(d)
    Vs = g * d**2 * (rho_p - rho) / (18 * mu)
    t = 1.0 / V
    when = f"{t / 3600:6.1f} h" if t > 3600 else (f"{t / 60:6.1f} min" if t > 60 else f"{t:6.1f} s")
    print(f" {d_mm:5.2f}  {Re:8.3g}  {sig3(V * 1000):>9}  {sig3(Vs * 1000):>14}      {when}")
```

Fine silt of 0.01 mm sinks at less than 0.1 mm/s and takes about three hours to settle through a metre of water, exactly as Stokes' law predicts. By 0.1 mm the Reynolds number is near 1 and Stokes is already 13% too fast; for a 1 mm grain it is six times too fast, and for 5 mm gravel, at $\mathrm{Re} \approx 2500$, it would be wrong by a factor of 45. One formula, applied outside its range, would have ruined a settling-tank design.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The table assumes smooth spheres falling alone in still water. Real sand grains are angular and settle perhaps 10–30% more slowly; crowds of particles hinder each other; and in a river the turbulence keeps the finest grains suspended indefinitely. The drag correlation is a fit to sphere experiments, so its accuracy is a few per cent at best, which is plenty for sizing a tank (with the generous margin designers add anyway) and nonsense for quoting three significant figures as truth.

</Narrator>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Pipes and pumps in professional practice">

Piping is designed by codes as much as by equations. Pressure piping in many countries follows the ASME B31 codes (B31.1 for power piping, B31.3 for process piping) or, in Europe, EN 13480 under the Pressure Equipment Directive, which set wall thicknesses, materials, supports, inspection and pressure testing. First sizing uses rules of thumb (pumped water lines are commonly sized for about 1–3 m/s, balancing pipe cost against energy, noise and erosion), checked with the calculations of this lesson. Real systems are networks: a city's water supply has thousands of pipes, loops, tanks, pumps and valves, analysed by solvers that apply mass conservation at every junction and the energy equation round every loop, solved by Newton's method. The best-known open-source tool is the US Environmental Protection Agency's [EPANET](https://www.epa.gov/water-research/epanet); commercial packages build on similar methods.

Transients matter as much as steady flow. Close a valve suddenly and the moving column of liquid must stop: a pressure wave runs back along the pipe at the speed of sound in the pipe-and-liquid system (around 1000–1400 m/s in a water main), raising the pressure by roughly $\rho a \Delta V$, Joukowsky's estimate. Stopping water flowing at 2 m/s can add 20 bar or more. This **water hammer** bursts pipes, which is why valves on large lines close slowly, pumps are stopped gradually, and surge tanks and relief valves protect long pipelines. Any system containing fluid under pressure is designed, tested and inspected under pressure-equipment rules, with relief devices sized for the worst case, and only trained people work on it.

Pump selection follows the method above: plot the system curve, choose a pump whose best-efficiency point sits near the duty, check NPSH, and consider how the duty varies over a year; the [Hydraulic Institute](https://www.pumps.org/) and pump makers publish guidance and selection software. Pumps that move water between reservoirs also store energy for electricity grids (see [renewables and storage](/en/ee/renewables-and-storage/) in Lumenary EE and <LessonLink slug="wind-and-water-turbines">wind and water turbines</LessonLink> later in this course). And when the geometry is too complicated for loss coefficients (a new valve, a pump inlet, an artery with a stent) engineers turn to <LessonLink slug="computational-fluid-dynamics">computational fluid dynamics</LessonLink>, and then test the result in a flow rig.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

The German engineer Gotthilf Hagen measured water flow in brass tubes in 1839, and the French physician Jean Léonard Marie Poiseuille, studying how blood moves in capillaries, found the same fourth-power law independently in experiments published in the early 1840s. Julius Weisbach in Saxony proposed the form of the friction law in 1845, and Henry Darcy, who had built Dijon's clean water supply, confirmed it with careful measurements on pipes of many materials in 1857. Osborne Reynolds's dye experiment of 1883 identified the two regimes and the number that separates them. Prandtl's boundary layer of 1904 reconciled ideal-flow theory with real drag, and his student Johann Nikuradse glued graded sand inside pipes to measure roughness in the early 1930s. Colebrook combined the smooth and rough laws in 1939, and Moody's chart of 1944 put them on every engineer's desk until computers made iteration free.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The pressure falls along a pipe because the fluid speeds up."** In a pipe of constant bore, continuity keeps the mean speed constant. The pressure falls because friction turns mechanical energy into internal energy; the pressure gradient is exactly what balances the wall shear.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Double the flow, double the pressure drop."** Only in laminar flow. In turbulent flow the pressure drop rises roughly as $Q^{1.75}$ in smooth pipes and as $Q^2$ in fully rough ones, so doubling the flow multiplies it by about 3.4 to 4, and the pumping power by about 7 to 8.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bigger pump gives more flow."** The flow is set by where the pump curve meets the system curve. A pump much bigger than the duty ends up throttled by a valve, wasting energy, or running far from its best efficiency, where it wears faster. Matching the pump to the system is the job.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A perfectly smooth pipe has no friction."** Even a smooth pipe has the viscous shear of the no-slip condition: its friction factor is $64/\mathrm{Re}$ if laminar and given by the smooth-pipe limit of Colebrook if turbulent. Roughness adds to friction only once the bumps poke out of the viscous layer at the wall.

</Misconception>
