<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="The parts every machine shares">

Open a bicycle hub, a washing machine, a car gearbox or a wind-turbine nacelle and you find the same family of parts. **Shafts** carry torque and hold things in line. **Bearings** let shafts turn with little friction. **Gears**, **belts** and **chains** carry power from one shaft to another and change speed and torque on the way. **Bolts** hold the whole thing together, and **springs** store energy, push parts into contact or soak up shocks. These are the **machine elements**.

Most of them are not designed from scratch. They are bought: bearings from a handful of large makers in Sweden, Germany, Japan, the United States and China; bolts and springs by the million to international standards; belts and chains in catalogued sizes. The engineer's job is to work out the loads, choose the right *kind* of element, size it, and pick the catalogue part that will survive, with margin, for the life the <LessonLink slug="engineering-design-and-cad">specification</LessonLink> demands.

This lesson follows one example through: a countershaft in a small farm machine, driven by a V-belt and carrying a spur pinion, transmitting 7.5 kW at 960 rev/min. You will size the shaft, choose its bearings, check its gears and belt, bolt down a bearing cap, and finish with a spring.

</Section>

<Section id="shafts" kind="definitions" title="Shafts: combined loading and fatigue">

A shaft transmits torque $T = P/\omega$, and the gears, pulleys and sprockets on it push it sideways, so it also bends. At the surface of a solid round shaft of diameter $d$, from <LessonLink slug="axial-torsion-and-bending">axial load, torsion and bending</LessonLink>,

$$\sigma = \frac{32M}{\pi d^3}, \qquad \tau = \frac{16T}{\pi d^3}.$$

The crucial fact about a rotating shaft is that the bending stress is **fully reversed**: a point on the surface is in tension at the top of each revolution and in compression at the bottom. A shaft turning at 960 rev/min goes through more than a billion stress cycles in 20 000 hours, so shafts are designed against **fatigue**, as in <LessonLink slug="failure-and-fatigue">failure and fatigue</LessonLink>. The torque, by contrast, is usually steady.

<Definition term="shaft sizing for combined bending and torsion">

For a static check against yield (distortion energy, von Mises), the equivalent stress $\sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}$ must not exceed $S_y/n$, which gives

$$d = \left[\frac{16\,n}{\pi S_y}\sqrt{4M^2 + 3T^2}\right]^{1/3}.$$

For infinite fatigue life with an alternating bending moment $M_a$ and a steady torque $T_m$, combining the von Mises equivalent stresses with the Goodman line gives

$$d = \left[\frac{16\,n}{\pi}\left(\frac{2K_f M_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sqrt{3}\,K_{fs} T_m}{S_{ut}}\right)\right]^{1/3},$$

where $S_e$ is the corrected endurance limit, $S_{ut}$ the tensile strength, and $K_f$, $K_{fs}$ the fatigue stress-concentration factors at the shoulder, groove or keyway being checked.

</Definition>

The moments come from statics. Resolve every force into two perpendicular planes, find the bearing reactions and bending moments in each, and combine them, $M = \sqrt{M_y^2 + M_z^2}$, because a round shaft bends about whichever axis the resultant points along.

<Section id="computation-shaft" kind="computation" title="Computing it: sizing the countershaft">

The program finds the forces from the power and speed, solves for the bearing reactions in the vertical and horizontal planes, computes the resultant bending moment along the shaft, and sizes the shaft at bearing B. The steel is a medium-carbon steel with $S_y = 350$ MPa and $S_{ut} = 600$ MPa; its endurance limit, corrected for surface finish, size and reliability as in the fatigue lesson, is taken as $S_e = 180$ MPa; the shoulder fillet at the bearing seat has $K_f = 1.7$ and $K_{fs} = 1.5$; and the design factor is $n = 2$.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A countershaft: bearings at A (x = 0) and B (x = 250 mm), a spur pinion at x = 90 mm,
# and an overhung V-belt pulley at x = 330 mm. It carries 7.5 kW at 960 rpm.
P, n = 7500.0, 960.0                          # W, rev/min
T = P / (2 * np.pi * n / 60) * 1e3            # torque, N*mm
d_gear, D_pulley = 120.0, 200.0               # mm
Wt = 2 * T / d_gear                           # gear tangential force (horizontal plane, z)
Wr = Wt * np.tan(np.radians(20))              # gear radial force (vertical plane, y)
belt = 2 * T / D_pulley * (3 + 1) / (3 - 1)   # T1 + T2 with T1/T2 = 3 (horizontal, z)
print(f"torque {T / 1e3:.1f} N m; gear Wt = {Wt:.0f} N, Wr = {Wr:.0f} N; belt pull {belt:.0f} N")

xA, xB, xG, xP = 0.0, 250.0, 90.0, 330.0
def reactions(loads):
    """Point loads [(x, F)] on a beam on supports at xA, xB: return (RA, RB) from equilibrium."""
    RB = sum(F * (x - xA) for x, F in loads) / (xB - xA)
    RA = sum(F for _, F in loads) - RB
    return RA, RB

y_loads = [(xG, Wr)]                          # vertical plane
z_loads = [(xG, Wt), (xP, belt)]              # horizontal plane (both act the same way here)
RAy, RBy = reactions(y_loads)
RAz, RBz = reactions(z_loads)

x = np.linspace(0, xP, 331)
def moment(x, loads, RA, RB):
    """Bending moment at each x: sum of moments of the forces to the left of the section."""
    M = RA * (x - xA) + np.where(x > xB, RB * (x - xB), 0.0)
    for xi, F in loads:
        M = M - np.where(x > xi, F * (x - xi), 0.0)
    return M
My = moment(x, y_loads, RAy, RBy)
Mz = moment(x, z_loads, RAz, RBz)
M = np.hypot(My, Mz)

print(f"bearing A radial load {np.hypot(RAy, RAz):.0f} N, bearing B {np.hypot(RBy, RBz):.0f} N")
print("\n  x (mm)   M (N m)")
for xi in [0, 45, 90, 130, 170, 210, 250, 290, 330]:
    m = M[int(xi)] / 1e3
    print(f"  {xi:5d}   {m:7.1f}  " + "#" * int(round(m / 3)))

# Size the shaft at the bearing seat B, where M is greatest and the torque also acts.
MB = M[int(xB)]
Sy, Sut, Se = 350.0, 600.0, 180.0            # MPa: yield, tensile, corrected endurance limit
Kf, Kfs, nd = 1.7, 1.5, 2.0                   # fatigue concentration (shoulder fillet), design factor
d_static = (16 * nd / (np.pi * Sy) * np.sqrt(4 * MB**2 + 3 * T**2)) ** (1 / 3)
# Rotating shaft: bending fully reversed (Ma = M), torque steady (Tm = T). DE-Goodman criterion:
d_fatigue = (16 * nd / np.pi * (2 * Kf * MB / Se + np.sqrt(3) * Kfs * T / Sut)) ** (1 / 3)
print(f"\nat bearing B: M = {MB / 1e3:.1f} N m, T = {T / 1e3:.1f} N m")
print(f"diameter for static yield (n = 2):        {d_static:.1f} mm")
print(f"diameter for infinite fatigue life (n = 2): {d_fatigue:.1f} mm")
d = 5 * np.ceil(d_fatigue / 5)                # next bearing bore size (multiples of 5 mm)
sa = Kf * 32 * MB / (np.pi * d**3)
tm = Kfs * 16 * T / (np.pi * d**3)
n_check = 1 / (sa / Se + np.sqrt(3) * tm / Sut)
print(f"choose d = {d:.0f} mm: sigma_a = {sa:.0f} MPa, tau_m = {tm:.0f} MPa, fatigue factor of safety {n_check:.2f}")
```

The torque is 74.6 N m. The bearings carry 430 N (A) and 2423 N (B), and the bending moment peaks at 119.4 N m at bearing B, where the overhung pulley acts. Yield alone would need only a 19.9 mm shaft; fatigue needs 29.7 mm. Rounded up to a 30 mm bearing seat, the shaft has a fatigue factor of safety of 2.06.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Look at the two diameters: 19.9 mm for yield, 29.7 mm for fatigue, which is more than three times the cross-section's bending resistance ($d^3$). Almost every rotating shaft is a fatigue problem first, and the overhung pulley is the reason this one is as thick as it is. Moving the pulley 40 mm closer to bearing B would halve the moment there, a design change worth more than a better steel.

</Narrator>

A full shaft design checks every shoulder, groove and keyway, not only the worst one, and adds two stiffness checks: the **slope** at the bearings (ball bearings tolerate only a small misalignment, and roller bearings far less) and the **deflection** at the gear, which spoils the tooth contact. It also checks that the first **critical speed**, at which the spinning shaft whirls like a skipping rope, is well above the running speed, a calculation you can do with the methods of <LessonLink slug="vibrations">vibrations</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="bearings" kind="definitions" title="Rolling bearings and their life">

A **plain bearing** (a bush, or a hydrodynamic journal bearing in which the shaft floats on a pressurised oil film) supports the shaft on a sliding surface. A **rolling-element bearing** puts balls or rollers between an inner and an outer ring. Deep-groove ball bearings take radial and moderate axial loads at high speeds and are the most common bearing in the world; angular-contact ball bearings take heavier axial loads in one direction; cylindrical roller bearings take heavy radial loads; tapered roller bearings, as in a car's wheel hub, take heavy combined loads; spherical roller bearings tolerate misalignment; needle bearings fit where there is little room.

Even a perfectly lubricated, correctly loaded rolling bearing eventually fails by **rolling-contact fatigue**: a crack starts just below the surface of a ring, where the contact shear stress peaks, and a flake of steel spalls out. Because fatigue is statistical, nominally identical bearings fail at very different times, and bearing life is defined statistically.

<Definition term="L10 life and basic dynamic load rating">

The **basic rating life** $L_{10}$ is the number of revolutions (or hours at a given speed) that 90% of a large group of identical bearings, under the same conditions, will reach or exceed. The **basic dynamic load rating** $C$, published by the maker and calculated by the method of ISO 281, is the constant load under which $L_{10}$ is one million revolutions. For any other equivalent load $P$,

$$L_{10} = \left(\frac{C}{P}\right)^{p} \times 10^6\ \text{revolutions}, \qquad p = 3 \text{ for ball bearings}, \quad p = \tfrac{10}{3} \text{ for roller bearings}.$$

When there is also an axial load, $P = X F_r + Y F_a$, with factors $X$ and $Y$ given in the maker's catalogue for each bearing type.

</Definition>

```python run
import numpy as np

# Rolling-bearing life for bearing B of the countershaft: 2423 N radial, no axial load, 960 rpm.
Fr, Fa, n = 2423.0, 0.0, 960.0
P = Fr                                   # equivalent dynamic load; with axial load, P = X Fr + Y Fa
p = 3.0                                  # life exponent: 3 for ball bearings, 10/3 for roller bearings
L_req_h = 20000                          # required L10 life in hours (an industrial machine on one shift)

L_req = 60 * n * L_req_h / 1e6           # in millions of revolutions
C_req = P * L_req ** (1 / p)
print(f"required life: {L_req_h} h at {n:.0f} rpm = {L_req:.0f} million revolutions")
print(f"required basic dynamic load rating C >= {C_req / 1e3:.1f} kN")

# Three 30 mm-bore deep-groove ball bearings of increasing size. The ratings are
# round illustrative numbers, not taken from any catalogue: always use the maker's data.
candidates = {"extra-light series": 13.0e3, "light series": 20.0e3, "medium series": 29.0e3}
print("\nbearing               C (kN)   L10 (million rev)   L10 (hours)")
for name, C in candidates.items():
    L10 = (C / P) ** p                    # millions of revolutions
    print(f"{name:20s} {C / 1e3:6.1f}   {L10:14.0f}   {L10 * 1e6 / (60 * n):12.0f}")

# L10 is the life that 90 % of bearings reach. For higher reliability, a two-parameter
# Weibull distribution with slope b = 1.5 (a common assumption for ball bearings) gives
# L_R = L10 * (ln(1/R) / ln(1/0.9)) ** (1/b).
b = 1.5
C = candidates["medium series"]
L10h = (C / P) ** p * 1e6 / (60 * n)
print("\nmedium-series bearing, life at other reliabilities:")
for R in [0.50, 0.90, 0.95, 0.99]:
    factor = (np.log(1 / R) / np.log(1 / 0.9)) ** (1 / b)
    print(f"  R = {R:.2f}: factor {factor:5.2f}, life {factor * L10h:8.0f} h")

# Life is very sensitive to load: double the load, and a ball bearing lasts 1/8 as long.
for k in [0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0]:
    print(f"load x {k:3.1f}: life x {(1 / k) ** p:6.3f}")
```

Twenty thousand hours at 960 rev/min is 1152 million revolutions, which needs $C \ge 25.4$ kN. Of the three illustrative 30 mm bearings, only the medium series qualifies, with an $L_{10}$ of about 29 800 hours; the light series would reach only about 9 800. If the machine needs 99% of its bearings to survive, the same bearing's life falls to about 6 200 hours, while the median bearing lasts about 105 000.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The cube law is the number to remember: 10% more load costs 25% of the life, and double the load leaves one eighth. That is why a modest overload, a misaligned seat or an unbalanced rotor kills bearings so quickly, and why vibration monitoring of bearings is one of the most valuable measurements in any factory.

</Narrator>

The basic rating life assumes clean, adequate lubrication. ISO 281 also defines a **modified rating life** that multiplies $L_{10}$ by a factor $a_\text{ISO}$ for lubricant film thickness, contamination and the fatigue limit of the steel; in clean, well-lubricated conditions it can be several times larger, and in dirty or poorly lubricated ones a fraction. In practice, most bearings do not die of fatigue at all but of contamination, poor lubrication, corrosion or bad fitting.

<Narrator who="gideon">

$L_{10}$ is not "the life of the bearing". It is a statement about a population: one bearing in ten will fail before it. A machine with twenty bearings, each at its $L_{10}$, has a much smaller chance of reaching that age with all of them intact, which is why designers of large machines size for longer lives or higher reliability.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="gears" kind="human" title="Gears">

Gears transmit power between shafts by teeth, without slip, with efficiencies of 98–99% per spur or helical stage. For the speed ratio to stay exactly constant as the teeth roll through mesh, the common normal at the contact point must always pass through one fixed point on the line of centres, the **pitch point** (the fundamental law of gearing). The **involute** tooth profile, traced by the end of a taut string unwound from a circle, satisfies this law, and keeps doing so even if the centre distance is slightly wrong, which is why almost every power gear is involute.

<Definition term="spur-gear geometry">

A gear with $z$ teeth and **module** $m$ (in mm) has **pitch diameter** $d = mz$. Meshing gears must share the same module and **pressure angle** $\varphi$ (20° is the usual standard). The **speed ratio** is $i = \omega_1/\omega_2 = z_2/z_1 = d_2/d_1$, and the **centre distance** is $a = m(z_1 + z_2)/2$. A standard tooth reaches one module above the pitch circle (the **addendum**) and 1.25 modules below it (the **dedendum**). The involute starts at the **base circle**, $d_b = d\cos\varphi$, and teeth are spaced along the line of action at the **base pitch** $p_b = \pi m \cos\varphi$.

</Definition>

The **contact ratio** is the length of the path of contact (the part of the line of action inside both tip circles) divided by the base pitch: the average number of tooth pairs in contact. It must exceed 1, or the next pair would not take over before the last let go; 1.4 to 1.9 is usual for spur gears. Too few teeth on a pinion and the cutting tool scoops out the base of the involute (**undercut**), weakening the tooth; with a 20° pressure angle and standard teeth that happens below about 17 teeth, unless the teeth are cut with a profile shift.

The tooth force acts along the line of action. Its **tangential** component $W_t = 2T/d = P/v$ does the work; the **radial** component $W_r = W_t \tan\varphi$ pushes the gears apart and loads the bearings. Gear teeth fail in two main ways, and both must be checked:

- **Bending fatigue at the root.** Wilfred Lewis, in Philadelphia in 1892, modelled a tooth as a cantilever and wrote the root stress as $\sigma = W_t/(b\,m\,Y)$, with face width $b$ and a form factor $Y$ depending on the tooth shape and number of teeth.
- **Surface fatigue (pitting)**, from the Hertz contact stress between the curved tooth flanks, which are modelled as two cylinders pressed together.

Modern rating standards, ISO 6336 and the AGMA standards, keep this structure and add factors for dynamic load, load distribution across the face, material, lubrication and reliability. **Helical gears** have teeth cut at an angle, so contact sweeps gradually across the face: they are quieter and stronger, at the cost of an axial thrust on the bearings. **Bevel gears** connect intersecting shafts, as in a car's differential, and **worm gears** give large ratios (often 10 to 60) in one stage between crossed shafts, with lower efficiency.

</Section>

<Section id="belts-chains" kind="human" title="Belts and chains">

A **belt** transmits power by friction between it and its pulleys, which makes it quiet, cheap and tolerant of misalignment, and lets it slip harmlessly under a shock. The tight side pulls with $T_1$, the slack side with $T_2$, and the power is $(T_1 - T_2)v$. How large the ratio $T_1/T_2$ may be before the belt slips is set by the **capstan equation** (Euler and Eytelwein, 18th century): for friction coefficient $\mu$ and wrap angle $\theta$,

$$\frac{T_1}{T_2} \le e^{\mu\theta}.$$

A **V-belt** wedges into its groove, so the normal force, and with it the friction, is multiplied by $1/\sin(\beta/2)$ for a groove of included angle $\beta$. **Synchronous** (toothed) belts have teeth that engage the pulley like a gear, so they cannot slip and are used for camshafts and printers. Belts must be tensioned, and that tension loads the shaft and bearings, as the belt pull in our countershaft did.

A **roller chain** runs on toothed sprockets and cannot slip either; it carries large forces in little space, as on every bicycle and motorcycle. Because the chain wraps the sprocket as a polygon, not a circle, its speed pulses once per tooth, the **chordal effect**, by a fraction $1 - \cos(180°/z)$ for a sprocket with $z$ teeth. Chains need lubrication and wear by stretching at their pins.

</Section>

<Section id="computation-gears" kind="computation" title="Computing it: a gear pair, a belt and a chain">

The countershaft's pinion has 40 teeth of module 3 mm and drives a 100-tooth gear; the face width is 30 mm. The program computes the geometry, the contact ratio, the tooth loads and rough bending and contact stresses, then the limiting tension ratios of a flat belt and a V-belt, and the chordal speed variation of chains. The Lewis form factor $Y \approx 0.39$ is a typical value for 40 teeth at 20°, and Barth's dynamic factor, from the 1890s, is a rough allowance for impact between imperfect teeth; a real rating uses ISO 6336 or AGMA factors instead.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A spur gear pair: the countershaft's 40-tooth pinion drives a 100-tooth gear.
m, z1, z2 = 3.0, 40, 100                 # module (mm), numbers of teeth
phi = np.radians(20.0)                   # pressure angle
T1, n1 = 74.6e3, 960.0                   # pinion torque (N mm) and speed (rpm)
b = 30.0                                 # face width, mm

d1, d2 = m * z1, m * z2                  # pitch diameters
a = (d1 + d2) / 2                        # centre distance
ra1, ra2 = d1 / 2 + m, d2 / 2 + m        # tip radii (addendum = 1 module)
rb1, rb2 = d1 / 2 * np.cos(phi), d2 / 2 * np.cos(phi)   # base-circle radii
pb = np.pi * m * np.cos(phi)             # base pitch: tooth spacing along the line of action
contact_length = np.sqrt(ra1**2 - rb1**2) + np.sqrt(ra2**2 - rb2**2) - a * np.sin(phi)
eps = contact_length / pb                # contact ratio: average number of tooth pairs in mesh
print(f"pitch diameters {d1:.0f} and {d2:.0f} mm, centre distance {a:.1f} mm, ratio {z2 / z1:.2f}")
print(f"output speed {n1 * z1 / z2:.0f} rpm, output torque {T1 * z2 / z1 / 1e3:.0f} N m (losses ignored)")
print(f"length of contact {contact_length:.1f} mm, base pitch {pb:.2f} mm, contact ratio {eps:.2f}")
print(f"fewest pinion teeth without undercut at 20 deg: {2 / np.sin(phi)**2:.1f}")

Wt = 2 * T1 / d1                         # tangential (useful) force at the pitch circle
Wr = Wt * np.tan(phi)                    # radial (separating) force
v = np.pi * d1 / 1e3 * n1 / 60           # pitch-line speed, m/s
Kv = (6.1 + v) / 6.1                     # Barth's dynamic factor for cut teeth (a rough, old estimate)
Y = 0.39                                 # Lewis form factor, about right for 40 teeth at 20 deg
sigma_b = Kv * Wt / (b * m * Y)
print(f"\nWt = {Wt:.0f} N, Wr = {Wr:.0f} N, pitch-line speed {v:.2f} m/s, Kv = {Kv:.2f}")
print(f"Lewis bending stress at the tooth root ~ {sigma_b:.0f} MPa")

# Hertz contact stress at the pitch point: two cylinders of radius d sin(phi) / 2.
E, nu = 206e3, 0.30
E_star = 1 / (2 * (1 - nu**2) / E)
R = 1 / (2 / (d1 * np.sin(phi)) + 2 / (d2 * np.sin(phi)))
w = Kv * Wt / (b * np.cos(phi))          # normal load per unit face width, N/mm
p0 = np.sqrt(w * E_star / (np.pi * R))
print(f"Hertz contact pressure at the pitch point ~ {p0:.0f} MPa")

# Belts: the ratio of tight-side to slack-side tension that friction can hold (capstan equation).
mu, wrap = 0.30, np.radians(160)
groove = np.radians(40)                  # included angle of a V-groove
print(f"\nflat belt, mu = 0.30, 160 deg wrap: T1/T2 up to {np.exp(mu * wrap):.1f}")
print(f"V-belt in a 40 deg groove:          T1/T2 up to {np.exp(mu / np.sin(groove / 2) * wrap):.1f}")

# Chains: the chordal effect makes the chain speed pulse once per tooth.
for z in [9, 12, 17, 21, 25]:
    print(f"sprocket with {z:2d} teeth: chain speed varies by {100 * (1 - np.cos(np.pi / z)):.1f} %")
```

The pair has a centre distance of 210 mm and reduces 960 rev/min to 384 rev/min, raising the torque to 186 N m (less a percent or two of losses). The contact ratio is 1.78: most of the time two pairs of teeth share the load. The tooth bending stress, about 70 MPa with the dynamic factor, is modest, but the contact pressure at the pitch point is about 464 MPa, more than six times higher. That is typical: in hardened, well-made gears, surface durability usually decides the size. The V-belt can hold a tension ratio of 11.6 against 2.3 for a flat belt on the same pulleys, which is why the countershaft's design ratio of 3 is comfortable for a V-belt and would slip a flat one. And a 12-tooth sprocket makes a chain's speed pulse by 3.4%, a 25-tooth one by only 0.8%, which is why designers avoid very small sprockets on fast, smooth drives.

</Section>

<Section id="fasteners-springs" kind="examples" title="Bolts, preload and springs">

A bolted joint is not just a bolt holding two plates. When the nut is tightened, the bolt is stretched like a stiff spring and the plates are squeezed like a stiffer one. That built-in tension, the **preload** $F_i$, is the whole point. While the plates stay in contact, an external load $P$ trying to pull the joint apart is shared: the bolt's tension rises by only $CP$, where the **joint constant** $C = k_b/(k_b + k_m)$ is the bolt's share of the combined stiffness, and the plates' compression falls by $(1 - C)P$. The joint separates only when $P$ reaches $F_i/(1 - C)$.

Preload is why a properly tightened bolt survives a fluctuating load that would quickly break a loose one: the bolt sees only a fraction $C$ of the fluctuation. Bolts are graded by **property class** under ISO 898-1: in class 8.8, the first number times 100 is the nominal tensile strength in MPa (800) and the second number gives the yield as a fraction of it (0.8, so 640 MPa). Preload is most often set by tightening torque, $T \approx K F_i d$, where the **nut factor** $K$ (about 0.2 for plain steel threads) lumps together thread and under-head friction, which swallow roughly 90% of the torque. Where preload matters most, engineers measure it more directly: by turning the nut through a set angle after snugging, by ultrasonic measurement of the bolt's stretch, by hydraulic tensioners that stretch the bolt before the nut is run down, or with direct-tension-indicating washers.

**Springs** store energy elastically. In a helical compression spring the wire is twisted as the spring is compressed, so the wire is in torsion. For wire diameter $d$, mean coil diameter $D$, $N_a$ active coils and shear modulus $G$,

$$k = \frac{G d^4}{8 D^3 N_a}, \qquad \tau_\text{max} = K_W \frac{8 F D}{\pi d^3}, \qquad K_W = \frac{4C - 1}{4C - 4} + \frac{0.615}{C},$$

where $C = D/d$ is the **spring index** (usually 4 to 12) and the Wahl factor $K_W$ corrects for direct shear and the curvature of the coil.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A preloaded M10 bolt, property class 8.8, clamping a bearing cap.
d, pitch = 10.0, 1.5                          # nominal diameter and thread pitch, mm
As = np.pi / 4 * (d - 0.9382 * pitch) ** 2    # tensile stress area of an ISO metric thread
Rm = 8 * 100.0                                # class 8.8: first number x 100 = nominal tensile strength, MPa
Re = 0.8 * Rm                                 # second number: yield = 0.8 x tensile strength
Fi = 0.70 * Re * As                           # target preload: 70 % of yield load
K = 0.20                                      # nut factor (friction in threads and under the head)
torque = K * Fi * d / 1e3                     # N m
print(f"stress area {As:.1f} mm^2, yield {Re:.0f} MPa, target preload {Fi / 1e3:.1f} kN")
print(f"tightening torque T = K F d = {torque:.0f} N m")
for K_actual in [0.15, 0.20, 0.25]:           # friction is never known exactly
    print(f"  if the real nut factor is {K_actual:.2f}, that torque gives {torque * 1e3 / (K_actual * d) / 1e3:5.1f} kN")

# The joint: bolt and clamped parts act as springs in parallel once preloaded.
E = 206e3
grip = 30.0                                   # clamped length, mm
kb = (np.pi / 4 * d**2) * E / grip            # bolt stiffness (unthreaded shank), N/mm
km = 3.0 * kb                                 # clamped parts are stiffer: from a pressure-cone model or FE
C = kb / (kb + km)                            # joint constant: share of external load felt by the bolt
P = 8e3                                       # external load pulling the joint apart, N
print(f"\nbolt stiffness {kb / 1e3:.0f} kN/mm, members {km / 1e3:.0f} kN/mm, joint constant C = {C:.2f}")
print(f"under P = {P / 1e3:.0f} kN: bolt force {(Fi + C * P) / 1e3:.1f} kN, clamp force {(Fi - (1 - C) * P) / 1e3:.1f} kN")
print(f"the joint separates only at P = Fi / (1 - C) = {Fi / (1 - C) / 1e3:.1f} kN")
print(f"if P cycles 0 -> 8 kN: bolt stress amplitude {C * P / 2 / As:.0f} MPa preloaded, {P / 2 / As:.0f} MPa if loose")

# A helical compression spring of music wire.
G = 79.3e3                                    # shear modulus of spring steel, MPa
dw, D, Na = 3.0, 24.0, 8                      # wire diameter, mean coil diameter (mm), active coils
k = G * dw**4 / (8 * D**3 * Na)               # spring rate, N/mm
Cs = D / dw                                   # spring index
Kw = (4 * Cs - 1) / (4 * Cs - 4) + 0.615 / Cs # Wahl factor: direct shear plus coil curvature
F = 100.0
tau = Kw * 8 * F * D / (np.pi * dw**3)
print(f"\nspring: index {Cs:.0f}, rate {k:.2f} N/mm, deflection under {F:.0f} N = {F / k:.1f} mm")
print(f"Wahl factor {Kw:.3f}, maximum shear stress {tau:.0f} MPa")
```

The M10 bolt's stress area is 58.0 mm², and a preload of 26.0 kN (70% of the yield load) needs about 52 N m. Under an 8 kN separating load, the bolt's force rises only to 28.0 kN while the clamping force falls to 20.0 kN; the joint would not open until 34.6 kN. If the 8 kN load cycles, the bolt's stress amplitude is 17 MPa when preloaded and 69 MPa when loose, four times more. The spring has a rate of 7.26 N/mm, deflects 13.8 mm under 100 N, and has a peak shear stress of 268 MPa, 18% above what the simple formula without the Wahl factor would say.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Look at the scatter: the same 52 N m gives 34.6 kN if the threads are slippery ($K = 0.15$) and 20.8 kN if they are dry ($K = 0.25$). The first is 93% of the yield load before counting the twisting stress from tightening, and could yield the bolt; the second might let the joint loosen under vibration. That ±25% is why critical joints are tightened by angle or by tensioner, and why a torque value on a drawing is meaningless without the lubrication it assumes.

</Narrator>

<Section id="selection" kind="human" title="Selecting standard parts from catalogues">

Professionals rarely design a bearing, a chain or a bolt. They select one, in a routine that is the same for every element:

1. **Define the duty**: loads (steady, alternating, shock), speeds, life, temperature, environment (dust, water, chemicals), space, and how the part will be assembled and maintained.
2. **Choose the type**: a deep-groove ball bearing or a tapered roller pair; a V-belt, a toothed belt or a chain; a socket-head cap screw or a hexagon bolt.
3. **Calculate the requirement** with the maker's method, which follows the international standard (ISO 281 for bearings, ISO 6336 or AGMA for gears, VDI 2230 for critical bolted joints) and adds the maker's own factors.
4. **Pick the smallest standard part** that meets it with margin, check its fit and installation requirements (shaft and housing tolerances, shoulder heights, lubrication), and record the part number in the bill of materials.
5. **Check availability and second sources.** A bearing size made by several makers in several countries is a safer choice than a special one made by one.

The makers' catalogues and online calculators, from SKF in Sweden, Schaeffler in Germany, NSK, NTN and JTEKT in Japan, Timken in the United States, and large Chinese makers such as ZWZ and HRB, give load ratings, limiting speeds, dimensions and fitting advice for tens of thousands of bearings; gear, belt, chain and spring makers publish the same for their products. Specialist calculation software (for example KISSsoft for gears and drivetrains, or the bearing makers' own tools) automates the standards' methods and is standard in industry. Learn the method by hand first, as in this lesson, so that you can tell when the software's answer is wrong.

<Analogy>

Choosing a machine element is like choosing shoes for an expedition. You do not cut and stitch your own; you work out the terrain, the distance and the load, decide on boots or sandals, find your size, and then pick the model with a little room to spare, from a maker you can buy from again.

</Analogy>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A shaft that does not yield is strong enough."** A rotating shaft is loaded in fully reversed bending a billion times; fatigue, not yield, almost always sets its diameter, as the countershaft showed (19.9 mm against 29.7 mm).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bearing rated for 30 000 hours will last 30 000 hours."** $L_{10}$ means 10% will fail sooner; the median bearing lasts several times longer, and contamination or poor lubrication can cut any of them short.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Tighter is always better for a bolt."** Too little preload lets joints loosen and bolts fatigue; too much yields the bolt or crushes the parts. The target is a specified preload, controlled as well as the joint requires.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Bigger gear teeth are always stronger."** Bending strength grows with the module, but surface durability depends mainly on the pitch diameters, face width, materials and hardness. Many gear pairs are limited by pitting, which a larger module alone does little to cure.

</Misconception>
