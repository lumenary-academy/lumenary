<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Machines that run in circles">

Almost every machine that turns heat into work, or uses work to move heat, does it in a **cycle**: the working fluid is compressed, heated, expanded and cooled, and comes back to where it started, thousands of times a minute in a car engine and continuously in a power station or a fridge. The two laws of thermodynamics from the last two lessons say what any such cycle can achieve. This lesson looks at the particular cycles that run the world: the **Otto** and **Diesel** cycles of piston engines, the **Brayton** cycle of gas turbines and jet engines, the **Rankine** cycle of steam power stations, and the **vapour-compression** cycle of refrigerators, air conditioners and heat pumps.

For each, engineers use an idealised model, simple enough to compute in a few lines, that captures how the cycle's performance depends on its main design choices: the compression ratio of an engine, the pressure ratio and turbine temperature of a gas turbine, the steam conditions of a power station, the temperatures a heat pump works between. Real machines fall short of the ideal, and you will see by how much and why. The lesson ends with a story that is still being written: the search for a refrigerant that is efficient, safe for people, and harmless to the ozone layer and the climate.

</Section>

<Section id="ideal-cycles" kind="intuition" title="Ideal cycles and what they are for">

A real engine is a mess of chemistry, turbulence, heat losses and leaks. To see the wood for the trees, gas-cycle analysis makes the **air-standard assumptions**:

- the working fluid is air, an ideal gas, circulating in a closed loop;
- combustion is replaced by heat added from an outside source, and the exhaust by heat rejected to the surroundings;
- every process is internally reversible;
- the specific heats are constant (the *cold-air-standard* assumption) at room-temperature values.

These models are not meant to predict a real engine's efficiency to the percent; they overestimate it, often by a third or more. They are meant to show **trends** — what happens when you raise the compression ratio or the turbine temperature — and to give an upper bound to measure real designs against. Two figures of merit appear throughout: the thermal efficiency $\eta = w_\text{net}/q_\text{in}$ from <LessonLink slug="second-law-and-entropy">the last lesson</LessonLink>, and the **back-work ratio** $w_\text{compressor}/w_\text{turbine}$, the fraction of the expansion work that has to be spent on compression.

Each cycle is a set of design decisions made visible. Where does heat enter: at constant volume, as in a petrol engine where a spark burns the mixture almost instantly, or at constant pressure, as in a diesel or a gas turbine where fuel burns as it is injected? How much is the gas compressed first? On a $p$–$v$ or $T$–$s$ diagram each decision shows as the shape of the loop, and the area inside it is the net work.

</Section>

<Section id="otto-diesel" kind="examples" title="Piston engines: the Otto and Diesel cycles">

A four-stroke engine draws in air (or an air–fuel mixture) as the piston moves down, compresses it as the piston moves up, burns it near the top of the stroke so that the hot gas drives the piston down in the power stroke, and pushes the exhaust out on the next upstroke. The ratio of the cylinder's largest volume to its smallest is the **compression ratio** $r = V_\text{max}/V_\text{min}$.

The **Otto cycle** models a spark-ignition (petrol) engine: (1→2) isentropic compression, (2→3) heat addition at constant volume, (3→4) isentropic expansion, (4→1) heat rejection at constant volume. The **Diesel cycle** models a compression-ignition engine, in which fuel injected into hot compressed air burns as the piston begins to move down, so heat is added at constant *pressure* (2→3), until the **cut-off ratio** $r_c = v_3/v_2$.

Applying the first law to each process, with $q_\text{in} = c_v(T_3 - T_2)$ and $q_\text{out} = c_v(T_4 - T_1)$, and using $T_2/T_1 = T_3/T_4 = r^{\gamma - 1}$ for the two isentropes, gives remarkably simple results:
$$
\eta_\text{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma - 1}}, \qquad \eta_\text{Diesel} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma - 1}} \cdot \frac{r_c^{\gamma} - 1}{\gamma\,(r_c - 1)} .
$$
The Otto efficiency depends only on the compression ratio. The Diesel formula has an extra factor, always greater than 1 when $r_c > 1$, so at the *same* compression ratio a Diesel cycle is less efficient than an Otto cycle.

```python run
# Air-standard Otto and Diesel cycles: efficiency against compression ratio.
gamma = 1.4

def otto(r):
    return 1 - r ** (1 - gamma)

def diesel(r, rc):
    """rc is the cut-off ratio: volume at the end of heat addition / volume at its start."""
    return 1 - r ** (1 - gamma) * (rc ** gamma - 1) / (gamma * (rc - 1))

print("  r     Otto   Diesel (rc = 2)   Diesel (rc = 3)")
for r in [6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22]:
    print(f"{r:3d}   {otto(r):6.1%}   {diesel(r, 2.0):12.1%}   {diesel(r, 3.0):14.1%}")

# A typical petrol engine is limited by knock to about r = 10-13; a diesel runs at 15-20.
print(f"\npetrol engine, r = 11:          {otto(11):.1%}")
print(f"diesel engine, r = 18, rc = 2:  {diesel(18, 2.0):.1%}")

# Temperatures in the ideal Otto cycle with r = 10, starting at 300 K,
# with 1800 kJ/kg of heat added at constant volume (cv = 0.718 kJ/(kg K)).
cv, r, T1, q_in = 0.718, 10, 300.0, 1800.0
T2 = T1 * r ** (gamma - 1)
T3 = T2 + q_in / cv
T4 = T3 / r ** (gamma - 1)
print(f"\nOtto, r = 10: T2 = {T2:.0f} K, T3 = {T3:.0f} K, T4 = {T4:.0f} K,"
      f" net work = {q_in - cv * (T4 - T1):.0f} kJ/kg")
```

So why are diesels more efficient in practice? Because they can use much higher compression ratios. A petrol engine compresses fuel already mixed with air, and if the mixture gets too hot it ignites on its own before the spark, a violent uncontrolled combustion called **knock**; that limits petrol engines to compression ratios of roughly 10 to 14. A diesel compresses only air, so it can go to 15 to 20, and at $r = 18$ the ideal Diesel cycle (63.2%) beats the Otto cycle at $r = 11$ (61.7%). Diesels also need no throttle valve, whose pressure drop wastes work in a petrol engine at part load.

Real engines achieve much less than these numbers. As of 2026 the best production petrol engines (many in hybrid cars, using a long-expansion variant called the Atkinson or Miller cycle) reach a peak of about 40%, heavy truck diesels about 45 to 50%, and the giant two-stroke diesels of container ships a little over 50%. The gap comes from heat lost to the cylinder walls, finite combustion speed, friction, pumping losses, and the properties of real hot gases.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Look at $T_3 = 3261$ K in the last line. With constant specific heats and no chemistry, the air-standard model overestimates the peak temperature: real combustion gases have larger specific heats when hot, and some of the energy goes into splitting molecules (dissociation), so real peaks are closer to 2500 to 2800 K. Use these models for trends and comparisons, never for sizing a part that must survive the real temperature.

<Section id="brayton" kind="computation" title="Gas turbines and jet engines: the Brayton cycle">

A **gas turbine** does continuously what a piston engine does in strokes. A compressor raises the pressure of a steady stream of air; fuel burns in it at nearly constant pressure in a combustor; the hot gas expands through a turbine, which drives the compressor and has power to spare. In a power station the spare power turns a generator. In a **turbojet** the turbine extracts only enough to drive the compressor, and the remaining energy accelerates the exhaust through a nozzle to make thrust; a **turbofan** uses a further turbine stage to drive a large fan whose bypass air provides most of the thrust, more efficiently.

The ideal (air-standard) **Brayton cycle** has isentropic compression and expansion and constant-pressure heat addition and rejection. With pressure ratio $r_p = p_2/p_1$, its efficiency is
$$
\eta_\text{Brayton} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{(\gamma - 1)/\gamma}} ,
$$
which, like Otto's, depends only on how much the air is compressed. The ideal cycle says: raise the pressure ratio. Real compressors and turbines say something more interesting, because the back-work ratio of a gas turbine is large — the compressor eats about half of the turbine's output — so every inefficiency in the two machines comes straight out of the small difference between them.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Simple Brayton cycle (gas turbine), air-standard with constant cp.
cp, gamma = 1005.0, 1.4
k = (gamma - 1) / gamma
T1 = 288.0                         # K: compressor inlet (ISO day, 15 C)

def brayton(rp, T3, eta_c=1.0, eta_t=1.0):
    """Thermal efficiency and net work (J/kg) for pressure ratio rp and turbine inlet T3 (K)."""
    T2 = T1 + T1 * (rp**k - 1) / eta_c          # compressor exit
    T4 = T3 - eta_t * T3 * (1 - rp**-k)         # turbine exit
    w_c, w_t = cp * (T2 - T1), cp * (T3 - T4)
    q_in = cp * (T3 - T2)                       # combustor
    return (w_t - w_c) / q_in, w_t - w_c, w_c / w_t

T3 = 1500.0
print(f"turbine inlet {T3:.0f} K; real case: compressor 0.86, turbine 0.90 isentropic efficiency")
print("  rp   ideal eta   real eta   real net work (kJ/kg)   back-work ratio")
for rp in [4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 30, 40]:
    eta_i, _, _ = brayton(rp, T3)
    eta_r, w_r, bwr = brayton(rp, T3, 0.86, 0.90)
    print(f"{rp:4d} {eta_i:10.1%} {eta_r:10.1%} {w_r / 1e3:15.0f} {bwr:20.2f}")

# Where are the optima? Search finely over the pressure ratio.
rps = np.linspace(2, 60, 5801)
res = np.array([brayton(rp, T3, 0.86, 0.90) for rp in rps])
print(f"\nreal cycle: most net work at rp = {rps[res[:, 1].argmax()]:.1f},"
      f" highest efficiency at rp = {rps[res[:, 0].argmax()]:.1f}")
for T3_try in [1100.0, 1300.0, 1700.0]:
    w = np.array([brayton(rp, T3_try, 0.86, 0.90)[1] for rp in rps])
    print(f"turbine inlet {T3_try:.0f} K: best net work {w.max() / 1e3:.0f} kJ/kg at rp = {rps[w.argmax()]:.1f}")
```

Three lessons are in that table. First, real efficiency is far below ideal and stops rising: with these component efficiencies it peaks near a pressure ratio of 31 and then falls, while the ideal keeps climbing. Second, the net work per kilogram of air peaks at a much lower pressure ratio, about 11.5, so a designer must choose between a compact, powerful machine and a more efficient one. Third, the turbine inlet temperature is king: raising it from 1100 K to 1700 K multiplies the best net work by two and a half, which means a smaller, lighter, cheaper engine for the same power.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Run the numbers backwards and you see why the industry fights for every degree. At 1500 K the cycle delivers 342 kJ/kg at its best; at 1700 K, 435 kJ/kg. The largest power-generation gas turbines now run with turbine inlet temperatures around 1600 °C, well above the melting point of their nickel-alloy blades, which survive only because cooling air flows through channels inside them and ceramic coatings shield their surfaces.

</Narrator>

Engineers improve the simple cycle in several ways: a **regenerator** uses the hot turbine exhaust to preheat the compressed air; **intercooling** between compressor stages reduces compression work, as the polytropic table in <LessonLink slug="first-law-of-thermodynamics">the first law</LessonLink> showed; **reheat** between turbine stages raises turbine work. The most important improvement is the **combined cycle**: the gas turbine's exhaust, still at 550 to 650 °C, raises steam for a Rankine cycle, and the pair reaches 60 to 64% efficiency. The blades themselves are a story in materials, told in Lumenary Materials' [metals and alloys](/en/materials/metals-and-alloys/); jet propulsion continues in <LessonLink slug="vehicles-and-propulsion">vehicles and propulsion</LessonLink>.

The cycles carry their inventors' names. Nikolaus Otto built the first successful four-stroke engine in Cologne in 1876, on a cycle that Alphonse Beau de Rochas had described in a French patent in 1862. Rudolf Diesel patented compression ignition in 1892 and ran his first working engine in Augsburg in 1897. George Brayton, an American engineer, patented a constant-pressure piston engine in 1872; his cycle found its true home decades later in turbines, when Frank Whittle in Britain and Hans von Ohain in Germany independently built the first jet engines in the late 1930s, and von Ohain's powered the first jet aircraft flight in 1939. William Rankine of Glasgow set out the theory of the steam cycle in his *Manual of the Steam Engine* of 1859.

<Section id="rankine" kind="computation" title="Steam power: the Rankine cycle in outline">

Most of the world's electricity from coal, nuclear fission, concentrated solar power, geothermal heat and biomass is made by boiling water. The ideal **Rankine cycle** has four steps: (1→2) a pump raises the pressure of liquid water; (2→3) a boiler or steam generator heats it, boils it and usually superheats it at constant pressure; (3→4) the steam expands through a turbine; (4→1) a condenser turns the exhaust steam back into liquid by rejecting heat to cooling water or air.

Its great advantage over a gas cycle is the pump: compressing a liquid takes very little work, because the specific volume of liquid water is about a thousandth of that of steam, and the pump work $v\,\Delta p$ is tiny. The back-work ratio of a Rankine cycle is under 1%, against roughly 50% for a gas turbine. Steam is not an ideal gas, so the cycle is computed from property data; the values below come from the NIST Chemistry WebBook.

```python run
# A simple Rankine cycle: boiler at 8 MPa and 500 C, condenser at 10 kPa.
# Water properties from the NIST Chemistry WebBook (IAPWS-95), rounded.
# Units: kJ/kg, kJ/(kg K), m^3/kg, kPa.
h_f, s_f, v_f = 191.81, 0.6492, 0.0010103     # saturated liquid at 10 kPa (45.8 C)
h_g, s_g = 2583.86, 8.1488                    # saturated vapour at 10 kPa
h3, s3 = 3399.49, 6.7266                      # superheated steam at 8 MPa, 500 C
p_low, p_high = 10.0, 8000.0
T_low = 45.81 + 273.15                        # K: condensing temperature

def rankine(eta_t=1.0, eta_p=1.0):
    h1 = h_f                                  # 1: liquid leaving the condenser
    w_p = v_f * (p_high - p_low) / eta_p      # pump work, incompressible liquid
    h2 = h1 + w_p                             # 2: liquid entering the boiler
    x4s = (s3 - s_f) / (s_g - s_f)            # 4s: wet steam after isentropic expansion
    h4s = h_f + x4s * (h_g - h_f)
    h4 = h3 - eta_t * (h3 - h4s)              # 4: actual turbine exit
    x4 = (h4 - h_f) / (h_g - h_f)
    w_t, q_in = h3 - h4, h3 - h2
    return w_t, w_p, q_in, x4, (w_t - w_p) / q_in

for label, et, ep in [("ideal", 1.0, 1.0), ("turbine 0.87, pump 0.85", 0.87, 0.85)]:
    w_t, w_p, q_in, x4, eta = rankine(et, ep)
    print(f"{label:24} turbine {w_t:6.0f}  pump {w_p:4.1f}  heat in {q_in:6.0f} kJ/kg;"
          f"  exit quality {x4:.3f};  efficiency {eta:.1%}")

w_t, w_p, q_in, x4, eta = rankine()
T_mean = q_in / (s3 - s_f)                    # mean temperature at which heat is received
print(f"\nmean temperature of heat addition: {T_mean:.0f} K ({T_mean - 273.15:.0f} C)")
print(f"Carnot at that mean temperature: {1 - T_low / T_mean:.1%};"
      f"  Carnot between 500 C and 45.8 C: {1 - T_low / 773.15:.1%}")
w_t, w_p, *_ = rankine(0.87, 0.85)
print(f"steam flow for 500 MW net (real cycle): {500e3 / (w_t - w_p):.0f} kg/s")
```

The pump needs 8 kJ/kg against the turbine's 1269 kJ/kg. The ideal cycle reaches 39.4%, and 34.2% with realistic turbine and pump efficiencies; a 500 MW plant like this would circulate nearly half a tonne of steam every second.

The middle lines explain why this cycle cannot reach the 58.7% Carnot efficiency between its highest and lowest temperatures. Much of the heat is added to *liquid* water warming from 46 °C towards its boiling point at 295 °C (the saturation temperature at 8 MPa), so the **mean temperature** at which heat enters is only 526 K (253 °C). A Carnot engine working from that mean temperature has exactly the Rankine cycle's efficiency, 39.4%. Every improvement to the Rankine cycle is a way of raising that mean temperature or lowering the condensing one:

- **higher boiler pressure and temperature** — modern ultra-supercritical coal plants run above the critical pressure, at around 25 to 30 MPa and 600 °C or more;
- **reheat** — sending partly expanded steam back to the boiler, which also keeps the turbine exhaust drier (the 0.81 quality above would erode the last blades);
- **regeneration** — preheating the feedwater with steam bled from the turbine, so less heat is added at low temperature;
- **a lower condenser pressure** — limited by the temperature of the cooling water.

Nuclear plants work with saturated steam at modest pressure, because of the reactor's temperature limits, and reach 33 to 37%. Low-temperature sources such as geothermal brine or industrial waste heat use the **organic Rankine cycle**, with a fluid that boils at a lower temperature than water. How whole power stations are built around these cycles is the subject of <LessonLink slug="power-plants-and-energy-conversion">power plants and energy conversion</LessonLink>.

</Section>

The refrigerant leaves the evaporator as low-pressure vapour (1). The compressor raises its pressure until its saturation temperature is above that of the warm side (2), so that in the condenser it can give up heat and condense (3). The expansion valve throttles the liquid to the low pressure, which, as you saw in <LessonLink slug="first-law-of-thermodynamics">the first law</LessonLink>, keeps the enthalpy constant but flashes part of it to vapour and drops its temperature below that of the cold side (4). In the evaporator the cold mixture boils, absorbing heat. Applying the steady-flow energy equation to each component:
$$
q_L = h_1 - h_4, \qquad w = h_2 - h_1, \qquad q_H = h_2 - h_3, \qquad h_4 = h_3 ,
$$
so $\text{COP}_R = q_L/w$ and $\text{COP}_{HP} = q_H/w$.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why waste the expansion work in a valve, when the steam cycle recovers it with a turbine? Because here it is tiny: expanding a liquid gives back about as little work as pumping one costs, and a turbine small enough for a fridge would cost more, wear out and leak. A valve or a thin capillary tube is cheap, has no moving parts and lasts for decades. Large chillers and some CO₂ systems, where the expansion work is bigger, do sometimes recover it with an expander or an ejector.

</Narrator>

The program computes an air-to-water heat pump using propane (refrigerant R-290), evaporating at −5 °C to take heat from outdoor air at a few degrees above freezing, and condensing at 45 °C to supply water for underfloor heating or large radiators at around 40 °C. The property values are from the NIST Chemistry WebBook; the compressor exit is found by interpolating in the superheated-vapour data at the entropy of the inlet.

```python run
import numpy as np

# An air-to-water heat pump using propane (R-290): evaporating at -5 C, condensing at 45 C.
# Properties from the NIST Chemistry WebBook, IIR reference state (h = 200 kJ/kg and
# s = 1 kJ/(kg K) for saturated liquid at 0 C). Units: kJ/kg, kJ/(kg K), kPa.
p_evap, h1, s1 = 406.0, 569.30, 2.37813       # 1: saturated vapour at -5 C
p_cond, h3 = 1534.3, 321.79                   # 3: saturated liquid at 45 C
# superheated vapour at the condensing pressure, 1534.3 kPa
T_sup = np.array([45.0, 50.0, 55.0, 60.0, 65.0])               # C
h_sup = np.array([618.12, 629.80, 641.22, 652.46, 663.58])
s_sup = np.array([2.33602, 2.37246, 2.40752, 2.44152, 2.47465])

h2s = np.interp(s1, s_sup, h_sup)             # 2s: same entropy as 1, at p_cond
T2s = np.interp(s1, s_sup, T_sup)
h4 = h3                                       # 4: throttling keeps h constant
q_L = h1 - h4                                 # heat taken from the outdoor air
K = 273.15
cop_carnot = (45 + K) / (45 - (-5))
print(f"pressure ratio {p_cond / p_evap:.2f}; isentropic discharge {T2s:.1f} C")
print(f"Carnot COP_HP between -5 C and 45 C: {cop_carnot:.2f}\n")
print(" compressor eta   work (kJ/kg)   heat out (kJ/kg)   COP_HP   COP_R")
for eta in [1.0, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6]:
    w = (h2s - h1) / eta
    q_H = q_L + w                             # first law for the whole loop
    print(f"{eta:12.1f} {w:14.1f} {q_H:16.1f} {q_H / w:10.2f} {q_L / w:7.2f}")

# Size it: 8 kW of heat to the house with a 0.7-efficient compressor.
w = (h2s - h1) / 0.7
m_dot = 8.0 / (q_L + w)                       # kg/s
print(f"\nfor 8 kW of heat: {m_dot * 1000:.1f} g/s of propane, compressor power {m_dot * w:.2f} kW,"
      f" {m_dot * q_L:.2f} kW drawn from the outdoor air")
```

Even with an ideal compressor the cycle's COP of 4.97 is well short of the Carnot value of 6.36, because throttling is irreversible and because the vapour leaves the compressor superheated, above the condensing temperature. With a realistic overall compressor efficiency of about 0.7 the COP is 3.78: for 2.12 kW of electricity, the house receives 8 kW of heat, 5.88 kW of it taken from cold outdoor air. That is the second-law argument of <LessonLink slug="second-law-and-entropy">the last lesson</LessonLink> turned into hardware.

Manufacturers rate heat pumps at standard conditions (in Europe under EN 14511, for example at 7 °C outdoor air and 35 °C water) and over a whole heating season (the seasonal coefficient of performance, SCOP, under EN 14825); in the United States, AHRI standards play the same role. A heat pump's COP falls as the temperature lift grows, which is why they work best with low-temperature underfloor heating and well-insulated buildings, the subject of <LessonLink slug="heating-cooling-and-buildings">heating, cooling and buildings</LessonLink>.

<Section id="refrigerants" kind="history" title="Refrigerants: from toxic gases to the ozone hole to low-GWP fluids">

A good refrigerant boils at a convenient temperature at a moderate pressure, carries a lot of heat per kilogram, is chemically stable, and does not hurt people or the planet when it escapes. No fluid is perfect, and the history of refrigeration is a history of trading one problem for another.

**The first refrigerants.** Jacob Perkins patented a vapour-compression machine in London in 1834, using ether. From the 1870s Carl von Linde in Germany built reliable ammonia compression machines for breweries and cold stores, and in the 1850s Peter von Rittinger in Austria had already built a heat pump to evaporate brine at a salt works. Early domestic refrigerators used ammonia, sulfur dioxide or methyl chloride: efficient fluids, but toxic, and leaks in homes in the 1920s caused deaths.

**CFCs.** In 1928, a team at General Motors' Frigidaire division led by Thomas Midgley Jr. developed dichlorodifluoromethane (CFC-12, R-12): non-toxic, non-flammable and stable. Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) made refrigerators and later air conditioning safe and common worldwide. Their stability turned out to be the problem. In 1974 Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland showed that CFCs survive long enough to drift into the stratosphere, where ultraviolet light breaks them apart and the chlorine released destroys ozone catalytically. In 1985 Joseph Farman, Brian Gardiner and Jonathan Shanklin of the British Antarctic Survey reported a dramatic seasonal loss of ozone over Antarctica: the ozone hole. The chemistry is told in Lumenary Chemistry's [environmental chemistry](/en/chemistry/environmental-chemistry/).

**The Montreal Protocol.** The world responded remarkably quickly. The Vienna Convention (1985) and the Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs, and then the transitional HCFCs such as R-22, in developed countries first; in 2009 the Protocol became the first United Nations treaty ratified by every member state. Molina, Rowland and Paul Crutzen shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The ozone layer is recovering: the 2022 scientific assessment for the Protocol expected it to return to its 1980 state around 2040 over most of the world and in the 2060s over Antarctica.

**HFCs and climate.** The replacements, hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) such as R-134a in car air conditioners and fridges and the blend R-410A in air conditioners, contain no chlorine and do not harm ozone. But they are powerful greenhouse gases. Their **global warming potential** (GWP), the warming caused by a kilogram over 100 years relative to a kilogram of CO₂, is roughly 1300 to 1500 for R-134a and about 2000 for R-410A (the exact figures differ a little between IPCC assessments). The Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol, agreed in 2016 and in force since 2019, commits countries to cut HFC use by 80 to 85% by the late 2040s, on schedules that differ between developed and developing countries. The European Union's F-gas Regulation (revised in 2024) and the United States' AIM Act of 2020 implement and in places go beyond it.

**The fluids of 2026.** The main options now are:

| Refrigerant | Type | GWP (100 yr) | Safety class | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R-600a (isobutane) | hydrocarbon | single digits | A3 (highly flammable) | most new domestic fridges and freezers worldwide |
| R-290 (propane) | hydrocarbon | single digits | A3 | many new heat pumps, small commercial units |
| R-744 (CO₂) | natural | 1 | A1 | supermarket refrigeration, some heat pumps; very high pressures |
| R-717 (ammonia) | natural | 0 | B2L (toxic, mildly flammable) | large industrial plants, cold stores |
| R-1234yf | hydrofluoroolefin (HFO) | below 1 to single digits | A2L (mildly flammable) | most new car air conditioners in Europe, North America and Japan |
| R-32 | HFC | about 700 | A2L | many split air conditioners, as a lower-GWP step |

The safety classes are those of ASHRAE Standard 34 and ISO 817: A means lower toxicity and B higher; 1 means no flame propagation, 2L lower flammability, and 3 high flammability. Every choice is a trade-off. Hydrocarbons are efficient and climate-friendly but flammable, so appliance standards limit how much a unit may contain and how it must be built. CO₂ is non-flammable and harmless to the climate in these quantities, but works at pressures above 100 bar in some systems. Ammonia is excellent thermodynamically and has been used for 150 years, but it is toxic and confined to industrial plants run by trained staff. HFOs break down quickly in the atmosphere, but one breakdown product, trifluoroacetic acid, is very persistent in water, and as of 2026 European regulators are weighing a broad restriction on PFAS ("forever chemicals") that may affect some fluorinated refrigerants.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Every refrigerant circuit is a pressure system, and many modern refrigerants are flammable or toxic; carbon dioxide systems run at pressures that can burst an unsuitable pipe. That is why, in most countries, only certified technicians may open, fill or recover a refrigerant circuit (in the EU under the F-gas rules, in the US under the EPA's Section 608 programme), why venting refrigerant is illegal in many places, and why nobody should take a fridge or air conditioner apart at home. The safe way to explore these cycles is on paper and in code, like this lesson.

</Narrator>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Cycles in professional practice">

- **Engines** are designed with one-dimensional engine simulation (commercial packages such as GT-SUITE and Ricardo WAVE), detailed combustion chemistry (the open-source Cantera, among others) and in-cylinder computational fluid dynamics (OpenFOAM, CONVERGE and others), all checked on test beds. Efficiency is reported as brake thermal efficiency, measured at the crankshaft, at specified test conditions.
- **Gas turbines and jet engines** are designed around cycle models like the one above, with real-gas properties, cooling flows and component maps; NASA has released an open-source cycle-modelling library, pyCycle, alongside long-established commercial tools. Acceptance tests follow codes such as ASME PTC 22 and ISO 2314.
- **Steam plants** are modelled with heat-balance software built on IAPWS-IF97 steam properties, and tested under ASME PTC 6 or IEC 60953.
- **Refrigeration and heat pumps** are designed with property libraries such as NIST REFPROP and CoolProp, and their ratings are tested at accredited laboratories against EN 14511 and EN 14825, AHRI standards or ISO 5151. You will meet the cooling side again, at its most demanding, in <LessonLink slug="thermal-management-for-computing">thermal management for computing</LessonLink>.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Diesel engines are more efficient because the Diesel cycle is more efficient."** At the same compression ratio the ideal Otto cycle is *more* efficient. Diesels win because they can use much higher compression ratios without knock, and need no throttle.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Higher pressure ratio is always better for a gas turbine."** Only in the ideal cycle. With real compressors and turbines, efficiency peaks and then falls, and the net work per kilogram of air peaks at a lower ratio still. Designers choose the ratio that suits the job: compact and powerful, or efficient.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Heat pumps don't work in cold weather."** They work, with a lower COP, because the temperature lift is larger. Cold-climate heat pumps heat homes through Nordic and Canadian winters; at very low outdoor temperatures some use a backup heater for the coldest hours.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A fridge needs its refrigerant topped up from time to time, like fuel."** The refrigerant circulates in a sealed loop and is not used up. If a system is short of refrigerant, it has a leak, which must be found and repaired by a certified technician.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Solving the ozone hole solved the refrigerant problem."** The fluids that replaced CFCs protect the ozone layer but many are strong greenhouse gases. The ozone problem and the climate problem have different chemistry, and each needed its own agreement: Montreal for ozone, Kigali for HFCs.

</Misconception>
