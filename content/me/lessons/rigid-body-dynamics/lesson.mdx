<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="When size and rotation matter">

In <LessonLink slug="particle-dynamics">Dynamics of particles</LessonLink> a lorry was a point. That was fine for how far it takes to stop, but useless for the questions that keep vehicle engineers busy: how much of the load moves onto the front tyres when it brakes, whether it lifts a wheel on a bend, how hard the brakes must grip to stop the spinning wheels as well as the lorry. Once the turning of a body matters, it is a **rigid body**: a body whose points keep their distances from each other, so that its whole motion is described by the motion of one point and one angle.

Rigid bodies are everywhere in machines: wheels and gears, crankshafts and connecting rods, flywheels, the links of a robot arm, the rotor of a wind turbine, a door swinging shut. This lesson stays in the plane, where a body has three degrees of freedom ($x$, $y$ and an angle $\theta$), which covers most machine elements and vehicles. Two new ingredients appear. The **moment of inertia** says how hard a body is to spin up, the way mass says how hard it is to push. And the second law grows a twin: forces set the acceleration of the centre of mass, moments set the angular acceleration. At the end, a brief excursion into three dimensions explains why a spinning wheel seems to defy gravity.

</Section>

<Section id="kinematics" kind="definitions" title="Planar kinematics of a rigid body">

A rigid body in the plane can **translate** (every line in it keeps its direction), **rotate about a fixed axis** (a wheel on a fixed shaft), or do both at once in **general plane motion** (a rolling wheel, a connecting rod). Every line drawn on a rigid body turns through the same angle in the same time, so the body has a single angular velocity $\omega = \dot\theta$ and angular acceleration $\alpha = \ddot\theta$.

<Definition term="relative velocity and acceleration in a rigid body">

For two points A and B of a rigid body in plane motion, with $\mathbf r_{B/A}$ the vector from A to B, $\boldsymbol\omega = \omega\,\mathbf k$ and $\boldsymbol\alpha = \alpha\,\mathbf k$ perpendicular to the plane,
$$
\mathbf v_B = \mathbf v_A + \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf r_{B/A}, \qquad
\mathbf a_B = \mathbf a_A + \boldsymbol\alpha \times \mathbf r_{B/A} - \omega^2\,\mathbf r_{B/A}.
$$
Seen from A, B simply moves on a circle round it: $\omega r$ across the line AB, plus, for acceleration, $\alpha r$ across it and $\omega^2 r$ towards A.

</Definition>

For a body rotating about a fixed axis O, set A = O: a point at radius $r$ has speed $\omega r$, tangential acceleration $\alpha r$ and centripetal acceleration $\omega^2 r$. For general plane motion there is, at each instant, one point of the body (or of its imaginary extension) whose velocity is zero, the **instantaneous centre of zero velocity**; at that instant the body is turning about it, and every point's velocity is $\omega$ times its distance from it, at right angles to the line joining them.

<Definition term="rolling without slipping">

A wheel of radius $R$ rolls without slipping on a fixed surface when its contact point has zero velocity. Then the centre moves at $v_G = \omega R$, and if the surface is straight, $a_G = \alpha R$.

</Definition>

<Example title="Points on a bicycle wheel">

A bicycle with 0.34 m wheels rides at 6.0 m/s. Rolling without slipping, each wheel turns at $\omega = v/R = 17.6$ rad/s. The top of the tyre moves at $2v = 12$ m/s, the point touching the road is at rest, and the valve, when it passes level with the hub at the front, moves at $\sqrt 2\,v = 8.5$ m/s, forwards and downwards. That is why the spokes at the top of a moving wheel blur in a photograph while those at the bottom look sharp.

</Example>

<Section id="inertia" kind="definitions" title="Moments of inertia">

<Definition term="moment of inertia, radius of gyration">

The **moment of inertia** of a body about an axis is $I = \int r^2\,\mathrm dm$, where $r$ is the distance of each element of mass $\mathrm dm$ from the axis, in kg m². The **radius of gyration** $k$ is defined by $I = mk^2$: the radius at which all the mass, concentrated in a ring, would have the same moment of inertia.

</Definition>

Because of the $r^2$, mass far from the axis counts for much more than mass near it. For uniform bodies about an axis through the centre of mass G, perpendicular to the plane of motion, the standard results are: a thin rod of length $L$, $mL^2/12$; a solid disc or cylinder of radius $R$, $mR^2/2$; a thin ring or pipe, $mR^2$; a rectangular plate $a \times b$, $m(a^2 + b^2)/12$; a solid sphere about a diameter, $\tfrac25 mR^2$; a thin spherical shell, $\tfrac23 mR^2$. These come from the integral by the methods of <TopicLink id="math.analysis.multivariable-calculus">multivariable calculus</TopicLink>, and CAD software computes the same integral for any modelled part. Most real parts are assembled from simpler ones, which is where the next theorem earns its keep.

<Theorem name="Parallel-axis theorem">

If $I_G$ is the moment of inertia of a body of mass $m$ about an axis through its centre of mass G, its moment of inertia about any parallel axis a distance $d$ away is $I = I_G + md^2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Put G at the origin and the new axis through the point $(d_x, d_y)$, with $d^2 = d_x^2 + d_y^2$. Then
$I = \int\big[(x - d_x)^2 + (y - d_y)^2\big]\,\mathrm dm = \int (x^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm dm - 2d_x\!\int x\,\mathrm dm - 2d_y\!\int y\,\mathrm dm + d^2\!\int \mathrm dm$.
The first integral is $I_G$, the middle two vanish because G is the centre of mass, and the last is $md^2$.

</Proof>

So $I_G$ is the *smallest* moment of inertia about any axis in a given direction. A thin rod about one end has $mL^2/12 + m(L/2)^2 = mL^2/3$, four times its value about the middle.

The program below computes moments of inertia the way a computer does for an arbitrary flat shape: cover it with a fine grid, keep the cells inside, and add up $r^2\,\mathrm dm$. It checks the method on three shapes with known answers, then applies it to a steel flywheel with four lightening holes, and compares the result with the parallel-axis theorem: the full disc, minus four small discs, each about its own centre plus $md^2$.

```python run
import numpy as np

def lamina(inside, box, mass, n=1000):
    """Moment of inertia of a flat plate of uniform density about the axis perpendicular to it
    through its centre of mass, by the midpoint rule on an n x n grid over box = (x0, x1, y0, y1)."""
    x0, x1, y0, y1 = box
    hx, hy = (x1 - x0) / n, (y1 - y0) / n
    x, y = np.meshgrid(x0 + hx * (np.arange(n) + 0.5), y0 + hy * (np.arange(n) + 0.5))
    w = inside(x, y)                                   # True inside the shape
    area = w.sum() * hx * hy
    rho = mass / area                                  # mass per unit area (kg/m^2)
    xc, yc = x[w].mean(), y[w].mean()                  # centre of mass
    I_G = rho * hx * hy * ((x[w] - xc) ** 2 + (y[w] - yc) ** 2).sum()
    return I_G, (xc, yc)

# Check the method on shapes with known answers (masses in kg, lengths in m).
m, R, a, b = 10.0, 0.20, 0.60, 0.10
disc, _ = lamina(lambda x, y: x**2 + y**2 <= R**2, (-R, R, -R, R), m)
rect, _ = lamina(lambda x, y: np.ones_like(x, bool), (-a / 2, a / 2, -b / 2, b / 2), m)
ring, _ = lamina(lambda x, y: (x**2 + y**2 <= R**2) & (x**2 + y**2 >= 0.15**2), (-R, R, -R, R), m)
print("shape                  numerical   formula")
print(f"disc, m R^2/2          {disc:9.5f}   {m * R**2 / 2:9.5f}")
print(f"plate, m(a^2+b^2)/12   {rect:9.5f}   {m * (a**2 + b**2) / 12:9.5f}")
print(f"ring, m(Ri^2+Ro^2)/2   {ring:9.5f}   {m * (0.15**2 + R**2) / 2:9.5f}")

# A steel flywheel: a 0.40 m disc, 30 mm thick, with four 80 mm lightening holes on a 0.12 m circle.
steel, t = 7850.0, 0.030                             # density (kg/m^3), thickness (m)
R, r, c = 0.20, 0.040, 0.12
holes = [(c * np.cos(q), c * np.sin(q)) for q in np.radians([0, 90, 180, 270])]
def flywheel(x, y):
    keep = x**2 + y**2 <= R**2
    for hx, hy in holes:
        keep &= (x - hx) ** 2 + (y - hy) ** 2 > r**2
    return keep
m_disc, m_hole = steel * t * np.pi * R**2, steel * t * np.pi * r**2
m_fly = m_disc - 4 * m_hole
I_num, G = lamina(flywheel, (-R, R, -R, R), m_fly, n=2000)
# By the parallel-axis theorem: the full disc minus four holes, each about its own centre plus m d^2.
I_pat = m_disc * R**2 / 2 - 4 * (m_hole * r**2 / 2 + m_hole * c**2)
print(f"\nflywheel mass {m_fly:.2f} kg (full disc {m_disc:.2f} kg); centre of mass at "
      f"({abs(G[0]):.4f}, {abs(G[1]):.4f}) m")
print(f"I about its axis: numerical {I_num:.4f} kg m^2, parallel-axis theorem {I_pat:.4f} kg m^2")
print(f"holes remove {100 * 4 * m_hole / m_disc:.1f}% of the mass "
      f"but only {100 * (1 - I_pat / (m_disc * R**2 / 2)):.1f}% of the moment of inertia")
```

The grid method agrees with the formulas to four or five figures, and the flywheel's two answers agree: 0.5199 kg m². Removing material at less than the outer radius saves proportionally more mass than inertia, here 16.0 per cent of the mass for 12.2 per cent of the inertia, and holes nearer the hub would do better still.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Put numbers on that ratio. The flywheel's 24.86 kg give 0.52 kg m²; the same mass formed into a thin ring at the 0.20 m rim would give $mR^2 = 0.99$ kg m², nearly twice as much, and so nearly twice the stored energy $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$ at the same speed. That is why the spoked cast-iron wheel of an old mill engine and the carbon-fibre rim of a modern storage flywheel both put their material at the rim.

</Narrator>

<Section id="newton-euler" kind="definitions" title="The Newton–Euler equations in the plane">

Treat the body as a cloud of particles, apply Newton's second law to each, and add. The internal forces cancel in pairs, and two equations survive.

<Theorem name="Newton–Euler equations for plane motion">

For a rigid body of mass $m$ moving in a plane that is a plane of symmetry of the body (or for a thin plate moving in its own plane), with centre of mass G,
$$
\sum \mathbf F = m\,\mathbf a_G, \qquad \sum M_G = I_G\,\alpha ,
$$
where $\sum M_G$ is the total moment of the external forces about G. If the body rotates about a fixed axis through O, the second equation may be replaced by $\sum M_O = I_O\,\alpha$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Summing $m_i\mathbf a_i = \mathbf F_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_j \mathbf F_{ij}$ over all particles, the internal forces cancel by Newton's third law, leaving $\sum\mathbf F = \sum m_i\mathbf a_i = m\mathbf a_G$ by the definition of G. For moments about G, write $\mathbf r_i$ from G to particle $i$. Then $\sum M_G = \sum \mathbf r_i \times m_i\mathbf a_i$ (the internal forces act along the lines joining particles, so their moments cancel too). Using the relative-acceleration formula, $\mathbf a_i = \mathbf a_G + \boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf r_i - \omega^2\mathbf r_i$: the $\mathbf a_G$ term gives $(\sum m_i\mathbf r_i)\times\mathbf a_G = \mathbf 0$, the $\omega^2$ term gives $\mathbf r_i\times\mathbf r_i = \mathbf 0$, and the $\alpha$ term gives $\sum m_i r_i^2\,\alpha\,\mathbf k = I_G\,\alpha\,\mathbf k$. For a fixed axis, repeat with O in place of G, where $\mathbf a_O = \mathbf 0$.

</Proof>

The working method is the one from particles, with one more arrow. Draw the free-body diagram; beside it, draw the **kinetic diagram**, with the vector $m\mathbf a_G$ at G and the couple $I_G\alpha$; and require the two diagrams to have the same resultant and the same moment about any point you like.

<Narrator who="gideon">

"About any point you like" applies to the two *diagrams*, not to the shortcut $\sum M_P = I_P\alpha$. In general $\sum M_P = I_G\alpha + (\mathbf r_{G/P}\times m\mathbf a_G)\cdot\mathbf k$. That reduces to $I_P\alpha$ when P is G or a fixed pivot, and to $I_G\alpha$ when the line from P to G is parallel to $\mathbf a_G$; about other points it is neither. Taking $\sum M = I\alpha$ about an arbitrary point, such as the contact point of an *accelerating* car, is the most common error in rigid-body dynamics.

</Narrator>

<Example title="Load transfer when a car brakes">

A 1500 kg car has a wheelbase of 2.70 m, with G 1.20 m behind the front axle, 1.50 m ahead of the rear axle and 0.55 m above the road. It brakes on all four wheels at $0.8g = 7.85$ m/s². Find the normal forces on the front and rear axles.

The car translates without rotating, so $\alpha = 0$ and the kinetic diagram is just $m a_G$ at G, pointing backwards. The braking forces act backwards at road level, 0.55 m below G. Take moments about the rear contact point, where the kinetic diagram's moment is $m a_G h$ (the line of $m\mathbf a_G$ passes 0.55 m above the road):
$$
N_f L - mg\,b_r = m a_G h \;\Rightarrow\; N_f = \frac{m(g\,b_r + a_G h)}{L} = \frac{1500(9.81 \times 1.50 + 7.85 \times 0.55)}{2.70} = 10.6 \text{ kN},
$$
and $N_r = mg - N_f = 4.1$ kN. At rest the front axle carries $mg\,b_r/L = 8.2$ kN, 56 per cent of the weight; braking hard it carries 72 per cent. That **load transfer** is why front brakes are bigger than rear ones, why brake systems apportion pressure front to rear, and, at the extreme, why a motorcycle braking hard can lift its rear wheel.

</Example>

<Example title="A rod released from rest">

A uniform rod of mass 6.0 kg and length 1.5 m is pinned at one end and held horizontal, then released. At the instant of release $\omega = 0$, and for rotation about the fixed pin O, $\sum M_O = I_O\alpha$ with $I_O = mL^2/3$:
$$
mg\,\frac L2 = \frac{mL^2}{3}\,\alpha \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha = \frac{3g}{2L} = 9.81 \text{ rad/s}^2 .
$$
G then accelerates downwards at $\alpha L/2 = 7.36$ m/s², three-quarters of $g$, and vertical force balance on the rod, $mg - R = m a_G$, gives a pin force of only $R = mg/4 = 14.7$ N. A quarter of the weight: the pin is relieved because the rod is partly falling. The same calculation, with real masses, sizes the hinges, brakes and counterweights of anything that swings: a barrier arm, a bascule bridge leaf, a robot link that loses power.

</Example>

<Section id="energy" kind="definitions" title="Energy methods for rigid bodies">

The work–energy principle from particles carries over once kinetic energy includes rotation.

<Definition term="kinetic energy of a rigid body in plane motion">

$$
T = \tfrac12 m v_G^2 + \tfrac12 I_G\,\omega^2 ,
$$
the energy of the centre of mass moving with the whole mass, plus the energy of rotation about it. For rotation about a fixed axis O this equals $\tfrac12 I_O\,\omega^2$. A couple $M$ turning the body through an angle $\mathrm d\theta$ does work $M\,\mathrm d\theta$, and delivers power $P = M\omega$.

</Definition>

Two facts make energy methods especially handy for rolling. The contact force on a wheel rolling without slipping acts at a point with zero velocity, so static friction there does **no work**: it moves energy between translation and rotation but removes none. And a wheel's speed then fixes its spin, so one energy equation gives one unknown speed.

<Example title="Energy in a flywheel">

A solid steel disc of 500 kg and radius 0.50 m spins at 6000 rpm, or $\omega = 628$ rad/s. Its moment of inertia is $\tfrac12 mR^2 = 62.5$ kg m², so it stores $\tfrac12 I\omega^2 = 12.3$ MJ, about 3.4 kWh. Because energy goes as $\omega^2$, slowing it to half speed releases three-quarters of that, 9.3 MJ. Its rim moves at 314 m/s, and the hoop stress in a spinning disc grows with the square of the rim speed; at this speed it is around 320 MPa, a large fraction of what a good steel can safely carry. Stress, not size, limits a flywheel's energy per kilogram, which is why storage flywheels use high-strength composite rims and spin in vacuum inside a strong containment.

</Example>

Write $I_G = k\,mr^2$, where the number $k$ depends only on the shape: $\tfrac25$ for a solid sphere, $\tfrac12$ for a solid cylinder, $\tfrac23$ for a hollow sphere, 1 for a thin pipe. Down the slope, $mg\sin\theta - f = ma_G$. About G, $fr = I_G\alpha = k\,mr^2\,(a_G/r)$, so $f = k\,m a_G$. Eliminating $f$,
$$
a_G = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + k}, \qquad f = \frac{k}{1 + k}\,mg\sin\theta .
$$
Rolling needs $f \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\cos\theta$, that is $\mu_s \ge \tan\theta\,k/(1 + k)$; on a steeper slope the body slides and spins at once. Energy gives the speed at the foot directly: $mgh = \tfrac12 m v^2 (1 + k)$.

```python run
import numpy as np

g = 9.81
theta = np.radians(10.0)          # slope of the ramp
L = 2.0                           # length of the ramp (m)
h = L * np.sin(theta)

# k = I_G / (m r^2): how much of the body's mass is far from its axis.
shapes = [("block, sliding, no friction", 0.0), ("solid sphere", 2 / 5), ("solid cylinder", 1 / 2),
          ("hollow sphere", 2 / 3), ("thin-walled pipe", 1.0)]

print("body                         a (m/s2)  time (s)  speed at foot  min friction coeff.")
for name, k in shapes:
    a = g * np.sin(theta) / (1 + k)             # Newton-Euler for rolling without slipping
    t = np.sqrt(2 * L / a)
    v = np.sqrt(2 * g * h / (1 + k))            # energy: m g h = (1/2) m v^2 (1 + k)
    mu = np.tan(theta) * k / (1 + k)            # friction needed: f = k m a <= mu m g cos(theta)
    print(f"{name:28s} {a:8.3f} {t:9.3f} {v:10.3f} m/s   {mu:8.3f}")
    assert np.isclose(v, a * t)                 # the two routes agree

# The steepest slope each body can roll down without slipping, if the static friction coefficient is 0.25.
mu_s = 0.25
print("\nsteepest slope for rolling with mu_s = 0.25:",
      ", ".join(f"{name.split(',')[0]} {np.degrees(np.arctan(mu_s * (1 + k) / k)):.1f} deg"
                for name, k in shapes[1:]))
```

On a 2 m ramp at 10°, a frictionless sliding block arrives first, in 1.53 s, then the solid sphere (1.81 s), the solid cylinder, the hollow sphere and last the pipe (2.17 s). The friction needed is small on a gentle slope, but with $\mu_s = 0.25$ a pipe starts to skid on slopes steeper than 26.6°, while a solid sphere keeps rolling up to 41.2°.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Look at what is *not* in the table: mass and radius. A marble and a bowling ball, a soup tin and a road roller, arrive together if their shapes match, because both the driving moment and the inertia scale the same way. Only $k$, the way mass is spread relative to the size, decides the race: put the mass near the hub for a wheel that speeds up and slows down readily, and at the rim for one that should keep turning.

</Narrator>

A body that is not rolling to begin with must get there through sliding friction, and this is where step-by-step integration of the Newton–Euler equations earns its place. A snooker cue ball struck through its centre leaves the tip sliding, with no spin. Kinetic friction at the cloth pushes backwards on the contact point: it slows the centre ($m\dot v = -\mu_k mg$) and spins the ball up ($I_G\dot\omega = \mu_k mg\,R$), until the contact point stops slipping, $v = R\omega$, and the ball rolls. Strike above the centre and the ball starts with topspin; below, with backspin.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A snooker ball on the cloth: it slides until its contact point stops slipping, then rolls.
g, mu = 9.81, 0.20                # kinetic friction coefficient, ball on cloth (illustrative)
m, R = 0.17, 0.02625              # mass (kg) and radius (m) of a snooker ball
I = 2 / 5 * m * R**2              # solid sphere about a diameter

def strike(v0, h, dt=1e-5, show=False):
    """A level cue strike at height h above the centre gives speed v0 and spin w0 = m v0 h / I.
    Step the Newton-Euler equations until the ball rolls. Returns (time, distance, speed, spin)."""
    v, w, x, n = v0, m * v0 * h / I, 0.0, 0
    s0 = np.sign(v - R * w)       # the direction in which the contact point slips
    while (v - R * w) * s0 > 0:
        if show and n % 5000 == 0:
            print(f"{n * dt:6.3f} {v:9.3f} {R * w:10.3f} {v - R * w:11.3f} {x:8.3f}")
        F = mu * m * g * s0       # kinetic friction opposes the slip
        v -= F / m * dt           # sum of forces:     m dv/dt = -F
        w += F * R / I * dt       # moments about G:   I dw/dt = F R
        x += v * dt
        n += 1
    return n * dt, x, v, w

print("struck through the centre at 2.0 m/s:")
print("  t (s)   v (m/s)   R w (m/s)  slip (m/s)   x (m)")
t, x, v, w = strike(2.0, 0.0, show=True)
print(f"{t:6.3f} {v:9.3f} {R * w:10.3f} {0.0:11.3f} {x:8.3f}   rolls from here on")
E0, E1 = 0.5 * m * 2.0**2, 0.5 * m * v**2 + 0.5 * I * w**2
print(f"theory: rolls after 2 v0 / (7 mu g) = {2 * 2.0 / (7 * mu * g):.3f} s at 5 v0 / 7 = {5 / 7 * 2.0:.3f} m/s")
print(f"kinetic energy lost to sliding friction: {100 * (1 - E1 / E0):.1f}% (theory: 2/7 = 28.6%)")

print("\ncue height h    spin R w0   slides for            then rolls at")
for h_over_R in [0.0, 0.4, -0.4]:
    Rw0 = m * 2.0 * h_over_R * R / I * R
    t, x, v, w = strike(2.0, h_over_R * R)
    print(f"{h_over_R:+5.1f} R    {Rw0:+8.2f} m/s   {t:5.3f} s and {x:5.3f} m   {v:5.3f} m/s")
```

Struck through its centre at 2.0 m/s, the ball slides for 0.29 s and half a metre, then rolls at $\tfrac57$ of its starting speed, 1.43 m/s, having lost $\tfrac27$ of its kinetic energy to sliding friction, exactly as the closed-form solution predicts. Struck at $0.4R$ above the centre, it rolls from the very first instant and loses nothing to sliding: the impulse there gives exactly the spin that rolling needs. Engineers call that height the **centre of percussion**, and it is why a well-designed hammer, bat or racket has a "sweet spot" where an impact sends no jolt into the hand. Struck at $0.4R$ below the centre, the backspin fights the motion, and the ball slides for 0.83 m before rolling on at only 0.86 m/s.

<Section id="gyroscopes" kind="human" title="Gyroscopic effects, in brief">

Everything so far happened in a plane, where angular velocity and angular momentum both point along the fixed axis $\mathbf k$ and only their sizes change. In three dimensions they can change *direction*, and the results are famously counter-intuitive. For a body spinning fast about its axis of symmetry, the **angular momentum** is, to a good approximation, $\mathbf H = I\boldsymbol\omega$, along the spin axis, and the rotational form of Newton's second law is
$$
\sum \mathbf M_G = \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf H}{\mathrm dt}.
$$
A moment changes $\mathbf H$ *in the direction of the moment*, not in the direction in which the moment would tip a body at rest.

Hang a spinning wheel by one end of its axle and its weight exerts a moment $M = mgr$ about the pivot, horizontal and at right angles to the axle. That moment adds angular momentum sideways, so the axle swings round the post, a motion called **precession**, at a rate $\Omega = M/(I\omega)$, instead of dropping. A bicycle wheel of 1.5 kg, with nearly all its mass at a 0.30 m rim ($I \approx mR^2 = 0.135$ kg m²), spun at 10 revolutions per second and supported 0.15 m from its centre, precesses at $\Omega = (1.5 \times 9.81 \times 0.15)/(0.135 \times 62.8) = 0.26$ rad/s, one turn in about 24 s. Halve the spin and it precesses twice as fast; this simple formula is valid only while the spin is much faster than the precession.

The same equation, read the other way, gives the **gyroscopic moment** that a machine must supply to turn a spinning rotor: $M = I\omega\,\Omega$. For a rotor with $I = 20$ kg m² at 6000 rpm (628 rad/s) inside a vehicle turning at 0.2 rad/s, the bearings must carry a moment of $20 \times 628 \times 0.2 = 2.5$ kN m, which is why the rotor bearings of ship and aircraft engines are designed for manoeuvres as well as weight. Engineers also put the effect to work: gyrocompasses that seek true north, gyrostabilisers that reduce the rolling of boats, and **control moment gyroscopes** that turn space stations and large satellites without using propellant by tilting fast-spinning wheels. The full three-dimensional theory, Euler's equations for a rigid body and the rotation matrices and quaternions that describe its attitude, is developed in Lumenary Robotics' [rotations and quaternions](/en/robotics/rotations-and-quaternions/) and [robot dynamics](/en/robotics/robot-dynamics/).

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="From the compound pendulum to the gyrocompass">

Christiaan Huygens solved the first rigid-body dynamics problem of real consequence in his *Horologium Oscillatorium* (1673): the period of a swinging body of any shape, which needed what we now call its moment of inertia and its centre of oscillation, for the pendulum clocks he was perfecting. Leonhard Euler named and defined the moment of inertia and, in a series of works culminating in his *Theoria motus corporum solidorum seu rigidorum* (1765), gave the equations of rigid-body motion in three dimensions that every multibody code still solves. The parallel-axis theorem is often named after the Swiss geometer Jakob Steiner, who studied such relations in the nineteenth century. Léon Foucault demonstrated and named the gyroscope in Paris in 1852, as a way to show the Earth's rotation. Practical gyrocompasses followed in the early twentieth century, from Hermann Anschütz-Kaempfe in Germany and Elmer Sperry in the United States, and gyroscopes went on to guide ships, aircraft and spacecraft until optical and micro-machined (MEMS) gyroscopes took over most of their tasks.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Rigid-body dynamics in professional practice">

**Mass properties from CAD.** Nobody integrates $r^2\,\mathrm dm$ by hand for a real part. CAD systems, from open-source [FreeCAD](https://www.freecad.org/) to the commercial packages used across industry, report a part's mass, centre of mass and full inertia tensor from its solid model, and engineers check them against hand estimates like the ones above. For assemblies whose density is uncertain (a car, a satellite, a robot with cables and electronics), inertia is *measured*, by swinging the object on a calibrated pendulum or a torsional "inertia table" and timing the oscillation.

**Balancing rotors.** A rotor whose centre of mass is off its axis, or whose principal axis is tilted, pushes on its bearings once every revolution with a force proportional to $\omega^2$. Fans, turbines, crankshafts and car wheels are therefore balanced, by adding or removing small masses, to quality grades set out in the ISO 21940 series published by [ISO](https://www.iso.org/). The rotating force that an imbalance creates is the forcing of <LessonLink slug="vibrations">the next lesson</LessonLink>.

**Multibody simulation.** Vehicles, landing gear, robots and machine tools are modelled as many rigid bodies joined by constraints, and simulated by multibody codes: commercial tools from companies in the United States, Germany, Belgium and South Korea, and open-source engines such as Project Chrono, [MuJoCo](https://mujoco.org/) and Drake, which robotics research relies on. Each of them assembles and integrates the Newton–Euler equations of this lesson, in three dimensions, for every body at every time step.

**Safety around rotating machinery.** Rotating parts store energy ($\tfrac12 I\omega^2$) and can catch clothing, hair and fingers in an instant. That is why machine-safety standards require fixed guards over belts, couplings and shafts, interlocks that stop a machine before a guard can be opened, and containment around high-speed rotors such as flywheels and turbine wheels. In a school or home workshop, rotating tools are used only with their guards in place, with long hair tied back and loose clothing secured, and with supervision.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A body has a moment of inertia."** It has many: one for every axis. The same rod has $mL^2/12$ about its middle and $mL^2/3$ about its end. Always say which axis, and use the parallel-axis theorem to move between parallel ones.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$\sum M = I\alpha$ works about any point."** It works about the centre of mass G and about a fixed axis of rotation. About any other point, the moment of $m\mathbf a_G$ must be included, which is exactly what the kinetic diagram is for.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Friction on a rolling wheel always slows it down and wastes energy."** Static friction at a contact point that does not slip does no work. On a slope it is what makes the wheel turn; on a driven wheel it is what pushes the vehicle forwards. Energy is lost in rolling only through slip, deformation of tyre and road (rolling resistance), and the bearings.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A heavier ball rolls down a slope faster."** For bodies of the same shape rolling without slipping, $a_G = g\sin\theta/(1 + k)$ depends only on the shape factor $k$, not on mass or size. Until air drag matters, a solid steel ball and a solid glass marble keep pace.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Bicycles stay upright because of the gyroscopic effect of their wheels."** The spinning wheels do contribute, but experiments with specially built bicycles whose gyroscopic effects were cancelled showed that a bicycle can still balance itself: steering geometry and the distribution of mass matter at least as much, and a rider's steering most of all.

</Misconception>
