<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Buildings as energy machines">

Buildings account for about 30% of the world's final energy use, according to the IEA's tracking of the buildings sector, and most of it goes on keeping people at a comfortable temperature, with clean air and hot water. In Helsinki, Harbin or Winnipeg the problem is heat: a poorly built house can need more energy in a winter than a car uses in years. In Riyadh, Delhi or Lagos the problem is cooling: the IEA's [The Future of Cooling](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-future-of-cooling) estimated in 2018 that air conditioners and fans already used about a fifth of the electricity in buildings worldwide, and that without better efficiency the energy used for space cooling could more than triple by 2050 as incomes and temperatures rise.

A building is a machine whose working fluid is the air inside it and whose job is to hold that air within a narrow band of temperature and humidity, whatever the weather does. The tools of this lesson are the ones you already have: conduction through layers and heat exchangers from <LessonLink slug="heat-transfer">Heat transfer</LessonLink>, the refrigeration cycle from <LessonLink slug="power-and-refrigeration-cycles">Power and refrigeration cycles</LessonLink>, the Carnot limit from <LessonLink slug="second-law-and-entropy">The second law and entropy</LessonLink>, and cogeneration from <LessonLink slug="power-plants-and-energy-conversion">Power plants and energy conversion</LessonLink>.

A building does three jobs: it slows heat down (insulation, airtightness, shading), it stores heat (thermal mass), and it moves heat deliberately (heating, cooling and ventilation). Good design does the first two so well that the third becomes small, and then makes the third efficient.

</Section>

<Section id="balance" kind="definitions" title="The energy balance of a building">

Treat the heated air and everything in thermal contact with it as one control volume. In steady conditions, the heating system must supply whatever the building loses minus whatever it gains for free:
$$\dot Q_\text{heating} = H\,(T_i - T_e) - \dot Q_\text{gains}, \qquad H = \sum_k U_k A_k + \sum_j \psi_j \ell_j + \rho c_p \dot V,$$
where $T_i$ and $T_e$ are the inside and outside temperatures, the **gains** are heat from people, appliances, cooking and sunshine through windows, and $H$ (W/K) is the building's **heat loss coefficient**. Its three terms are conduction through the fabric, extra losses at **thermal bridges** (junctions, lintels, balcony slabs) of length $\ell_j$, and **ventilation**, the air deliberately or accidentally exchanged, with volume flow $\dot V$.

<Definition term="Thermal resistance and U-value">

A layer of thickness $d$ and thermal conductivity $k$ has a **thermal resistance** $R = d/k$ (m²K/W) per square metre. Resistances in series add, including the **surface resistances** of the air films on each side. The **U-value** (thermal transmittance) of an element is $U = 1/\sum R$, in W/(m²K): the heat flow per square metre per kelvin of temperature difference.

</Definition>

Ventilation is easy to underestimate. Air has a volumetric heat capacity of about 1.2 kJ/(m³K), so a 250 m³ house that exchanges its air once an hour loses about 84 W for every kelvin of temperature difference, as much as 300 m² of a well-insulated wall. Old houses exchange their air through cracks whether anyone wants it or not; new ones are built airtight and ventilated mechanically, often with **heat recovery** that warms the incoming air with the outgoing.

Annual demand follows from summing the balance over the year. The outside temperature below which heating is needed, the **balance-point temperature** $T_b = T_i - \dot Q_\text{gains}/H$, falls as a building is insulated, because the same gains cover a larger share of a smaller loss. Summing $T_b - T_e$ over the days colder than $T_b$ gives the **heating degree-days**, and the annual heating energy is roughly $H \times \text{degree-days} \times 24$ hours. Cooling degree-days are defined in the same way above a cooling base.

</Section>

<Section id="u-values" kind="computation" title="U-values, computed for a wall">

The program computes the U-value of a brick-and-block cavity wall with mineral wool in the cavity, the temperature at each layer on a cold day, and the effect of timber studs bridging the insulation in a timber-frame wall. The conductivities are typical values; real designs use the declared values of the actual products.

```python run
import math

# U-value of a wall: thermal resistances in series, R = thickness / conductivity.
R_SI, R_SE = 0.13, 0.04          # m^2 K/W: conventional inside and outside surface resistances
cavity_wall = [                  # (layer, thickness m, conductivity W/(m K)), outside to inside
    ("brick", 0.102, 0.77),
    ("mineral wool", 0.100, 0.035),
    ("concrete block", 0.100, 0.50),
    ("gypsum plaster", 0.013, 0.40),
]

def u_value(layers):
    R = R_SE + sum(d / k for _, d, k in layers) + R_SI
    return 1 / R, R

U, R = u_value(cavity_wall)
U0, _ = u_value([lay for lay in cavity_wall if lay[0] != "mineral wool"])
print(f"insulated cavity wall: R = {R:.2f} m^2K/W, U = {U:.3f} W/(m^2 K)")
print(f"same wall without insulation:     U = {U0:.3f} W/(m^2 K)")

# Temperature through the wall on a winter day: 20 C inside, -5 C outside.
T_in, T_out = 20.0, -5.0
q = U * (T_in - T_out)                       # W/m^2 through the wall
print(f"\nheat flux {q:.1f} W/m^2; temperatures from outside to inside:")
T = T_out + q * R_SE
print(f"  outer surface            {T:6.1f} C")
for name, d, k in cavity_wall:
    T += q * d / k
    print(f"  inner side of {name:15}{T:6.1f} C")
print(f"  room air                 {T + q * R_SI:6.1f} C")

# Dew point of the room air (Magnus formula): where could water vapour condense?
def dew_point(T, rh):
    g = math.log(rh) + 17.62 * T / (243.12 + T)
    return 243.12 * g / (17.62 - g)
print(f"dew point of room air at 20 C and 50% humidity: {dew_point(20, 0.5):.1f} C")

# A timber-frame wall: studs (15% of the area) bridge the insulation.
frame = [("render board", 0.012, 0.25), ("insulation between studs", 0.140, 0.035), ("plasterboard", 0.0125, 0.25)]
studs = [("render board", 0.012, 0.25), ("timber stud", 0.140, 0.13), ("plasterboard", 0.0125, 0.25)]
f_stud = 0.15
R_paths = 1 / ((1 - f_stud) * u_value(frame)[0] + f_stud * u_value(studs)[0])   # parallel heat-flow paths
k_mix = (1 - f_stud) * 0.035 + f_stud * 0.13                                 # or: average each layer first
R_planes = R_SE + 0.012 / 0.25 + 0.140 / k_mix + 0.0125 / 0.25 + R_SI
print(f"\ntimber frame: U = {u_value(frame)[0]:.3f} through the insulation alone; with the studs, "
      f"{1 / R_paths:.3f} (parallel paths) or {1 / R_planes:.3f} (averaged layers),")
print(f"so about {2 / (R_paths + R_planes):.3f} W/(m^2 K) using the mean of the two resistances")
```

Ten centimetres of mineral wool cut the wall's U-value from 1.869 to 0.295 W/(m²K), a factor of more than six: on a day with 25 K between inside and outside, each square metre loses 7.4 W instead of about 47 W. Almost the whole temperature drop happens across the insulation, from −3.7 °C to 17.3 °C.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The dew point of the room air, 9.3 °C, lies inside the insulation. If moist indoor air can diffuse or leak into the wall, water may condense in its cold outer part, soaking the insulation and rotting timber. That is why walls carry a vapour control layer on the warm side and are designed to dry outwards, and why designers check every new build-up with a moisture calculation, not just a U-value.

</Narrator>

The timber-frame wall shows a **thermal bridge** at work. Through the insulation alone its U-value would be 0.234, but the studs, only 15% of the area, conduct heat about four times better, and the wall as a whole comes to about 0.316 W/(m²K), 35% worse. International practice (ISO 6946) brackets such walls between the two calculations in the program, heat flowing in parallel paths or through averaged layers, and takes the mean of the two resistances. Bridges at junctions are computed separately by two-dimensional conduction models.

<Section id="climates" kind="computation" title="Heating and cooling across climates">

The second program puts one house, of 100 m² of floor, into seven climates in two versions: uninsulated and draughty, as many houses built before the 1970s were, and insulated and airtight, with mechanical ventilation and heat recovery. Each climate is a smooth annual cycle of daily mean temperatures, with rough values resembling a real city, and the house is heated to 20 °C and cooled when it would exceed 26 °C. When it is cooler outside than in, the occupants open windows before anyone switches on air conditioning.

```python run
import math

# A two-storey house of 100 m^2 floor area: areas (m^2) and U-values (W/(m^2 K)).
OLD = {"walls": (110, 1.6), "roof": (50, 2.0), "floor": (50, 0.8), "windows": (20, 4.8), "door": (2, 3.0)}
NEW = {"walls": (110, 0.20), "roof": (50, 0.12), "floor": (50, 0.15), "windows": (20, 0.9), "door": (2, 1.0)}
VOLUME = 250.0                                   # m^3 of heated air

def loss_coefficient(elements, ach, recovery=0.0, bridges=0.05):
    """Heat loss coefficient H (W/K): fabric + thermal bridges + ventilation."""
    area = sum(a for a, _ in elements.values())
    H_fabric = sum(a * u for a, u in elements.values()) + bridges * area
    H_vent = 1.2 * 1005 * VOLUME * ach / 3600 * (1 - recovery)   # rho * cp * airflow
    return H_fabric + H_vent

houses = {
    "1960s, uninsulated, draughty": loss_coefficient(OLD, ach=1.0, bridges=0.15),
    "insulated, mechanical ventilation with heat recovery": loss_coefficient(NEW, ach=0.5, recovery=0.85),
}
for name, H in houses.items():
    print(f"{name:54} H = {H:4.0f} W/K")

# Climates as a smooth annual cycle of daily mean temperature (rough, illustrative values;
# the southern hemisphere's seasons are reversed, which does not change the annual totals).
CLIMATES = {                     # (annual mean C, half the summer-winter difference K)
    "cold (like Helsinki)": (6.0, 11.0),
    "temperate oceanic (like London)": (11.5, 6.5),
    "continental (like Beijing)": (13.0, 15.0),
    "Mediterranean (like Athens)": (18.5, 8.5),
    "tropical highland (like Nairobi)": (18.0, 1.5),
    "hot desert (like Riyadh)": (26.5, 10.0),
    "hot humid (like Singapore)": (27.5, 1.0),
}
H_WINDOWS = 1.2 * 1005 * VOLUME * 4 / 3600   # W/K of extra ventilation from opening windows (4 ach)

def annual_kwh(H, mean, amp, heat_set=20.0, cool_set=26.0):
    """Heating and cooling needs (kWh/yr) from a daily steady-state balance."""
    heat = cool = 0.0
    for day in range(365):
        season = math.cos(2 * math.pi * (day - 20) / 365)      # +1 in late January, -1 in July
        T_out = mean - amp * season
        gains = 300.0 + 300.0 - 200.0 * season                  # W: internal 300, sun 100 to 500
        heat += max(0.0, H * (heat_set - T_out) - gains)        # W, averaged over the day
        H_cool = H + (H_WINDOWS if T_out < cool_set else 0.0)   # open the windows if it is cooler outside
        cool += max(0.0, gains - H_cool * (cool_set - T_out))
    return heat * 24 / 1000, cool * 24 / 1000                   # kWh per year

print("\nannual heating and cooling needs, kWh per m^2 of floor per year")
print(f"{'climate':34}{'1960s house':>20}{'insulated house':>22}")
print(f"{'':34}{'heat':>10}{'cool':>10}{'heat':>11}{'cool':>11}")
for name, (mean, amp) in CLIMATES.items():
    row = []
    for H in houses.values():
        h, c = annual_kwh(H, mean, amp)
        row += [h / 100, c / 100]
    print(f"{name:34}{row[0]:10.0f}{row[1]:10.0f}{row[2]:11.0f}{row[3]:11.0f}")
```

Insulation, airtightness and heat recovery cut the heat loss coefficient from 537 to 73 W/K, and the results show how differently that plays out by climate. In the cold climate, heating falls from 605 to 49 kWh per square metre a year; in the temperate oceanic one from 347 to 20, close to the Passivhaus target of 15. In the hot desert the uninsulated house needs 195 kWh/m² of cooling and the insulated one 56. In the tropical highlands, neither house needs much of anything, and in the hot, humid tropics cooling dominates whatever the walls are made of, because the outside air is never cooler than the house for long.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Look at the Mediterranean row: insulating the house removes nearly all its heating (142 down to 6) but leaves its cooling almost unchanged (18 to 16), because in summer what matters is the 800 W of sunshine and appliances trapped inside. That is the signature of a climate where shading, thermal mass and night ventilation, computed below, matter as much as insulation.

</Narrator>

The steady daily model leaves out two important things. It ignores the swing between day and night, which in hot, dry climates is the designer's best ally. And it counts only **sensible** heat: in humid climates an air conditioner must also condense water out of the air, a **latent** load that in Singapore or Lagos can be a third or more of the total, which is why tropical systems are designed around dehumidification as much as temperature.

</Section>

<Section id="heat-pumps" kind="computation" title="Heat pumps in buildings">

A heat pump is the refrigeration cycle of <LessonLink slug="power-and-refrigeration-cycles">Power and refrigeration cycles</LessonLink> used for its hot side: it takes heat from outdoor air, the ground or water, and delivers it at a useful temperature, with a **coefficient of performance** $\text{COP} = \dot Q_\text{delivered}/\dot W$ well above 1. The second law caps it at the Carnot value $T_H/(T_H - T_L)$, so everything depends on the **temperature lift**: the colder the source and the hotter the water the heat pump must make, the lower its COP. Real machines reach roughly half of Carnot between the refrigerant's actual temperatures, which sit several kelvin beyond the air and water because heat exchangers need a temperature difference to work.

Two engineering choices follow. **Emitters**: underfloor heating or large radiators work with water at 35 to 45 °C, small radiators need 55 °C or more. **Weather compensation**: the controller lowers the water temperature on mild days, when less heat is needed. The program estimates the **seasonal COP** (SCOP), heat delivered over electricity used for a year, for a house with $H = 200$ W/K in three climates, with day-to-day weather from a seeded random model.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

# An air-source heat pump heating a house with heat loss coefficient H = 200 W/K.
H, GAINS, T_ROOM = 200.0, 450.0, 20.0
T_BAL = T_ROOM - GAINS / H                        # below this outdoor temperature, heating is needed

def cop(T_out, T_flow, carnot_fraction=0.55):
    """COP from the Carnot COP between evaporator (6 K below the air) and condenser (5 K above the water)."""
    T_hot, T_cold = T_flow + 5 + 273.15, T_out - 6 + 273.15
    c = carnot_fraction * T_hot / (T_hot - T_cold)
    return c * (0.9 if -5 < T_out < 5 else 1.0)    # frost forms on the outdoor coil: defrosting costs ~10%

def season(mean, amp, design_flow, seed=1):
    """Heat delivered and electricity used over a year of daily mean temperatures."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    anomaly, heat, elec, T_design = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, mean - amp - 8.0   # design day: a cold snap
    for day in range(365):
        anomaly = 0.8 * anomaly + rng.normal(0.0, 2.0)                  # weather that persists for days
        T_out = mean - amp * math.cos(2 * math.pi * (day - 20) / 365) + anomaly
        load = max(0.0, H * (T_BAL - T_out))                            # W
        if load == 0.0:
            continue
        frac = min(1.0, (T_BAL - T_out) / (T_BAL - T_design))           # weather compensation:
        T_flow = T_ROOM + 10 + (design_flow - T_ROOM - 10) * frac       # cooler water on mild days
        heat += load * 24 / 1000
        elec += load / cop(T_out, T_flow) * 24 / 1000
    return heat, elec

print(f"COP at  7 C outdoors, 35 C water: {cop(7, 35):.2f};  at -7 C, 55 C water: {cop(-7, 55):.2f}")
print("\nseasonal COP (heat delivered / electricity used)")
print(f"{'climate':34}{'underfloor (35 C)':>18}{'radiators (55 C)':>18}")
for name, mean, amp in (("cold (like Helsinki)", 6.0, 11.0), ("temperate oceanic (like London)", 11.5, 6.5),
                        ("continental (like Beijing)", 13.0, 15.0)):
    s = [season(mean, amp, flow) for flow in (35.0, 55.0)]
    print(f"{name:34}{s[0][0] / s[0][1]:18.2f}{s[1][0] / s[1][1]:18.2f}")

# Carbon dioxide per kWh of heat: heat pump (temperate climate, radiators) against a gas boiler.
heat, elec = season(11.5, 6.5, 55.0)
scop = heat / elec
gas = 0.20 / 0.92                                 # kg CO2 per kWh of gas burned, boiler efficiency 92%
print(f"\ngas boiler: {gas * 1000:.0f} g CO2 per kWh of heat")
for grid in (50, 250, 500, 800):                  # g CO2 per kWh of electricity
    print(f"heat pump on a grid of {grid:3} g/kWh: {grid / scop:4.0f} g CO2 per kWh of heat")
```

The instantaneous COP falls from 4.42 on a mild day with 35 °C water to 2.51 on a frosty one with 55 °C water. Over a season, underfloor heating beats radiators everywhere (4.59 against 3.72 in the temperate climate, 3.70 against 3.05 in the cold one), and even the radiator system delivers three to nearly four units of heat for each unit of electricity. On a grid emitting 250 g of CO₂ per kWh, the heat pump's heat carries 67 g/kWh against the gas boiler's 217; only on a grid as carbon-intensive as 800 g/kWh, typical of electricity made almost entirely from coal, does the advantage vanish.

<Narrator who="gideon">

This model is kinder than reality. Field trials in several European countries have measured seasonal performance factors for air-source heat pumps mostly between about 2.5 and 4, lower than models suggest, because real systems also heat domestic hot water to 50 °C or more, cycle on and off, run pumps and fans, and are sometimes installed with emitters too small or controls set badly. The margin to watch is the design day: a heat pump sized for an average winter will struggle in a cold snap, when its output falls just as the house needs most.

</Narrator>

The IEA's [The Future of Heat Pumps](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-future-of-heat-pumps) (2022) calls heat pumps powered by low-emission electricity the central technology for decarbonising heating. Heat pumps are already common in Nordic homes, despite the cold winters, and China is both the largest market and the largest manufacturer. **Refrigerants** are changing too: under the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol and the EU's F-gas Regulation of 2024, manufacturers are moving from high-global-warming hydrofluorocarbons to propane (R-290), R-32 and carbon dioxide (R-744). Propane is flammable, so its use is governed by charge limits and safety standards, and installing or servicing any refrigerant circuit is work for trained, certified technicians.

</Section>

<Section id="comfort" kind="human" title="Ventilation and thermal comfort">

People need fresh air. Indoors, carbon dioxide from breathing is a convenient indicator of how well a room is ventilated: outdoor air holds about 420 ppm, and a sedentary adult breathes out roughly 0.005 litres of CO₂ per second.

<Example title="Ventilating a classroom">

In steady state, a room's CO₂ concentration rises above outdoors by the CO₂ generation divided by the ventilation flow, $C_\text{in} = C_\text{out} + G/\dot V$. To keep a classroom of 30 pupils below 1000 ppm, $\dot V = 30 \times 0.005/(1000 - 420)\times 10^{6}$ L/s $\approx 260$ L/s, about 8.6 litres per second per person, in line with the 8 to 10 L/s per person that many guidelines recommend. On a 0 °C day, warming that air to 20 °C takes $1.2 \times 1.005 \times 0.26 \times 20 \approx 6.3$ kW, a large heater's worth, unless heat is recovered from the outgoing air.

</Example>

Ventilation standards such as ASHRAE 62.1 and EN 16798-1 set rates per person and per square metre, by type of room; after the COVID-19 pandemic, attention to ventilation and filtration in schools and offices rose sharply because they reduce the airborne spread of infections.

**Thermal comfort** depends on more than air temperature. P. O. Fanger's comfort model, developed in Denmark around 1970 and the basis of ISO 7730 and ASHRAE 55, combines six factors: air temperature, the **mean radiant temperature** of the surrounding surfaces, air speed, humidity, the occupants' metabolic rate and their clothing. It predicts the average vote of a large group on a scale from cold to hot, and the percentage likely to be dissatisfied, which never falls below about 5%: no single condition pleases everyone. Cold windows and walls make a room feel colder than its air temperature, which is one reason well-insulated buildings feel comfortable at lower air temperatures.

For buildings with windows people can open, field studies by Richard de Dear, Gail Brager and others led to the **adaptive** comfort model: people who live with the climate, dress for it and control their surroundings accept a wider band, and the temperature they find comfortable rises with the outdoor temperature of the preceding days. Buildings in Ahmedabad, Accra or Brisbane need not be held at the same 22 °C as an office in Stockholm, and designing for that saves a great deal of energy.

</Section>

<Section id="thermal-mass" kind="computation" title="Thermal mass and night ventilation, simulated">

Traditional buildings in hot, dry climates use the large swing between day and night. Thick adobe and stone walls in Yazd, Marrakech or Santa Fe absorb heat during the day and release it at night, when wind towers (the *bādgir* of Iran and the Gulf), courtyards and open windows flush it out with cool night air. The program tests the idea with a **lumped thermal model**, one capacity $C$ at one temperature,
$$C\frac{dT}{dt} = H_\text{total}(t)\,\bigl(T_\text{out}(t) - T\bigr) + \dot Q_\text{gains}(t),$$
integrated over a week by the explicit Euler method in five-minute steps, the same finite-difference idea as in <LessonLink slug="heat-transfer">Heat transfer</LessonLink>. Outside, the temperature swings from 22 °C at 03:00 to 38 °C at 15:00; there is no air conditioning.

```python run
import math

# Thermal mass and night ventilation in a hot, dry summer, with no air conditioning.
# The house is one thermal capacity C (J/K) at one temperature T: a lumped "RC" model,
#   C dT/dt = (H_fabric + H_vent) (T_out - T) + gains.
H_FABRIC = 60.0                           # W/K: an insulated house of 100 m^2
H_AIR_PER_ACH = 1.2 * 1005 * 250 / 3600   # W/K for each air change per hour of a 250 m^3 house

def outdoor(t_h):
    return 30.0 - 8.0 * math.cos(2 * math.pi * (t_h - 3) / 24)       # 22 C at 03:00, 38 C at 15:00

def gains(t_h, shading):
    sun = max(0.0, math.sin(math.pi * (t_h - 6) / 12))               # daylight from 06:00 to 18:00
    return 300.0 + (1200.0 if not shading else 400.0) * sun           # W: people and appliances + sun

def simulate(C, night_vent, shading=False, days=7, dt=300.0):
    T, warm_hours, trace = 28.0, 0.0, []
    for step in range(int(days * 86400 / dt)):
        t_h = (step * dt / 3600) % 24
        T_out = outdoor(t_h)
        night = t_h >= 20 or t_h < 8
        ach = 6.0 if (night_vent and night and T_out < T) else 0.5   # open up at night if cooler outside
        H = H_FABRIC + ach * H_AIR_PER_ACH
        T += dt * (H * (T_out - T) + gains(t_h, shading)) / C         # explicit Euler step
        if step * dt >= (days - 1) * 86400:                           # record the last day
            trace.append(T)
            warm_hours += dt / 3600 if T > 30.0 else 0.0
    return min(trace), max(trace), warm_hours

print("outdoors: 22 C at night to 38 C in the afternoon")
print(f"{'house':36}{'indoor min':>11}{'indoor max':>11}{'hours > 30 C':>14}")
cases = [("light (8 MJ/K)", 8e6, False, False), ("light + night ventilation", 8e6, True, False),
         ("heavy (26 MJ/K)", 26e6, False, False), ("heavy + night ventilation", 26e6, True, False),
         ("heavy + night ventilation + shading", 26e6, True, True)]
for label, C, vent, shade in cases:
    lo, hi, hours = simulate(C, vent, shade)
    print(f"{label:36}{lo:9.1f} C{hi:9.1f} C{hours:12.1f}")
```

Mass alone does not help: closed up all day and night, both houses settle above 34 °C, warmer than the outdoor average, because the sunshine and the occupants' heat have nowhere to go. <Narrator who="chloe">

The heavy wall is a low-pass filter. Its time constant, $C/H$, is many hours, so it barely responds to the daily cycle and sits near the average of what it sees. Night ventilation lowers that average by letting the house "see" the cool night far more strongly than the hot afternoon. Where nights stay warm and humid, as in Singapore, there is nothing cool to store, and the strategy fails.

</Narrator>

<Section id="district" kind="human" title="District heating and cooling">

A **district heating** network distributes heat from central sources through insulated pipes under the streets, so that each building needs only a heat exchanger. Its history is a steady fall in temperature, described by Henrik Lund and colleagues as five generations: steam systems from the 1880s (New York's still runs); pressurised water above 100 °C; the pre-insulated pipes at 70 to 100 °C that serve Scandinavian cities; **fourth-generation** networks at 50 to 70 °C, which accept low-grade heat; and **fifth-generation** "ambient" loops at 10 to 25 °C, from which each building's own heat pump draws. Lower temperatures cut pipe losses and, more importantly, let networks use heat that would otherwise be wasted: CHP plants, industry, sewage, data centres, large heat pumps and solar thermal fields.

Denmark is the leading example, with about two thirds of its households on district heating and pioneering solar plants with large seasonal pit stores, as at Dronninglund and Vojens. Reykjavík heats nearly the whole city with geothermal water; Helsinki draws heat from treated wastewater with large heat pumps; northern Chinese cities run some of the largest networks in the world, still mostly fed by coal CHP and moving to cleaner sources. **District cooling** works the same way with chilled water: Paris draws on the Seine, Toronto on the cold deep water of Lake Ontario, and Gulf cities such as Dubai and Doha cool whole districts from central chiller plants, which are more efficient and easier to maintain than thousands of small units.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How it is done in practice">

Building services engineers calculate U-values under ISO 6946, design heat loads under EN 12831 or the ASHRAE Handbook's methods, ventilation under ASHRAE 62.1 or EN 16798-1, and comfort under ISO 7730 and ASHRAE 55; national codes, such as the energy codes of the EU's member states under the recast Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (2024), ASHRAE 90.1 in many countries, and India's Energy Conservation Building Code, set minimum performance. Voluntary standards such as Passivhaus go further. These documents are published by [ASHRAE](https://www.ashrae.org/), [ISO](https://www.iso.org/) and [CEN](https://www.cencenelec.eu/) and national bodies; this lesson describes their methods only in outline.

For design decisions, engineers simulate whole buildings hour by hour with tools such as the open-source [EnergyPlus](https://energyplus.net/) and its OpenStudio interface, TRNSYS, IDA ICE, IES VE and DesignBuilder, or the Modelica Buildings Library for control studies. Those tools solve, for every zone and every hour of a weather file, a much richer version of the lumped model above. An EnergyPlus model describes the wall of this lesson in its text input format like this (not run here):

The frontier in 2026 is less about single buildings than about buildings as part of the energy system: heat pumps and thermal storage that shift demand to hours of cheap, clean electricity, retrofitting the existing stock at scale, embodied carbon in materials (<LessonLink slug="sustainable-engineering">Sustainable engineering</LessonLink>), and the comfort of the billions of people in hot countries who do not yet have cooling.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Thicker insulation always pays."** Each layer helps less than the one before: U falls as $1/R$, so doubling the insulation of an already good wall saves far less than the first few centimetres did. Beyond a point, money is better spent on airtightness, windows, thermal bridges or the heating system.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A heat pump makes heat out of electricity, like a heater but better."** A heat pump *moves* heat from outdoors (even from air at −10 °C, which still holds plenty of thermal energy) and adds the electricity it uses. Its COP is set by the temperature lift, which is why low water temperatures matter so much.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Houses need to breathe through their walls."** Houses need controlled *ventilation*; walls need to manage *moisture*, letting water vapour out without letting moist air in. Uncontrolled leakage wastes energy and can carry moisture into the structure.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Heavy walls keep a building cool."** Mass only stores heat; it keeps a building cool only if the heat it absorbs by day is removed at night. Without night ventilation, the simulated heavy house was as hot as the light one.

</Misconception>
