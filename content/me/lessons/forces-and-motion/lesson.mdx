<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why does anything move?">

Kick a football and it flies off across the grass, slows down, and stops. Stop pedalling a bicycle and it rolls on for a while before it, too, comes to a halt. A rocket stands still on its launch pad for weeks, then rises into the sky on a column of hot gas.

What makes things start moving? What makes them stop? Why does a full shopping trolley feel so much harder to get going than an empty one?

Three short rules, published by Isaac Newton in 1687, answer all of these questions. Engineers use them every day to design cars, lifts, bridges, cranes, aircraft and spacecraft. In this lesson you will learn those rules, and then use a few lines of Python to watch them work.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here is the question to keep in your head all lesson: is a force needed to keep something *moving*, or only to *change* how it moves? Most people's first answer is the wrong one, and finding out why is the heart of mechanics.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="forces" kind="intuition" title="Pushes and pulls">

A **force** is a push or a pull. You push a door open, pull a rope, squeeze a lemon, stretch an elastic band. Forces also act without anything touching: the Earth pulls you downwards all the time (that is **gravity**), and a magnet pulls a paper clip from a distance.

Every force has two things you need to know: **how big** it is and **which way** it acts. Pushing a box to the left is not the same as pushing it to the right, even if you push equally hard. That is why engineers draw forces as **arrows**: the arrow points the way the force acts, and a longer arrow means a bigger force.

<Definition term="force">

A **force** is a push or a pull on an object. It has a size and a direction. Forces are measured in **newtons** (symbol N), named after Isaac Newton. Holding a small apple of about 100 grams in your hand, you feel a force of about 1 newton.

</Definition>

Usually several forces act on an object at the same time. To see what the object will do, engineers draw a **free-body diagram**: a sketch of just the object, with an arrow for every force acting on it. Here is one for a small model cart driven by a motor along a level floor.

The up and down arrows are equal and opposite, so they cancel: the cart does not sink into the floor or float up off it. The sideways forces do not cancel. The push is 1.5 N forwards and friction is 0.5 N backwards, so overall there is 1.5 − 0.5 = 1 N pushing the cart forwards. That overall force is called the **resultant** (or net) force.

<Definition term="resultant force">

The **resultant force** on an object is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces on it together. For forces along one line, add the ones pointing one way and subtract the ones pointing the other way. If the resultant is zero, the forces are **balanced**; otherwise they are **unbalanced**.

</Definition>

<Section id="newtons-laws" kind="definitions" title="Newton's three laws">

### The first law: things keep doing what they are doing

<Definition term="Newton's first law">

An object stays still, or keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line, unless an unbalanced force acts on it.

</Definition>

This is the answer to Chloe's question. A force is *not* needed to keep something moving; a force is needed to **change** its motion: to start it, stop it, speed it up, slow it down or turn it. Then why does a bicycle stop when you stop pedalling? Because forces are still acting on it: friction in the wheels and, above all, the push of the air. Take those away and it would roll on forever.

You can see the first law where friction is small. A puck slides a long way across ice. Astronauts on the International Space Station push off gently from one wall and drift across the cabin at a steady speed until they reach the other side. And in a car that brakes suddenly, your body keeps moving forwards, which is exactly why seat belts exist: the belt supplies the force that slows you down with the car.

The tendency of things to keep doing what they are doing is called **inertia**. The more mass an object has, the more inertia: a loaded lorry is much harder to start or stop than a bicycle.

### The second law: force, mass and acceleration

When the forces are unbalanced, the object **accelerates**: its speed or direction changes. Acceleration is measured in metres per second, per second (m/s²). An acceleration of 2 m/s² means the speed goes up by 2 metres per second every second.

<Definition term="Newton's second law">

The resultant force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration:

$$
F = m \times a
$$

with $F$ in newtons, $m$ in kilograms and $a$ in metres per second squared. Turned around, $a = F \div m$: a bigger force gives more acceleration, and a bigger mass gives less.

</Definition>

This rule even defines the newton: 1 N is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s².

<Example title="A cyclist pulling away">

Mei and her bicycle together have a mass of 70 kg. As she sets off, her pedalling drives the bicycle forwards with a force of 140 N, while the air and the friction in the bike push back with 35 N.

- Resultant force: 140 − 35 = **105 N** forwards.
- Acceleration: $a = 105 \div 70 = $ **1.5 m/s²**.

So every second her speed goes up by 1.5 metres per second, as long as the forces stay the same. (They don't stay the same for long: the faster she goes, the harder the air pushes back, until the forces balance and she cruises at a steady speed. That is the first law again.)

</Example>

<Example title="A firm pass in football">

A football has a mass of about 0.43 kg. Suppose a boot pushes on it with an average force of 430 N. Then $a = 430 \div 0.43 = 1000$ m/s², a huge acceleration. But the boot touches the ball for only about a hundredth of a second, so the ball's speed goes up by 1000 × 0.01 = **10 m/s**, which is 36 km/h. A light ball and a large force give a big acceleration, but only for a moment.

</Example>

### The third law: forces come in pairs

<Definition term="Newton's third law">

When one object pushes or pulls on a second object, the second pushes or pulls back on the first, with a force of the same size in the opposite direction.

</Definition>

You cannot push something without being pushed back. Push off from the side of a swimming pool and the wall pushes you away. Walking works the same way: your foot pushes the ground backwards, and the ground pushes your foot forwards. On slippery ice, your foot cannot push backwards on the ground, so the ground cannot push you forwards, and you go nowhere.

A rocket is the third law in its purest form. Its engines throw enormous amounts of hot gas downwards at great speed; the gas pushes back on the rocket with an equal force upwards, called **thrust**. A rocket does not need air to push against, which is why rockets work in space, where there is no air at all.

<Example title="Will the rocket rise?">

A rocket has a mass of 500 000 kg (500 tonnes) and its engines give a thrust of 7 000 000 N upwards.

- Its weight (you will meet weight properly in a moment) is 500 000 × 9.8 = 4 900 000 N downwards.
- Resultant: 7 000 000 − 4 900 000 = 2 100 000 N upwards.
- Acceleration: 2 100 000 ÷ 500 000 = **4.2 m/s²** upwards.

If the thrust were less than the weight, the resultant would point down, and the rocket would stay sitting on the pad. As the engines burn fuel the rocket gets lighter, so the same thrust gives it more and more acceleration.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice that the two forces of a third-law pair act on **different** objects: one on the gas, one on the rocket. That is why they never cancel each other out. Forces can only cancel when they act on the same object, as the weight and the support did on the cart.

</Narrator>

<Section id="mass-weight-friction" kind="examples" title="Mass, weight and friction">

### Mass is not weight

In everyday speech we say "I weigh 40 kilograms", but engineers keep two ideas apart.

<Definition term="mass and weight">

**Mass** is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg). It is the same wherever the object is. **Weight** is the force with which gravity pulls the object, measured in newtons (N):

$$
\text{weight} = \text{mass} \times g
$$

where $g$, the **gravitational field strength**, is about 9.8 newtons per kilogram at the Earth's surface.

</Definition>

Take a student with a mass of 40 kg. Her mass is 40 kg everywhere, but her weight changes from world to world:

| Place | $g$ (N/kg) | Weight of a 40 kg student |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earth | 9.8 | 392 N |
| Moon | 1.6 | 64 N |
| Mars | 3.7 | 148 N |
| Jupiter (at its cloud tops) | 24.8 | 992 N |

On the Moon she could carry a backpack six times heavier than on Earth with the same effort. But she would still find it just as hard to get a heavy trolley *moving* sideways, because that depends on mass, and mass does not change.

### Friction: friend and enemy

**Friction** is the force that appears when two surfaces slide, or try to slide, over each other. It always acts against the sliding. Even surfaces that look smooth are rough under a microscope, and their tiny bumps catch and stick on each other. The push of air or water on a moving object, called **drag** or air resistance, acts against motion too.

Friction is often **helpful**:

- You can walk, run and turn only because your shoes grip the ground.
- A bicycle's brakes press pads against the wheel rim or a disc, and friction slows the wheel.
- Tyres grip the road so a car can steer; that is why icy roads, where the grip almost vanishes, are so dangerous.
- A knot stays tied and a nail stays in wood thanks to friction.

And it is often **unhelpful**:

- It wastes effort: some of the push from your legs on a bicycle goes into heating the chain and the bearings instead of moving you.
- It wears parts away: brake pads, shoe soles and tyres all slowly rub away, getting warm as they do (rub your hands together and feel it).

Engineers fight unhelpful friction with oil and grease, ball bearings, smooth surfaces and streamlined shapes, and increase helpful friction with tyre treads, rubber soles and grippy brake pads.

</Section>

<Section id="simulation" kind="computation" title="A cart in Python">

Now let's put the second law to work. The program below follows the model cart from the free-body diagram, second by second. Its motor pushes with 1.5 N for 4 seconds and then switches off, while friction pulls back with 0.5 N whenever the cart is moving.

The idea is simple: chop time into small steps. In each step, work out the resultant force, divide by the mass to get the acceleration, and use the acceleration to update the speed. Then the distance goes up by the average speed during the step times the length of the step. Press **Run**:

```python run
# A model cart on a level floor. Its motor pushes for 4 seconds, then switches off.
mass = 2.0          # kilograms
motor_push = 1.5    # newtons, while the motor is on
friction = 0.5      # newtons, against the motion whenever the cart moves
motor_time = 4.0    # seconds the motor stays on
dt = 1.0            # seconds in each time step

t = 0.0             # time, in seconds
speed = 0.0         # metres per second
distance = 0.0      # metres

print("time  push  net force  accel  speed  distance")
print(" (s)   (N)        (N) (m/s2)  (m/s)       (m)")
while t <= 30:                             # follow the cart for 30 seconds at most
    push = motor_push if t < motor_time else 0.0
    if speed == 0 and push <= friction:
        net_force = 0.0                    # too weak to get it going: friction holds it still
    else:
        net_force = push - friction        # friction points backwards, so we subtract it
    acceleration = net_force / mass        # Newton's second law: a = F / m
    bar = "#" * int(speed * 10)
    print(f"{t:4.0f} {push:5.1f} {net_force:10.2f} {acceleration:6.2f} {speed:6.2f} {distance:9.2f}  {bar}")
    if speed == 0 and net_force == 0:
        print("The cart is at rest, after travelling", distance, "metres.")
        break
    new_speed = max(speed + acceleration * dt, 0.0)   # friction can stop the cart, never push it backwards
    distance += (speed + new_speed) / 2 * dt          # average speed during the step x time
    speed = new_speed
    t += dt
```

The row of `#` signs is a little sideways graph of the speed. While the motor runs, the resultant is +1 N, the acceleration is +0.5 m/s², and the speed climbs by 0.5 m/s every second, reaching 2 m/s at 4 seconds. Then the motor stops, the only sideways force left is friction, the resultant becomes −0.5 N, and the cart slows by 0.25 m/s every second until it comes to rest at 12 seconds, 12 metres from where it started.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Notice that the cart rolls *further* while slowing down (8 metres) than while speeding up (4 metres). Friction is only half the size of the resultant that the motor gave it, so it takes twice as long to take the speed away. Now change `mass` to `4.0` and run again: every acceleration is halved, the top speed is only 1 m/s, and the cart stops after 6 metres instead of 12.

</Narrator>

More things to try: make `friction` bigger than `motor_push` (the cart never starts), or set `friction` to `0.0`. With no friction at all, you will see the first law in action: after the motor stops, nothing slows the cart, and its speed stays at 3.00 m/s for the rest of the 30 seconds, and would for ever.

</Section>

<Section id="engineering" kind="human" title="From Aristotle to engineers today">

The ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle taught that a moving object keeps moving only while something pushes it, which matched everyday life, full of friction, so well that it was hard to doubt. Later scholars puzzled over things that did not fit, such as an arrow that keeps flying after it leaves the bow: John Philoponus in sixth-century Alexandria, Ibn Sina (Avicenna) in eleventh-century Persia and Jean Buridan in fourteenth-century Paris each argued that a moving object carries something within it that keeps it going. In the early 1600s Galileo Galilei rolled balls down gentle ramps in Italy and saw that, with less and less friction, a ball would keep rolling almost indefinitely. Isaac Newton put the pieces together in his *Principia* (1687).

Engineers today still begin almost every problem the way you did here: draw the object, draw every force on it, and apply Newton's laws. A team designing a lift works out the forces in its cables as it starts and stops; sports engineers measure the forces on footballs, running shoes and bicycle frames. Rocket engineers balance thrust, weight and drag using programs that chop time into tiny steps exactly as your cart program does, only with millions of steps instead of twelve.

You will meet these ideas again in more detail later in Lumenary ME: free-body diagrams in <LessonLink slug="statics-and-equilibrium">Statics and equilibrium</LessonLink>, and motion step by step in <LessonLink slug="particle-dynamics">Particle dynamics</LessonLink>. Robots obey the same laws; see [Moving](/en/robotics/moving/) in Lumenary Robotics.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A moving object needs a force to keep it moving."** A force is needed to *change* motion, not to keep it. Things on Earth seem to slow down by themselves only because friction and air resistance are always acting. When the forces on an object are balanced, it carries on at a steady speed in a straight line.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Heavier things always fall faster."** Without air, everything falls with the same acceleration. In 1971 the astronaut David Scott let go of a hammer and a falcon feather together on the Moon, where there is no air, and they reached the ground at the same moment. On Earth, a feather drifts down slowly because air resistance is large compared with its tiny weight.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Mass and weight are the same thing."** Mass, in kilograms, is how much matter there is and never changes from place to place. Weight, in newtons, is the pull of gravity on that mass, and is different on the Moon or Mars.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Action and reaction cancel out, so nothing can ever move."** The two forces of a third-law pair act on *different* objects. The ground's push acts on you and your push acts on the ground. Only the forces on *one* object decide how that object moves.

</Misconception>
