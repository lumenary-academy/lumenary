<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Turning one motion into another">

A motor turns. A piston slides. A windscreen wiper swings back and forth. An excavator bucket curls, a sewing-machine needle stabs up and down in step with a shuttle below, and a car's wheel moves up and down over a bump while staying nearly upright. Almost every machine has to turn one motion, usually steady rotation, into another, and the parts that do it are **mechanisms**: rigid links joined so that they can move only in the ways the designer intends.

This lesson is about **kinematics**, the geometry of motion, without yet asking what forces cause it (that was <LessonLink slug="rigid-body-dynamics">rigid-body dynamics</LessonLink>). You will count how many ways a mechanism can move, solve the position of a four-bar linkage with Newton's method, trace the curves its coupler draws, compute the violent accelerations inside an engine, shape a cam so that it does not hammer its follower, and work out the ratios of the planetary gear sets inside automatic gearboxes and hybrid cars.

</Section>

<Section id="mobility" kind="definitions" title="Links, joints and degrees of freedom">

<Definition term="link, joint and kinematic pair">

A **link** is a body treated as rigid. A **joint** (or kinematic pair, in Franz Reuleaux's term from the 1870s) connects two links and allows some relative motion. **Lower pairs** have surface contact: the **revolute** joint (a pin, R) allows rotation, the **prismatic** joint (a slider, P) allows translation. **Higher pairs** have point or line contact, like a cam on its follower or two gear teeth, and allow rolling with or without sliding. A chain of links with one link fixed as the frame (the **ground**) is a **mechanism**.

</Definition>

A free rigid body in a plane has three degrees of freedom: two translations and one rotation. Each revolute or prismatic joint removes two of them; a higher pair that allows both rolling and sliding removes one.

<Theorem name="Grübler–Kutzbach mobility criterion (planar)">

A planar mechanism with $n$ links (counting the ground), $j_1$ one-freedom joints (pins and sliders) and $j_2$ two-freedom joints (cam and gear contacts) has mobility

$$M = 3(n - 1) - 2j_1 - j_2,$$

the number of independent inputs needed to fix its position, *provided* the constraints are independent. In space, each body has six freedoms and the corresponding formula is $M = 6(n - 1) - \sum_i (6 - f_i)$ over joints of $f_i$ freedoms.

</Theorem>

A **four-bar linkage** has 4 links and 4 pins: $M = 9 - 8 = 1$, so one motor drives it. A triangle of three bars has $M = 0$: it is a structure, the truss of <LessonLink slug="trusses-and-frames">trusses and frames</LessonLink>. A five-bar linkage has $M = 2$ and needs two motors, which is how some drawing robots and parallel pick-and-place arms work.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Grübler's formula counts constraints; it cannot see geometry. A parallelogram linkage with a fifth bar added parallel to two of its sides has $M = 3 \times 4 - 2 \times 6 = 0$ by the formula, yet it moves freely, because the extra bar's constraint repeats one that is already there. Such linkages are called overconstrained. They work only if made accurately, and real ones often bind when the parts are slightly out of true.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="four-bar" kind="intuition" title="The four-bar linkage">

The four-bar is the simplest and most useful closed linkage: the **ground** link (length $r_1$) between two fixed pivots, an input **crank** ($r_2$), the **coupler** ($r_3$) that floats between the moving pins, and an output **rocker** or follower ($r_4$). Any point on the coupler, extended as a triangle or plate, traces a **coupler curve**, and these curves are wonderfully varied: ovals, figures of eight, teardrops and shapes with nearly straight stretches.

Whether the crank can turn all the way round depends only on the link lengths. Let $s$ and $l$ be the shortest and longest links and $p$, $q$ the other two.

<Theorem name="Grashof condition">

If $s + l \le p + q$, at least one link can rotate fully relative to the others (a **Grashof** linkage). Then the linkage is a **crank–rocker** if the shortest link is the crank, a **double crank** (drag link) if the shortest link is the ground, and a **double rocker** if the shortest link is the coupler. If $s + l > p + q$, no link can turn fully: every motion is a rocking motion.

</Theorem>

When $s + l = p + q$ exactly, the linkage passes through a flat *change point* where it can switch between assembly modes, a configuration designers usually avoid. Grashof published the condition in 1883; Franz Reuleaux's large collection of teaching models from the same period, now at Cornell University, still shows these motions beautifully.

### Solving the position: loop closure

Treat each link as a vector. Going round the loop from $O_2$ via $A$ and $B$ to $O_4$ and back along the ground brings you to where you started:

$$r_2 e^{i\theta_2} + r_3 e^{i\theta_3} - r_4 e^{i\theta_4} - r_1 = 0.$$

Its real and imaginary parts are two nonlinear equations in the two unknown angles $\theta_3$ and $\theta_4$. A closed-form solution exists (Freudenstein's equation of 1954 is the classic), but the numerical approach generalises to every linkage: guess the angles, linearise the equations with their Jacobian matrix, solve for a correction, and repeat. That is **Newton's method**, and it converges in a handful of steps from a good guess. Stepping the crank in small increments and starting each solve from the last answer keeps the solution on the same **assembly branch**: for most input angles there are two ways to assemble the linkage (open and crossed), and a careless solver can jump between them.

The **transmission angle** $\mu$, between coupler and rocker, measures how much of the coupler's force turns the rocker. Near 90° it is ideal; near 0° or 180° the coupler pushes almost along the rocker and the linkage binds or chatters. A common rule of thumb keeps $\mu$ between about 40° and 140°.

</Section>

<Section id="computation-four-bar" kind="computation" title="Computing it: mobility and the four-bar by Newton's method">

```python run
import numpy as np

def mobility(n_links, full_joints, half_joints=0):
    """Grubler-Kutzbach mobility of a planar mechanism (the ground counts as a link)."""
    return 3 * (n_links - 1) - 2 * full_joints - half_joints

print("four-bar linkage      M =", mobility(4, 4))
print("slider-crank          M =", mobility(4, 4))     # 3 pins + 1 sliding joint
print("five-bar linkage      M =", mobility(5, 5))
print("triangle (a truss)    M =", mobility(3, 3))
print("cam with roller       M =", mobility(4, 3, 1))   # the roller's own spin is a passive freedom

# A crank-rocker four-bar (mm): ground r1, crank r2, coupler r3, rocker r4
r1, r2, r3, r4 = 100.0, 30.0, 90.0, 70.0
s, l = min(r1, r2, r3, r4), max(r1, r2, r3, r4)
p_plus_q = r1 + r2 + r3 + r4 - s - l
print(f"\nGrashof: s + l = {s + l:.0f} <= p + q = {p_plus_q:.0f}? {s + l <= p_plus_q}")

def solve_position(t2, guess, tol=1e-12):
    """Newton-Raphson on the loop-closure equations for (theta3, theta4)."""
    t3, t4 = guess
    for it in range(50):
        f = np.array([r2 * np.cos(t2) + r3 * np.cos(t3) - r4 * np.cos(t4) - r1,
                      r2 * np.sin(t2) + r3 * np.sin(t3) - r4 * np.sin(t4)])
        J = np.array([[-r3 * np.sin(t3),  r4 * np.sin(t4)],
                      [ r3 * np.cos(t3), -r4 * np.cos(t4)]])
        step = np.linalg.solve(J, -f)
        t3, t4 = t3 + step[0], t4 + step[1]
        if np.max(np.abs(step)) < tol:
            return t3, t4, it + 1
    raise RuntimeError("Newton did not converge")

guess = (np.radians(40), np.radians(100))       # a rough sketch of the open configuration
print("\ncrank   coupler   rocker   transmission   Newton")
print("theta2  theta3    theta4   angle mu       steps")
for deg in range(0, 360, 30):
    t2 = np.radians(deg)
    t3, t4, its = solve_position(t2, guess)
    guess = (t3, t4)                              # start the next step from here: stays on this branch
    mu = np.degrees(t4 - t3)                      # transmission angle: between coupler and rocker
    print(f"{deg:5d}  {np.degrees(t3):7.1f}  {np.degrees(t4):7.1f}   {mu:9.1f}      {its}")

# Fine sweep for the rocker's swing and the extreme transmission angles
t2s = np.radians(np.arange(0, 360.5, 0.5))
guess = solve_position(0.0, (np.radians(40), np.radians(100)))[:2]
t4s, mus = [], []
for t2 in t2s:
    t3, t4, _ = solve_position(t2, guess)
    guess = (t3, t4)
    t4s.append(np.degrees(t4))
    mus.append(np.degrees(t4 - t3))
print(f"\nrocker swings from {min(t4s):.1f} to {max(t4s):.1f} deg: {max(t4s) - min(t4s):.1f} deg of swing")
print(f"transmission angle (coupler to rocker) ranges from {min(mus):.1f} to {max(mus):.1f} deg")
```

The counts come out as expected, including $M = 2$ for the cam with a roller follower: the extra freedom is the roller spinning on its pin, a *passive* freedom that does not affect the follower's motion. The linkage satisfies Grashof ($130 \le 160$) with the shortest link as the crank, so it is a crank–rocker. Newton's method converges in five steps at every crank angle in the table, the rocker swings through 51.8° (from 92.0° to 143.8°), and the transmission angle stays between 50.0° and 108.0°, comfortably inside the 40°–140° rule of thumb.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Five Newton steps to twelve-digit accuracy is quadratic convergence at work: each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits. The same loop, with more unknowns, is what CAD assembly solvers and robot inverse-kinematics routines run thousands of times a second.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="coupler-curves" kind="examples" title="Coupler curves and straight lines">

Before electric motors and linear guides, making a point move in a straight line was a serious problem: a steam engine's piston rod had to move straight, but the beam that drove it swung in an arc. James Watt's "parallel motion" of 1784 was a linkage whose coupler point moves almost straight over a useful stroke; he was proud of it all his life. Pafnuty Chebyshev in Russia studied how well such linkages could approximate a line, which led him to the polynomials named after him. Today, nearly-straight coupler curves are still used where a cheap pinned linkage must replace a sliding guide.

One elegant example is **Hoeken's linkage**, a crank–rocker with ground 2, crank 1, coupler 2.5 and rocker 2.5 units, whose coupler point lies on the coupler extended to 5 units from the crank pin. The program solves its position in closed form, by intersecting two circles, and plots the curve.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Hoeken's linkage: a crank-rocker whose coupler point traces a nearly straight line.
# Proportions: ground 2, crank 1, coupler 2.5, rocker 2.5; the coupler point P lies on the
# coupler line extended, 5 units from the crank pin. Scale: 1 unit = 40 mm.
u = 40.0
r1, r2, r3, r4, rp = 2 * u, 1 * u, 2.5 * u, 2.5 * u, 5 * u

def coupler_point(t2):
    """Closed-form position: crank pin A, rocker pin B by intersecting two circles, then P."""
    A = np.array([r2 * np.cos(t2), r2 * np.sin(t2)])
    O4 = np.array([r1, 0.0])
    d = np.linalg.norm(O4 - A)
    a = (r3**2 - r4**2 + d**2) / (2 * d)          # distance from A along A->O4 to the chord
    h = np.sqrt(r3**2 - a**2)
    e = (O4 - A) / d
    B = A + a * e + h * np.array([-e[1], e[0]])   # the upper of the two assemblies
    return A + rp * (B - A) / r3

t = np.radians(np.arange(0, 360, 2.0))
P = np.array([coupler_point(ti) for ti in t])

# Small ASCII plot of the coupler curve (x to the right, y upwards; y is stretched about 3x)
W, H = 64, 20
x0, x1 = P[:, 0].min(), P[:, 0].max()
y0, y1 = P[:, 1].min(), P[:, 1].max()
grid = [[" "] * W for _ in range(H)]
for x, y in P:
    col = int((x - x0) / (x1 - x0) * (W - 1))
    row = int((y1 - y) / (y1 - y0) * (H - 1))
    grid[row][col] = "*"
print("coupler curve of Hoeken's linkage:")
for row in grid:
    print("|" + "".join(row) + "|")
print(f"x from {x0:.0f} to {x1:.0f} mm, y from {y0:.0f} to {y1:.0f} mm")

# How straight is the flat part? Take the points over the crank angles where P moves
# along it, and fit a straight line.
flat = (np.degrees(t) >= 120) & (np.degrees(t) <= 240)
xs, ys = P[flat, 0], P[flat, 1]
k, c = np.polyfit(xs, ys, 1)
dev = ys - (k * xs + c)
print(f"\ncrank 120-240 deg: P travels {xs.max() - xs.min():.0f} mm along x")
print(f"best-fit line slope {k:.4f}; largest deviation from it {np.abs(dev).max():.2f} mm")
speed = np.hypot(np.diff(xs), np.diff(ys))
print(f"distance per 2-degree step: {speed.min():.2f} to {speed.max():.2f} mm (nearly constant speed)")
```

The curve is a flattened loop with one nearly straight side. While the crank turns through the third of a revolution from 120° to 240°, the coupler point travels 109 mm along a line from which it never strays by more than 0.22 mm, and at an almost constant speed: 1.79 to 1.87 mm per 2° of crank. That makes it useful for walking machines, where the straight stretch is the foot on the ground, and for feeding mechanisms that must push material at a steady rate. Theo Jansen's wind-driven *Strandbeest* walkers on Dutch beaches use a more elaborate single-freedom leg linkage, found by computer search, on the same principle.

Finding link lengths that make a coupler point pass through chosen positions is **linkage synthesis**, a field founded by Ludwig Burmester in the 1880s, now done with numerical optimisation and with algebraic methods that find every solution of the synthesis equations.

</Section>

<Section id="slider-crank-cams" kind="human" title="Slider–cranks and cams">

### The slider–crank

Replace the rocker of a four-bar by a slider and you have the **slider–crank**, the mechanism of every piston engine, reciprocating compressor and pump. With crank radius $r$, connecting rod $l$ and crank angle $\theta$ from top dead centre, the piston pin's distance from the crank centre is

$$x = r\cos\theta + \sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta}.$$

The piston speeds up and slows down twice per revolution, and because the rod is not infinitely long, the motion is not simple harmonic: the acceleration at top dead centre is $r\omega^2(1 + r/l)$, larger than the $r\omega^2(1 - r/l)$ at bottom dead centre. These accelerations, multiplied by the piston's mass, are the shaking forces that engine designers balance with counterweights and with the arrangement of cylinders.

### Cams

A **cam** is a shaped disc or cylinder that pushes a **follower** through any motion the designer chooses: rise, dwell, return, dwell. Cams open the valves of most car engines, drive packaging and textile machines, and time the mechanisms inside clocks and music boxes. The follower may be a knife edge, a roller or a flat face, and it is held against the cam by a spring or by a groove.

Choosing the **motion law** for each rise and return is the heart of cam design. The follower's acceleration, multiplied by its mass, is a force; a sudden *jump* in acceleration is a shock that excites vibration and noise. A rise at **constant velocity** is the worst: the velocity jumps at each end, so the acceleration is momentarily infinite and the follower is hammered. A **simple harmonic** rise has smooth velocity but jumps in acceleration where it meets the dwells. A **cycloidal** rise has zero velocity and zero acceleration at both ends, at the price of a higher peak acceleration, and is the usual choice for fast cams; polynomial laws (the 3-4-5 polynomial, for example) and splines give designers finer control.

The **pressure angle**, between the follower's line of motion and the normal at the contact point, plays the part the transmission angle played for linkages. A large pressure angle pushes the follower sideways in its guide and can jam it; designers usually keep it below about 30° for a translating roller follower, and the main way to reduce it is a larger cam.

```python run
import numpy as np

# 1. Slider-crank of a small engine: crank radius r, connecting rod l, 6000 rev/min.
r, l, rpm = 0.040, 0.140, 6000.0               # m
w = 2 * np.pi * rpm / 60                       # rad/s
step = np.radians(0.25)
th = np.arange(0, 2 * np.pi, step)
x = r * np.cos(th) + np.sqrt(l**2 - (r * np.sin(th))**2)   # piston pin, measured from crank centre
dt = step / w
v = (np.roll(x, -1) - np.roll(x, 1)) / (2 * dt)            # central differences, wrapping round
a = (np.roll(x, -1) - 2 * x + np.roll(x, 1)) / dt**2
i_bdc = len(th) // 2
print(f"stroke {1000 * (x.max() - x.min()):.0f} mm, mean piston speed {2 * 2 * r * rpm / 60:.1f} m/s")
print(f"peak piston speed {np.abs(v).max():.1f} m/s at crank angle {np.degrees(th[np.argmax(np.abs(v))]):.1f} deg")
print(f"|acceleration| at top dead centre    {abs(a[0]):7,.0f} m/s^2 "
      f"(r w^2 (1 + r/l) = {r * w**2 * (1 + r / l):,.0f}), about {abs(a[0]) / 9.81:,.0f} g")
print(f"|acceleration| at bottom dead centre {abs(a[i_bdc]):7,.0f} m/s^2 (r w^2 (1 - r/l) = {r * w**2 * (1 - r / l):,.0f})")

# 2. Cam follower: a rise of h = 20 mm over beta = 120 deg of cam rotation, between two dwells.
#    s(u) with u = theta / beta from 0 to 1; derivatives with respect to the cam angle theta.
h, beta = 20.0, np.radians(120)
u = np.linspace(0, 1, 2001)
laws = {
    "constant velocity": (h * u,
                          h / beta * np.ones_like(u),
                          np.zeros_like(u)),
    "simple harmonic":   (h / 2 * (1 - np.cos(np.pi * u)),
                          h * np.pi / (2 * beta) * np.sin(np.pi * u),
                          h * np.pi**2 / (2 * beta**2) * np.cos(np.pi * u)),
    "cycloidal":         (h * (u - np.sin(2 * np.pi * u) / (2 * np.pi)),
                          h / beta * (1 - np.cos(2 * np.pi * u)),
                          2 * np.pi * h / beta**2 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * u)),
}
cam_rpm = 600
w2 = (2 * np.pi * cam_rpm / 60) ** 2
print(f"\nrise law             s' at ends   s'' at ends   peak |s''|   peak accel at {cam_rpm} rpm")
print("                     (mm/rad)     (mm/rad^2)    (mm/rad^2)   (m/s^2)")
for name, (s, ds, d2s) in laws.items():
    peak = np.inf if ds[0] > 0 else np.abs(d2s).max()   # a velocity jump means an infinite acceleration
    print(f"{name:18s}   {ds[0]:9.2f}   {d2s[0]:10.2f}    {peak:9.2f}    {peak * w2 / 1000:8.1f}")
print("(the dwells on either side have s' = s'' = 0, so any non-zero value at the ends is a jump)")

# 3. Pressure angle of an in-line roller follower: tan(phi) = s' / (Rp + s)
s, ds, _ = laws["cycloidal"]
for Rp in [30.0, 40.0, 60.0]:                  # prime-circle radius, mm
    phi = np.degrees(np.arctan(ds / (Rp + s)))
    print(f"prime circle {Rp:4.0f} mm: largest pressure angle on the cycloidal rise {phi.max():.1f} deg")
```

The engine has an 80 mm stroke and a mean piston speed of 16 m/s, but the piston peaks at 26.1 m/s at 75° after top dead centre, not at 90°, because of the rod's angularity. At top dead centre it decelerates at about 20 300 m/s², over 2000 times the acceleration of gravity, against 11 280 m/s² at bottom dead centre. For the cam, the constant-velocity rise is ruled out by its velocity jump; the harmonic rise has the lowest peak acceleration, 89 m/s² at 600 rev/min, but jumps to it from zero; the cycloidal rise peaks higher, at 113 m/s², but starts and ends smoothly. Its pressure angle reaches 26.1° on a 30 mm prime circle and falls to 15.4° on a 60 mm one.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Those numbers assume a perfectly rigid follower train. A real valve train is a spring–mass system, and if the cam excites one of its natural frequencies, as in <LessonLink slug="vibrations">vibrations</LessonLink>, the follower can leave the cam and come down hard, "valve float". Smooth acceleration laws reduce the excitation, but the spring must still be stiff enough to keep contact at the highest speed.

<Section id="gear-trains" kind="definitions" title="Gear trains and planetary gears">

The gears of <LessonLink slug="machine-elements">machine elements</LessonLink> combine into **trains**. In a **simple train**, each shaft carries one gear and the idlers in between only change the direction; the ratio is set by the first and last gears. In a **compound train**, a shaft carries two gears of different sizes, and the ratios of the meshing pairs multiply: three stages of 3:1, 3:1 and 4:1 give 36:1. A **reverted** compound train brings the output back onto the input's axis, as in a clock or a lathe gearbox.

<Definition term="planetary (epicyclic) gear set">

A **sun** gear in the centre meshes with several **planet** gears, which also mesh with an internally toothed **ring**; the planets' pins are carried by a **carrier**. With tooth counts $z_s$ and $z_r$ (where, for standard gears, $z_r = z_s + 2z_p$), the speeds of sun, ring and carrier obey the **Willis equation**

$$\frac{\omega_s - \omega_c}{\omega_r - \omega_c} = -\frac{z_r}{z_s},$$

which is the fixed-axis ratio seen by an observer riding on the carrier. The set has two degrees of freedom: hold one member (or drive two) and the third's speed follows.

</Definition>

```python run
from fractions import Fraction as Fr

# Compound train: ratios of the meshing pairs multiply.
pairs = [(17, 51), (20, 60), (18, 72)]          # (driver teeth, driven teeth)
ratio = 1
for zd, zn in pairs:
    ratio *= Fr(zn, zd)
print(f"compound train {pairs}: overall ratio {ratio} = {float(ratio):.1f} : 1")

# A planetary (epicyclic) gear set: sun S, three planets P, ring R, carrier C.
zs, zp = 24, 30
zr = zs + 2 * zp                                 # the planets must fit between sun and ring
N = 3
print(f"\nsun {zs}, planets {zp}, ring {zr} teeth; (zs + zr) / N = {(zs + zr) / N:g}"
      f" -> {'equally spaced planets fit' if (zs + zr) % N == 0 else 'planets cannot be equally spaced'}")

def willis(ws=None, wr=None, wc=None):
    """Solve (ws - wc) / (wr - wc) = -zr / zs for the one unknown speed (pass None for it)."""
    k = Fr(-zr, zs)
    if ws is None:
        return wc + k * (wr - wc)
    if wr is None:
        return wc + (ws - wc) / k
    return (ws - k * wr) / (1 - k)              # carrier

print("\ninput        output     held     ratio (input speed / output speed)")
print(f"sun          carrier    ring     {str(Fr(1) / willis(ws=1, wr=0)):>6}  = {float(1 / willis(ws=1, wr=0)):6.3f}")
print(f"ring         carrier    sun      {str(Fr(1) / willis(ws=0, wr=1)):>6}  = {float(1 / willis(ws=0, wr=1)):6.3f}")
print(f"sun          ring       carrier  {str(Fr(1) / willis(ws=1, wc=0)):>6}  = {float(1 / willis(ws=1, wc=0)):6.3f}  (reverses)")
print(f"carrier      ring       sun      {str(Fr(1) / willis(ws=0, wc=1)):>6}  = {float(1 / willis(ws=0, wc=1)):6.3f}  (overdrive)")

# With no member held, the set adds speeds: a power-split device.
for ws, wr in [(3000, 0), (3000, 1000), (-1000, 1500)]:
    print(f"sun {ws:5d} rpm, ring {wr:5d} rpm -> carrier {float(willis(ws=ws, wr=wr)):7.1f} rpm")
```

One small set gives four useful ratios: 4.5 (sun in, carrier out, ring held), 9/7 ≈ 1.29 (ring in, carrier out), −3.5 (a reverse, with the carrier held) and 7/9 ≈ 0.78 (an overdrive). An automatic gearbox chains two to four such sets and selects ratios by engaging clutches and brakes that hold or join their members, without ever taking the gears out of mesh. With nothing held, the carrier's speed is a weighted average of the sun's and ring's, $\omega_c = (z_s\omega_s + z_r\omega_r)/(z_s + z_r)$, so the set *adds* two motions; that is how a hybrid car's power-split device blends an engine and two electric machines, and how a car's differential, with bevel gears in place of the ring and sun, lets two wheels turn at different speeds round a corner. The tooth counts also pass the assembly check: three planets can be equally spaced only if $(z_s + z_r)/3$ is a whole number, here 36.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Planetary sets are popular for a simple reason: the load is shared by several planets, and the input and output are on one axis. That makes them compact and powerful for their size, which is why they sit in wind-turbine gearboxes, electric-vehicle drives, robot joints and power screwdrivers alike.

</Narrator>

<Section id="everyday" kind="history" title="Mechanisms in everyday machines and in history">

Look for these mechanisms and you will find them everywhere:

- **Windscreen wipers.** A motor-driven crank–rocker turns rotation into a sweep; a parallelogram linkage then moves the second arm in step.
- **Excavators and loaders.** Hydraulic cylinders act as prismatic joints in linkages; the bucket is curled by a four-bar that multiplies the cylinder's stroke into a large rotation.
- **Car suspensions.** A double-wishbone suspension is a four-bar in each view, designed so that the wheel moves up and down while its tilt and track change in controlled ways.
- **Bicycles.** The derailleur moves the chain on a parallelogram linkage; the pedals and legs form a slider–crank-like chain; hub gears are planetary sets.
- **Folding furniture, umbrellas, pliers and car jacks.** Linkages that change shape, multiply force or lock flat.

Mechanisms are old. Hero of Alexandria described gear trains and cam-like devices in the first century; the Antikythera mechanism, a bronze astronomical calculator from Greece of about the second century BC, has some thirty surviving gears. Ismail al-Jazari, working in the Artuqid court at Diyarbakır, described a crank and connecting rod, segmental gears and a camshaft in his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices* in 1206. Su Song's astronomical clock tower in Kaifeng, China, completed in 1088, used a chain drive and an escapement to turn an armillary sphere. Franz Reuleaux in Berlin gave the subject its modern vocabulary in 1875, and the robotics of today extends it: a robot arm is a chain of links and joints whose position is solved exactly as in this lesson, in [forward kinematics](/en/robotics/forward-kinematics/) and [inverse kinematics](/en/robotics/inverse-kinematics/).

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Grübler's formula always gives the true mobility."** It counts constraints, assuming they are independent. Special geometry (parallel links, equal lengths) can make a mechanism with $M = 0$ move, or leave extra passive freedoms such as a spinning roller.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The position of a linkage is determined by its input angle alone."** Usually there are two assembly branches (open and crossed), and a numerical solver must be kept on the right one, by continuation from the last answer.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A piston moves in simple harmonic motion."** Only with an infinitely long connecting rod. A real rod makes the motion faster near top dead centre, with peak acceleration $r\omega^2(1 + r/l)$ there.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A smooth-looking cam profile gives smooth motion."** What matters is the follower's velocity and acceleration as functions of cam angle. A constant-velocity rise looks smooth and hammers the follower at each end.

</Misconception>
