<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Everything around you was made by someone">

Pick up the nearest object: a pencil, a water bottle, a spoon. Someone decided its shape, its material, and how millions of copies could be made quickly, cheaply and well. A pencil is a rod of baked graphite and clay glued between two grooved strips of wood, which are then shaped and painted. A plastic bottle starts as a small test-tube shape that is heated and blown up, like a balloon, inside a bottle-shaped mould.

In this lesson you will meet the **design cycle**, the step-by-step way engineers solve problems; the main ways of **making** things; how to **choose materials**; how to **draw** a design so someone else can make it; and the rules that keep people safe in a **workshop**.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Shape, material and way of making are not three separate choices: each one limits the others. A metal spoon can be stamped out of a flat sheet; a plastic one is usually moulded; a wooden one is carved. The same job, three materials, three completely different ways of making it. Keep asking, for every object you see: why *this* material, made *this* way?

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Definition term="the design cycle">

1. **Problem.** Work out exactly what is needed, and write it as a list of **requirements** you can check: what it must do, how big, how heavy, how strong, how much it may cost, and who will use it.
2. **Ideas.** Sketch several different solutions, not just the first one, and compare them against the requirements.
3. **Prototype.** Make a quick, cheap version of the most promising idea: in cardboard, in modelling clay, on a 3D printer, or as a model on a computer.
4. **Test.** Check the prototype against every requirement, and measure what you can.
5. **Improve.** Use what the test taught you to change the design, and go round again.

</Definition>

<Example title="A stand for a tablet in the kitchen">

Amara follows recipes on a tablet while she cooks, but it keeps sliding flat on the worktop.

**Problem.** Her requirements: it holds a tablet 25 cm wide at about 60° from the worktop; it does not tip over when she taps the screen; it wipes clean; it fits in a drawer; and it costs very little.

**Ideas.** She sketches three: a folded triangle of card, a wooden block with a sawn slot, and a plastic stand with two hooked arms.

**Prototype and test.** The card triangle takes ten minutes to make. It holds the tablet at the right angle, but a firm tap on the top of the screen tips it over backwards, and card cannot be wiped clean.

**Improve.** She makes the base longer behind the tablet, so the weight stays over the base when she taps, and decides the final version will be printed in plastic. The second prototype passes the tap test; the printed one passes them all.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice that every requirement Amara wrote could be **checked**: an angle, a width, a tap that does or does not tip it over. "It should be good" cannot be tested, so it cannot guide a design. Turning a vague wish into measurable requirements is often the hardest, and most important, step of the whole cycle.

</Narrator>

A failed test is not a failure of the engineer. It is information, and usually the cheapest information you will ever get, because it arrived while the design was still made of card.

</Section>

<Section id="materials" kind="definitions" title="Choosing a material">

Every material is a bundle of properties: how strong it is, how stiff, how heavy, how it copes with heat and water, how easy it is to shape, how much it costs, and whether it can be reused or recycled. Choosing a material means finding the bundle that best fits the requirements.

| Material | Good at | Watch out for | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wood | Light, strong along the grain, easy to cut, grows back | Swells when wet, burns | Furniture, roof frames, models |
| Steel | Strong, stiff, cheap, weldable, widely recycled | Heavy, rusts unless protected | Bridges, car bodies, tools |
| Aluminium | Light, resists rusting, easy to extrude and machine | Dearer and less stiff than steel | Aircraft, bicycles, cans, window frames |
| Plastics | Light, easy to mould into complicated shapes | Softer, many soften when hot, some hard to recycle | Bottles, toys, phone cases |
| Glass and ceramics | Hard, resist heat and chemicals | Brittle: they crack rather than bend | Windows, cups, tiles |
| Composites (such as carbon fibre) | Very strong and stiff for their weight | Expensive, slow to make, hard to recycle | Racing bicycles, aircraft wings, wind-turbine blades |

There is rarely a perfect choice, only a good balance. A racing bicycle frame might be carbon fibre (light, but expensive); a hire bike in a city scheme is usually steel or aluminium (heavier, but tough and cheaper). Lumenary Materials goes much deeper, starting with [materials around us](/en/materials/materials-around-us/) and [choosing and reusing materials](/en/materials/choosing-and-reusing-materials/).

</Section>

### Cutting: take material away

Start with a block, sheet or bar bigger than you need, and remove everything that is not the part. **Sawing**, **drilling** and **filing** are the hand-tool versions. In factories, a **lathe** spins the material while a tool shapes it into round parts such as axles, and a **milling machine** spins the tool instead, carving flat faces, slots and pockets. Most are now **CNC** machines (computer numerical control), which move the tool along a path worked out from the design, accurate to a few hundredths of a millimetre. **Laser** and **water-jet cutters** slice flat sheets into complicated outlines. Cutting is accurate and works on almost any material, but everything cut away is waste, to be collected and recycled.

### Shaping: change the form without removing material

Solid material can be **bent**, **pressed**, **rolled**, **forged** (hammered or squeezed, often red hot) or **extruded** (pushed through a shaped hole, like toothpaste from a tube or pasta dough through a pasta maker). Car body panels are pressed from steel sheet, aluminium window frames are extruded, and tools and crankshafts are forged, which makes the metal tough. Shaping wastes very little material.

### Casting and moulding: fill a mould

Pour or push a liquid into a hollow mould, let it harden, and take it out: an ice-cube tray is a casting mould for water. Metal casting is one of the oldest technologies there is. Bronze-workers in Shang-dynasty China cast great ritual vessels over three thousand years ago, and the bronze statues of the Chola kingdom in South India, a thousand years ago, were made by the **lost-wax** method: a wax model is coated in clay, the wax is melted out, and molten bronze fills the space it left. Today engine blocks are cast in sand moulds.

Plastic parts are made by **injection moulding**: melted plastic is forced into a precise steel mould, cooled for a few seconds, and pushed out, again and again. Toy bricks, bottle tops and the cases of most electronics are made this way. The mould is expensive, but each part is then very cheap, as you will compute below. Molten metal and hot plastic are dangerous, so casting and moulding happen in foundries and factories, with protective clothing, training and machines that cannot run while a door is open, never at home.

### 3D printing: add material layer by layer

A 3D printer builds a part from the bottom up, one thin layer at a time. The common kind in schools melts a plastic filament in a hot nozzle and draws each layer like a very precise line of hot glue; others harden liquid resin with light, or fuse metal powder with a laser. Engineers call this **additive manufacturing**. No mould is needed, so every part can be different: it is ideal for prototypes, one-offs such as a custom prosthetic hand, and shapes no other process can make, such as the metal fuel nozzles inside some jet engines. It is too slow for millions of identical parts.

You will meet all four families in detail later, in <LessonLink slug="manufacturing-processes">Manufacturing processes</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="additive-manufacturing">Additive manufacturing</LessonLink>.

Each **view** looks straight at one face of the part: from the front, from above, from the side. Together they show every edge; dashed lines show hidden edges, like the hole through the base seen from the front. Sizes are in **millimetres**, and a good drawing also says how accurate each must be. A hole marked "10 ± 0.1" may be anywhere from 9.9 to 10.1 mm across. Most designs begin as sketches on paper and then move into **CAD** (computer-aided design) software. In CAD you build the part as a 3D model, change a size and watch the whole model update, check that parts fit together, test them with simulations, and produce the drawings automatically. The same model can be sent to a 3D printer or turned into tool paths for a CNC machine. Beginners can start with free tools such as [Tinkercad](https://www.tinkercad.com/), which runs in a web browser, or the open-source [FreeCAD](https://www.freecad.org/); professionals also use commercial packages from companies in France, Germany, the United States and elsewhere. <Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Print it or mould it?">

A school robotics club has designed a phone stand and wants to make some. Should they 3D print each one, or pay for an injection mould? Printing has no start-up cost but each stand is fairly expensive. Moulding needs a costly steel mould first, but then each stand is very cheap. The program compares them for different numbers of stands. Press **Run**:

```python run
# A school club has designed a plastic phone stand. Should it be 3D printed or moulded?
# These prices are invented for the example, but they are the right kind of size (euros).
mould_cost = 6000.00     # making the steel mould for injection moulding: paid once
moulded_each = 0.40      # plastic and machine time for each moulded stand
printed_each = 3.00      # plastic, electricity and printer time for each printed stand

print("stands   3D printing    moulding    cost of each      cheaper")
print("made       (total)      (total)     moulded stand")
for n in [1, 10, 100, 1000, 2000, 3000, 10000, 100000]:
    printing = n * printed_each
    moulding = mould_cost + n * moulded_each
    cheaper = "printing" if printing < moulding else "moulding"
    print(f"{n:6}  {printing:11.2f}  {moulding:11.2f}  {moulding / n:12.2f}      {cheaper}")

# Where do the two lines cross? Printing costs 3.00 - 0.40 = 2.60 more per stand,
# so the mould has paid for itself after 6000 / 2.60 stands.
break_even = mould_cost / (printed_each - moulded_each)
print(f"\nBoth ways cost the same at about {break_even:.0f} stands.")

# And how long does one printed stand take? The printer builds it one thin layer at a time.
height = 60.0            # millimetres
layer = 0.2              # millimetres per layer
seconds_per_layer = 30
layers = round(height / layer)
minutes = layers * seconds_per_layer / 60
print(f"One stand: {layers} layers, about {minutes:.0f} minutes ({minutes / 60:.1f} hours) of printing.")
```

For the club's first 10 stands, printing costs 30 euros and moulding over 6000: printing wins easily. But every moulded stand saves 2.60 euros, so after about **2308** stands the mould has paid for itself, and for 100 000 stands each moulded one costs just 46 cents. On a graph, each way of making is a straight line of total cost against the number of stands, and the break-even point is where the two lines cross, an idea you can explore in <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-relationships">linear relationships and simultaneous equations</TopicLink>. That is why prototypes and small batches are printed, while the toys, bottle tops and phone cases sold in shops are moulded.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Cost is not the only thing that changes with numbers; time does too. At 2.5 hours each, 10 000 stands would keep one printer busy for 25 000 hours, nearly three years without a break, while an injection-moulding machine makes a part every few seconds to a minute. Try changing `mould_cost` to 20000 and see how far the break-even point moves.

</Narrator>

<Section id="safety" kind="human" title="Staying safe in a workshop">

Workshops are wonderful places, and they are also where tools that cut wood and metal can cut people. Every rule below exists because someone, somewhere, was hurt without it.

- **Protect your eyes.** Wear safety glasses or goggles whenever anything is being cut, drilled, sanded, hammered or snapped, by you or by anyone near you. Chips and splinters fly fast, in odd directions.
- **Work with supervision and training.** Use a tool only after someone has shown you how, and only with an adult or trained supervisor present. Power tools and machines are for people trained on them.
- **Respect the guards.** Saws, drills, lathes and laser cutters have **guards**, covers that keep hands away from moving and cutting parts. Many have **interlocks** that stop the machine when a door opens, and a big red **emergency stop** button. Never remove or get round a guard.
- **Nothing loose near spinning parts.** Tie back long hair, roll up loose sleeves, and take off scarves and dangling jewellery: a rotating drill or lathe can catch them and pull you in.
- **Clamp the work.** Clamp the piece you are cutting or drilling, cut away from your body, and keep your fingers well behind the blade.
- **Keep it tidy and breathe clean air.** Clear offcuts and spills from the floor, and use dust extraction, a mask or good ventilation when sanding, gluing, painting or 3D printing.
- **Know what is hot.** A 3D printer's nozzle and bed, a soldering iron, a hot-glue gun and freshly drilled metal can all burn.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Why can't you just be careful instead of using a guard? Because human reactions take about a fifth of a second. A lathe chuck 160 mm across, turning at 1000 revolutions per minute, goes round more than three times in that fifth of a second, and its rim moves at about 8 metres per second. Nobody can pull back in time; a guard does not need to.

</Narrator>

**What you can safely make.** With an adult, you can do a great deal: cardboard models joined with tape and glue; models from balsa or pine strip cut with a junior hacksaw in a clamped mitre box; card prototypes cut with scissors, or with a craft knife and a metal safety ruler on a cutting mat, cutting away from your fingers; and parts designed in CAD and made on a 3D printer, used exactly as its maker intends, with any enclosure closed and the room aired. School workshops, makerspaces and engineering clubs can teach you more, with the right equipment and supervision. Lumenary EE's [building and measuring safely](/en/ee/building-and-measuring-safely/) gives the matching rules for electronics.

</Section>

<Section id="engineers" kind="history" title="Engineers as problem-solvers">

Mechanical engineers work wherever things move, carry loads, get hot or need making: hospitals, farms, factories, power stations, vehicle makers, space agencies and food plants. What connects them is the design cycle. Four stories from four continents:

- **The Jaipur Foot (India, 1968).** Ram Chandra Sharma, a craftsman, and Dr Pramod Karan Sethi, a surgeon, designed a cheap artificial foot of rubber and wood, made locally, that let people walk barefoot, squat and sit cross-legged, which imported feet did not allow. Their requirements came from how their patients actually lived.
- **The Wright brothers' wind tunnel (United States, 1901).** When their gliders flew worse than published tables predicted, Wilbur and Orville Wright built a small wind tunnel in their bicycle workshop and tested about two hundred model wings. Two years later they made the first powered aeroplane flights.
- **The kingfisher train (Japan, 1990s).** High-speed trains leaving tunnels made a loud boom. Eiji Nakatsu, an engineer and bird-watcher, thought of how a kingfisher dives into water with hardly a splash; the long, beak-like nose of the 500 Series Shinkansen, tested and refined many times, made the train quieter and more efficient.
- **A windmill from scrap (Malawi, 2000s).** As a teenager, William Kamkwamba studied a physics book from a village library and built a small wind turbine from bicycle parts, a tractor fan and wooden poles, which lit his family's home.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Good engineers get it right first time."** Good engineers expect to go round the design cycle many times, and make early prototypes quick and cheap so that mistakes cost little.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"3D printing will replace every other way of making things."** It is excellent for prototypes, one-offs and complicated shapes, but for large numbers of identical parts, moulding, pressing and casting are far faster and cheaper, as the program showed.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Safety glasses are only for the person using the machine."** Chips and splinters fly well beyond the machine. Everyone in the area of cutting, drilling or grinding should protect their eyes.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"CAD software designs the part for you."** CAD is a powerful drawing and checking tool, but the engineer still decides the requirements, the shape, the material and how it will be made. A beautiful 3D model can still be impossible to manufacture.

</Misconception>

</Section>
