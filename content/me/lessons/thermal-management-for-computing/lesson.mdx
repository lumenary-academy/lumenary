<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A kilowatt on a postage stamp">

A kitchen hob ring turns 2 kW into heat over about 200 cm², some 10 W/cm², and glows red doing it. An AI accelerator of 2026 turns about 1 kW into heat in a package whose silicon has an area of perhaps 16 cm², over 60 W/cm² on average and several times that at local hot spots, and it must keep that silicon below about 90–105 °C, ideally much cooler, for years. As [Hardware for AI](/en/ee/ai-hardware/) in Lumenary EE describes, one rack of 72 such accelerators draws about 120 kW, ten times a typical rack of a decade earlier, and designs of several hundred kilowatts per rack are announced for 2027.

Every one of those watts becomes heat. Getting it out is a mechanical engineering problem from end to end: conduction through layers a fraction of a millimetre thick, convection into air or water, phase change in heat pipes and boiling liquids, pumps, pipes and heat exchangers, and finally rejection to the outdoors or to a city's heating network. The Lumenary EE lesson on [powering data centres](/en/ee/powering-data-centres/) follows the electricity in and introduces PUE and WUE. This lesson follows the heat out, with the tools of <LessonLink slug="heat-transfer">heat transfer</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="viscous-flow-and-pipes">viscous flow</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="why-hot" kind="intuition" title="Why chips get hot, and why it matters">

A CMOS chip's power is mostly **dynamic power**, $P \approx \alpha C V^2 f$: every clock cycle a fraction $\alpha$ of its switched capacitance $C$ is charged to the supply voltage $V$ and discharged again, $f$ times a second; to that is added **leakage** through transistors that are nominally off. For decades, shrinking transistors lowered $C$ and $V$ together and kept power per unit area constant. Since about 2005 the supply voltage has barely fallen, so packing in more transistors raises the power density; [transistor scaling](/en/ee/transistor-scaling/) tells that story. AI accelerators add a second push: they keep thousands of multipliers busy at once, and their makers gain performance by spending more power.

Temperature matters for three reasons:

- **Reliability.** Wear-out mechanisms in chips, such as electromigration in the wiring, speed up roughly exponentially with temperature (Arrhenius behaviour), so a few kelvin can change lifetime noticeably.
- **Leakage.** Leakage current grows with temperature, which raises power, which raises temperature: a positive feedback that cooling must keep stable.
- **Performance.** Modern processors measure their own temperature and slow their clocks when it climbs. Better cooling buys sustained speed.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The difficulty is not the kilowatt; a kettle handles that. It is the kilowatt's **density**: the heat has to cross a few square centimetres before it can spread out. Almost everything in this lesson is a way of spreading heat sideways quickly, carrying it away with a fluid that can absorb a lot of energy per kelvin, or both.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="chain" kind="definitions" title="The thermal resistance chain">

<Definition term="Thermal resistance">

For a steady heat flow $Q$ through a path whose ends differ in temperature by $\Delta T$, the thermal resistance is $R = \Delta T/Q$, in K/W. A plane layer of thickness $t$, conductivity $k$ and area $A$ has $R = t/(kA)$; a surface with convection coefficient $h$ has $R = 1/(hA)$; a contact between two surfaces has an **interface resistance** $R''/A$, where $R''$ (in m²·K/W, often quoted in cm²·K/W) characterises the joint. Resistances in series add.

</Definition>

The heat from a transistor's junction crosses a chain of layers, each with its own resistance. The package makers' standard metrics, defined by JEDEC's JESD51 series, include $\theta_{JC}$ (junction to case) and $\theta_{JA}$ (junction to ambient, in a specified test set-up).

The program below assembles the chain for a 1 kW accelerator with a lid and a cold plate. Every value is illustrative but in the range published for such packages: a good polymer interface material of 0.10 cm²·K/W, a finned copper cold plate with an effective heat-transfer coefficient of $10^5$ W/(m²·K) over its footprint, and coolant (25% propylene glycol in water) flowing at 1.5 L/min per kilowatt. Hot spots are modelled crudely: where the local heat flux is three times the average, the two layers nearest the die see three times the temperature gradient.

```python run
# Junction-to-coolant thermal resistance chain of a liquid-cooled accelerator (illustrative values)
A_die, A_plate = 16e-4, 25e-4                # m^2: silicon area; area the lid and cold plate spread over
R_tim = 0.10e-4                              # m^2 K/W (0.10 cm^2 K/W): a good polymer thermal interface
lidded = [  # (layer, resistance in K/W)
    ("silicon die, 0.5 mm",          0.5e-3 / (130 * A_die)),
    ("TIM1, die to lid",             R_tim / A_die),
    ("copper lid, 2 mm",             2e-3 / (390 * A_die)),     # 1-D through the die footprint: pessimistic
    ("TIM2, lid to cold plate",      R_tim / A_plate),
    ("cold plate base, 2 mm Cu",     2e-3 / (390 * A_plate)),
    ("convection to the coolant",    1 / (1.0e5 * A_plate)),    # effective h of a finned plate, per footprint
]
R = sum(r for _, r in lidded)
Q, lpm_per_kW, rho, cp = 1000.0, 1.5, 1020.0, 3900.0           # coolant: 25 % propylene glycol in water
m_dot = Q / 1000 * lpm_per_kW / 60 / 1000 * rho
rise = Q / (m_dot * cp)                                         # coolant temperature rise across the plate
print(f"{'layer':30s} {'R (K/W)':>8s}  {'dT at 1 kW':>10s}")
for name, r in lidded:
    print(f"{name:30s} {r:8.4f}  {Q * r:8.1f} K")
print(f"{'conduction + convection':30s} {R:8.4f}  {Q * R:8.1f} K")
print(f"coolant at {lpm_per_kW} L/min per kW warms by {rise:.1f} K (mean junction sees half of it)")

# Hot spots: if the local heat flux is 3x the average, the layers nearest the die see 3x the gradient
hot = 3.0
def junction_rise(P, R_chain, R_near):
    return P * R_chain + (hot - 1) * P * R_near + rise / 2      # flow scaled with power: rise is constant

near = lidded[0][1] + lidded[1][1]
lidless = [lidded[0][1], R_tim / A_die, lidded[4][1], lidded[5][1]]   # cold plate on the bare die
print(f"\nlidless stack (die, one TIM, plate, convection): {sum(lidless):.4f} K/W\n")
print(" power (W)   junction rise (K)      highest coolant inlet for a 95 C junction")
print("             lidded   lidless       lidded    lidless")
for P in (700, 1000, 1400, 2000):
    a, b = junction_rise(P, R, near), junction_rise(P, sum(lidless), near)
    print(f"  {P:6d}    {a:6.1f}   {b:6.1f}        {95 - a:6.1f} C  {95 - b:6.1f} C")
```

The chain totals 0.022 K/W, about 22 K at 1 kW, and the largest single term is the thin interface between die and lid: 0.1 mm of material costs more temperature than 2 mm of copper. That is why the industry fights over interface materials (indium and other metal solders, liquid metals, graphite and carbon-nanotube pads) and why many accelerators now ship **lidless**, with the cold plate pressed onto the bare die through one interface. Here that saves 0.007 K/W.

The bottom table is the heart of the matter. The junction must stay below its limit (95 °C here), so the chain fixes the warmest coolant the chip can accept. At 700 W the lidded package tolerates coolant at 62.5 °C; at 2 kW, only 11.5 °C, which means mechanical refrigeration all year in any climate, unless the stack improves. The lidless stack buys back about 14 K at 2 kW. Every kelvin of resistance removed from the chain becomes a kelvin of warmer coolant, and, as the last computation in this lesson shows, warmer coolant is what makes chiller-free cooling, low water use and heat reuse possible.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The one-dimensional chain is a first estimate, not a design. It ignores heat spreading sideways in the lid and plate (which helps), the non-uniform power map of a real chip (the factor of three is a guess), the coolant warming along the plate so that dies near the outlet run hotter, the ageing of interface materials (pump-out and drying of greases under thermal cycling), and the manufacturing scatter in bond-line thickness. Real designs are checked with conjugate heat-transfer CFD and with thermal test chips that carry heaters and sensors in a known pattern.

</Narrator>

<Section id="air" kind="computation" title="Air: heat sinks, fins and fans">

Most of the world's chips, from phones to laptops to the CPUs in most servers, are still cooled by air. A heat sink spreads heat from a small base into many thin fins, multiplying the area exposed to the air; a fan pushes air between them. Two classic results from <LessonLink slug="heat-transfer">heat transfer</LessonLink> decide its performance. A fin of height $H$ and thickness $t_f$ conducts heat outwards while losing it from its faces, so its tip is cooler than its root, and its **fin efficiency** is $\eta_f = \tanh(mH)/(mH)$ with $m = \sqrt{2h/(k t_f)}$. And the air warms as it absorbs heat: with heat-capacity rate $C = \rho Q c_p$ and $hA$ the effective conductance of the fins, the heat sink's resistance from base to inlet air is $R = 1/\big[C\,(1 - e^{-hA/C})\big]$, which can never be less than $1/C$.

More fins add area and narrow the gaps, which raises the heat-transfer coefficient, but narrow gaps also resist the flow, and a fan delivers less air against more resistance. The flow settles where the fan curve meets the system curve, exactly as for the pumps in <LessonLink slug="viscous-flow-and-pipes">viscous flow and pipes</LessonLink>. The program finds the best number of fins for a 100 mm × 100 mm aluminium heat sink with 40 mm fins, cooled by a fan whose curve is a simple parabola, carrying 300 W.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Plate-fin heat sink in a duct, blown by a fan: how many fins?
W, L, H, tf = 0.100, 0.100, 0.040, 0.8e-3      # base width, flow length, fin height, fin thickness (m)
k_al = 200.0                                   # aluminium alloy, W/(m K)
rho, cp, mu, k_air, Pr = 1.16, 1007.0, 1.85e-5, 0.0263, 0.71   # air near 30 C
dp_max, Q_max = 150.0, 0.015                   # fan curve: dp = dp_max (1 - (Q/Q_max)^2), Pa and m^3/s
P_chip = 300.0                                 # W

def heat_sink(N):
    s = (W - N * tf) / (N - 1)                 # gap between fins
    A_flow = (N - 1) * s * H
    Dh = 2 * s * H / (s + H)                   # hydraulic diameter of one channel
    def system_dp(Q):                          # laminar friction (parallel plates, f Re = 96) + entry and exit
        V = Q / A_flow
        return (96 * mu / (rho * V * Dh) * L / Dh + 1.5) * 0.5 * rho * V**2
    lo, hi = 1e-6, Q_max                       # operating point: fan curve meets system curve (bisection)
    for _ in range(60):
        Q = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        lo, hi = (Q, hi) if dp_max * (1 - (Q / Q_max) ** 2) > system_dp(Q) else (lo, Q)
    V = Q / A_flow
    Re = rho * V * Dh / mu
    Gz = Dh / L * Re * Pr                      # Graetz number: how far from fully developed
    h = (7.54 + 0.03 * Gz / (1 + 0.016 * Gz ** (2 / 3))) * k_air / Dh   # laminar, entry region included
    m = np.sqrt(2 * h / (k_al * tf))
    eta_f = np.tanh(m * H) / (m * H)           # fin efficiency
    hA = h * (eta_f * 2 * N * H * L + (W - N * tf) * L)
    C = rho * Q * cp                           # heat-capacity rate of the air, W/K
    R = 1 / (C * (1 - np.exp(-hA / C)))        # base to inlet air, including the air's own warming
    return s, Q, Re, h, eta_f, system_dp(Q), P_chip / C, R

print(" fins  gap(mm)  flow(L/s)    Re   h(W/m2K)  fin eff  dp(Pa)  air rise(K)  R(K/W)")
results = {}
for N in (20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 70):
    s, Q, Re, h, eta, dp, rise, R = heat_sink(N)
    results[N] = R
    print(f" {N:4d}  {s * 1e3:6.2f}   {Q * 1e3:7.2f}  {Re:5.0f}  {h:7.1f}   {eta:6.3f}  {dp:5.0f}  {rise:9.1f}   {R:7.4f}")
N_best = min(results, key=results.get)
print(f"\nbest: {N_best} fins, {results[N_best]:.3f} K/W, so the base runs "
      f"{results[N_best] * P_chip:.0f} K above the inlet air at {P_chip:.0f} W")
```

From 20 to 50 fins the resistance falls from 0.25 to 0.098 K/W: more area, and a higher $h$ in the narrower gaps. Beyond about 50 fins the fan can no longer push enough air through the 1 mm gaps, the flow collapses, and the resistance rises again. At the optimum the air leaving the fins is already 25 K warmer than it entered, close to the $1/C$ limit: the heat sink is running out of *air*, not of area.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Scale that up to the 1 kW accelerator. Even a perfect heat sink with no thermal resistance at all needs enough air to carry the heat: at a 20 K rise, $Q = P/(\rho c_p \Delta T) \approx 1000/(1.16 \times 1007 \times 20) \approx 0.043$ m³/s per chip, about 3 m³/s for a rack of 72, through a cabinet the size of a wardrobe. Water, with about 3500 times the volumetric heat capacity, does it with 1.5 L/min per kilowatt. That ratio, more than anything else, is why AI racks went liquid.

</Narrator>

<Section id="heat-pipes" kind="computation" title="Heat pipes, vapour chambers and boiling">

A **heat pipe** is a sealed tube, usually copper with water inside at low pressure, lined with a **wick** of sintered copper powder, grooves or mesh. At the hot end (the evaporator) the water boils; the vapour rushes to the cold end (the condenser), condenses and gives up its latent heat; and capillary forces in the wick pull the liquid back. Because it moves heat as latent heat with only a small temperature drop, a heat pipe can have an effective conductivity tens to hundreds of times that of solid copper. A **vapour chamber** is a flat heat pipe that spreads heat in two dimensions under a hot chip, and is common under high-power processors, in phones and in gaming laptops.

Two limits matter most. The **capillary limit**: the wick's maximum capillary pressure, $2\sigma/r_\text{eff}$ for surface tension $\sigma$ and effective pore radius $r_\text{eff}$, must drive the liquid through the wick (Darcy flow, with permeability $K$) against any gravity head. The working fluid enters through its **merit number** $\rho_l \sigma h_{fg}/\mu_l$; water's is the highest of any common fluid between about 30 and 200 °C. And the **boiling limit**: a heated surface under a pool of liquid can only shed heat by boiling up to the **critical heat flux** (CHF), beyond which a vapour blanket insulates it and its temperature jumps. Novak Zuber's hydrodynamic theory of 1958 gives

$$q''_\text{max} = C\,h_{fg}\,\rho_v^{1/2}\big[\sigma g (\rho_l - \rho_v)\big]^{1/4}, \qquad C \approx 0.131,$$

for a large flat heater.

```python run
import math
g = 9.81

# 1. Capillary limit of a copper-water heat pipe with a sintered wick (illustrative geometry)
rho_l, mu_l, sigma, h_fg = 983.0, 4.67e-4, 0.0662, 2.358e6      # water at 60 C
merit = rho_l * sigma * h_fg / mu_l                                # the liquid's figure of merit, W/m^2
K, r_eff = 3e-11, 40e-6                                          # wick permeability (m^2), pore radius (m)
A_wick = math.pi / 4 * (5.4e-3**2 - 4.4e-3**2)                   # 0.5 mm wick inside a 6 mm pipe
L_eff, L_total = 0.15, 0.20                                      # effective and total length (m)
p_cap = 2 * sigma / r_eff
print(f"merit number of water at 60 C: {merit:.2e} W/m^2;  capillary pressure {p_cap:.0f} Pa")
print(" tilt (evaporator above condenser)   gravity head (Pa)   capillary limit (W)")
for deg in (0, 15, 30, 60, 90):
    p_grav = rho_l * g * L_total * math.sin(math.radians(deg))
    q_max = (p_cap - p_grav) * rho_l * h_fg * K * A_wick / (mu_l * L_eff)   # Darcy flow in the wick
    print(f"   {deg:3d} degrees                           {p_grav:6.0f}           {max(q_max, 0):6.1f}")

# 2. Critical heat flux in pool boiling (Zuber): q_max = C h_fg rho_v^0.5 [sigma g (rho_l - rho_v)]^0.25
fluids = {  # rho_l, rho_v (kg/m^3), h_fg (J/kg), sigma (N/m), at boiling under 1 atm, approximate
    "water, 100 C": (958.4, 0.598, 2.257e6, 0.0589),
    "fluorinated dielectric, ~56 C": (1600.0, 13.4, 9.48e4, 0.0083),
}
print("\n fluid                            CHF (W/cm^2)")
for name, (rl, rv, hfg, sig) in fluids.items():
    chf = 0.131 * hfg * math.sqrt(rv) * (sig * g * (rl - rv)) ** 0.25
    print(f" {name:32s} {chf / 1e4:8.1f}")
print(f" average flux of 1 kW from 16 cm^2 of silicon: {1000 / 16:.1f} W/cm^2")
```

The 6 mm heat pipe carries about 25 W lying flat, but only 11 W standing upright with its hot end at the top, because the wick must then lift the liquid 20 cm against gravity. That is why a laptop's cooling is designed for a known orientation, and why phones use thin vapour chambers whose capillary paths are short.

The boiling numbers explain a constraint on **two-phase immersion**. Water can boil away about 110 W/cm² from a flat surface, but water conducts electricity, so it cannot touch the chips. The dielectric fluids that can, mostly fluorinated, have small latent heats and surface tensions, and their critical heat flux is only about 15 W/cm², a quarter of the accelerator's *average* flux. Two-phase immersion of high-power chips therefore relies on **boiling enhancement**: porous coatings, sintered or finned "boiler plates" attached to the chip that multiply the boiling area and the nucleation sites.

</Section>

<Section id="liquid" kind="human" title="Liquid cooling for AI data centres">

**Direct-to-chip cooling**, the mainstream for AI racks as of 2026, puts a **cold plate** on each accelerator and CPU. Its fins are fine channels, typically a few tenths of a millimetre wide, machined, skived (sliced up from the copper base) or made by additive manufacturing, and the coolant is usually water with propylene glycol and corrosion inhibitors, carefully filtered so that the channels do not clog. The idea goes back to 1981, when David Tuckerman and Fabian Pease at Stanford etched microchannels into the back of a silicon chip and removed 790 W/cm². In the rack, manifolds and drip-free quick disconnects connect each server to a **coolant distribution unit** (CDU), which pumps the server loop and exchanges its heat with the building's **facility water** through a plate heat exchanger. Fans still cool memory, power supplies and network cards: typically most of a rack's heat, but not all, goes to liquid.

The alternatives, as of 2026:

- **Single-phase immersion**: whole servers submerged in tanks of a dielectric oil (hydrocarbon or synthetic ester) that is pumped through a heat exchanger. Simple and quiet, with no fans, but heavy, messy to service, and the fluids can affect cable and label materials. Used at some sites, notably for cryptocurrency mining and some edge and high-density installations.
- **Two-phase immersion**: servers in a bath of a fluid that boils on the chips and condenses on coils above. Excellent heat transfer, but it has depended on fluorinated fluids. 3M, a major supplier, ended production of its fluorinated fluids at the end of 2025 as part of its exit from PFAS manufacturing, and several proposed replacements also fall within broad regulatory definitions of PFAS, which regulators in Europe and elsewhere are still settling (see [sustainable chemistry](/en/chemistry/sustainable-chemistry/) in Lumenary Chemistry). The approach remains a niche.
- **Two-phase cold plates**: a refrigerant boils inside the cold plate itself, pumped round a closed loop. Boiling absorbs heat at nearly constant temperature, and the flow needed is several times lower than for water, which is attractive for the hottest chips; the challenges are flow distribution between many parallel plates, instabilities, and the refrigerants' own environmental rules. Several start-ups and established cooling firms offer them, and they are a leading candidate for racks of several hundred kilowatts.
- **Cooling inside the chip**: coolant channels etched into the silicon or into the package, as Tuckerman and Pease first did, and as recent research has developed further (van Erp and colleagues at EPFL, 2020). Microsoft reported a prototype in 2025 with channels etched into the back of a chip. It removes most of the chain at once, at the cost of new manufacturing and reliability questions.

Standards are catching up. ASHRAE's Technical Committee 9.9 publishes classes for liquid cooling named by the facility water supply temperature, and the Open Compute Project publishes open specifications for cold plates, CDUs, quick disconnects and immersion tanks, so that equipment from different makers can work together.

<Note>

The facility side of liquid cooling involves pumps, pressurised pipework, heat exchangers and refrigeration plant, as well as the high-voltage electrical systems described in Lumenary EE. It is designed, installed and maintained by qualified engineers and technicians under pressure-equipment, refrigerant-handling and electrical-safety rules. Experiments with cooling at home are best kept to a small fan and heat sink on a low-voltage project board.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="water-energy" kind="computation" title="Water, energy and reusable heat">

The heat ends up outdoors, or in someone's radiator. How much energy and water that costs depends almost entirely on the **temperature** at which it is rejected. A **dry cooler** (a radiator with fans) can cool the facility water to within a few kelvin of the outdoor air, using only fan power. When the air is too warm, sites add **evaporative** cooling, which can cool towards the lower wet-bulb temperature but consumes water, or mechanical **chillers**, which use electricity (the refrigeration cycles of <LessonLink slug="power-and-refrigeration-cycles">power and refrigeration cycles</LessonLink>). The program counts the hours in a year when a dry cooler alone can supply coolant at a given temperature, for two synthetic climates.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A synthetic year of hourly air temperatures: annual mean, a seasonal swing and a daily swing (deg C)
hours = np.arange(8760)
day, hour = hours / 24, hours % 24
def climate(mean, seasonal, daily, hottest_day=200):
    return (mean + seasonal * np.cos(2 * np.pi * (day - hottest_day) / 365)
            + daily * np.cos(2 * np.pi * (hour - 15) / 24))

climates = {"cool temperate": climate(9, 8, 4), "hot": climate(27, 8, 6)}
approach = 6.0          # a dry cooler can bring the coolant to within about 6 K of the air temperature

print(" coolant supply   hours a year a dry cooler alone suffices")
print("   temperature    " + "   ".join(f"{name:>16s}" for name in climates))
for supply in (18, 25, 32, 40, 45):
    shares = [np.mean(T <= supply - approach) for T in climates.values()]
    print(f"     {supply:3d} C       " + "   ".join(f"{s:15.0%} " for s in shares))
for name, T in climates.items():
    print(f"{name}: coldest hour {T.min():.0f} C, hottest {T.max():.0f} C")
```

In the cool climate, coolant at 25 °C can come from dry coolers 94% of the year and at 32 °C all year. In the hot climate, 18 °C coolant is never possible without chillers, while 40 °C coolant is available 81% of the hours and 45 °C 95%. Put that next to the first program: a chip stack that tolerates 45 °C coolant can be cooled almost anywhere without chillers or evaporation, and one that needs 11 °C cannot be cooled anywhere without them. Thermal resistance in the package becomes energy and water at the site.

**Water.** Evaporating water absorbs about 2.4 MJ/kg, so rejecting 1 kWh of heat purely by evaporation uses roughly 1.5 litres; the EE lesson computes what that means for a campus. Closed-loop liquid cooling with dry coolers uses almost no water on site, but more fan and, on hot days, chiller electricity, and the power stations supplying that electricity may themselves use water. The honest comparison is site-specific: the local climate, the stress on the local watershed, and the source of the electricity.

**Heat reuse.** Return water from cold plates at 40–60 °C is warm enough for low-temperature district heating directly, or for a heat pump to lift it to 70–90 °C much more efficiently than from outdoor air. Data centres already feed district heating in Odense (Denmark), Stockholm and the Helsinki region, among other places, and Germany's Energy Efficiency Act of 2023 requires new data centres to reuse a growing share of their heat. The limits are practical: a heat customer close by, heat demand that is seasonal while the data centre's output is constant, and contracts between parties with very different time horizons.

<Narrator who="margaret">

How much does a heat pump need to lift data-centre water from 45 °C to 75 °C? The Carnot limit is $T_\text{hot}/(T_\text{hot} - T_\text{cold}) = 348/30 \approx 11.6$; real machines reach roughly half of Carnot, so a coefficient of performance of about 5 to 6. Each kilowatt-hour of electricity then delivers 5 to 6 kWh of district heat, most of it heat that would otherwise have been thrown away.

</Narrator>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view">

Thermal engineers for electronics work at every scale: package and interface materials with the chip makers; heat sinks, cold plates and vapour chambers with specialist suppliers in Taiwan, China, Japan, Europe and the United States; whole servers and racks with conjugate heat-transfer CFD tools from several vendors; and data-centre halls with airflow and facility-loop models. Design rules come from JEDEC for package metrics, ASHRAE TC 9.9 for environmental classes, and the Open Compute Project for open hardware specifications. Every design ends in testing: thermal test vehicles with programmable heaters, infrared imaging, flow and pressure-drop rigs, and long reliability runs with thermal cycling, because interface materials and pumps age.

The trends to watch, as of late 2026: rack powers climbing from about 120 kW towards several hundred kilowatts, with higher-voltage power delivery (described in Lumenary EE); warmer facility water, to cut chillers and enable reuse; two-phase cold plates for the hottest chips; cooling built into packages and chips; and growing regulatory attention to data centres' energy, water and heat reuse in the EU and elsewhere. Claims of dramatic gains deserve the usual questions: compared with what baseline, at what coolant temperature, and measured how?

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"More thermal paste conducts more heat."** Interface materials conduct far worse than metal; their job is only to fill the microscopic gaps between two surfaces. The thinnest continuous layer is best, which is why bond-line thickness is controlled to tens of micrometres.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Colder coolant is always better."** It lowers the junction temperature, but chilling water costs electricity and often water, rules out free cooling for much of the year and makes heat reuse impossible. The goal is coolant as *warm* as the chip allows, which is why reducing the package's thermal resistance is so valuable.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Liquid-cooled data centres use more water."** Whether a site consumes water depends on how it finally rejects heat (evaporative towers or dry coolers), not on whether the chips are cooled by liquid. Warm liquid cooling often makes dry cooling possible.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A heat pipe conducts heat like a copper rod, only better."** It works only within its limits (capillary, boiling, sonic and others), depends on orientation, and has a startup temperature. Past the capillary limit the evaporator dries out and its temperature climbs sharply.

</Misconception>
