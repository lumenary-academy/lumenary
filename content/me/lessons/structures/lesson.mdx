<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why doesn't it fall down?">

You are probably sitting on a chair, inside a building. The chair holds you up, the floor holds the chair up, the walls or columns hold the floor up, and the foundations hold the walls up. Outside, bridges carry lorries over rivers and towers hold up antennas in the wind. We hardly ever think about them, but each one is working all the time, carrying weight safely down to the ground.

A **structure** is anything built to carry loads: a bridge, a building, a chair, a bicycle frame, a bookshelf, even a bird's nest. Its designers ask: what loads must it carry, which way do the forces travel through it, and is each part strong enough, and stiff enough, for its share? In this lesson you will meet the two kinds of force inside every structure, the shapes engineers use again and again, and the reason triangles are everywhere. Then you will compare beam shapes in Python and build a bridge of your own.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here is the question to carry with you: when you stand on a bridge, where does your weight *go*? It cannot just vanish. It has to travel, through one part after another, all the way into the ground. Engineers call that route the **load path**, and every good structure has a clear one.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="tension-compression" kind="intuition" title="Loads, tension and compression">

### Loads

The forces a structure carries are called **loads**. Engineers sort them into a few kinds:

- **Its own weight** (the *dead load*): a concrete bridge deck is very heavy, and it is there all the time.
- **What it holds up** (the *live load*): people, furniture, cars, trains, books on a shelf. These come and go.
- **Nature's loads**: wind pushing on a tower, snow piling on a roof, the ground shaking in an earthquake, and even the Sun, which warms a long steel bridge so that it grows a few centimetres longer on a summer afternoon.

A good design carries all of these, in their worst combinations, with plenty to spare.

### Pulling and squashing

Inside a structure, every part is being either pulled or squashed (and some are being bent, which, as you will see, is both at once).

<Definition term="tension and compression">

A part is in **tension** when the forces on it pull it apart, trying to stretch it. A rope holding a swing, the cables of a suspension bridge and the spokes of a bicycle wheel are in tension.

A part is in **compression** when the forces on it push it together, trying to squash it. The legs of a chair, the columns of a building and the stones of an arch are in compression.

</Definition>

You can see bending for yourself with a long kitchen sponge. Draw straight lines across one side of it with a felt-tip pen, rest it across two cups, and press down gently in the middle. The lines along the top crowd together (compression) while the lines along the bottom spread apart (tension). Somewhere in the middle there is a layer that is neither squeezed nor stretched.

### Materials have favourites

**Stone, brick and concrete** are very strong in compression but crack easily when pulled. **Rope, wire and cable** are wonderful in tension but useless in compression: try pushing something with a rope! **Steel** and **wood** are good at both. That is why so much modern building uses **reinforced concrete**: steel bars are cast inside the concrete where the tension will be, so the concrete takes the squashing and the steel the pulling. You will meet many more materials in Lumenary Materials' [materials around us](/en/materials/materials-around-us/).

**Beams.** A beam carries loads across a gap by bending: a shelf, a floor joist, a simple concrete bridge. As the sponge showed, its top is squeezed and its bottom stretched. What matters most is how **deep** it is: a plank laid flat sags, but the same plank on its edge hardly bends. That is why steel beams are shaped like a capital I, with material far from the middle, where the squeezing and stretching are greatest.

**Columns.** A column carries a load straight down by being squashed: a chair leg, a pillar, the steel frame of a skyscraper. Short, fat columns are very hard to break. Long, thin ones have a weakness: press the ends of a drinking straw or a long strip of card together and, long before it is crushed, it bows out sideways. That sudden sideways bowing is called **buckling**, and it is the reason tall columns are thick, or braced from the side.

**Arches.** An arch is a curved shape that turns a downward load into compression running along its curve. That makes it perfect for stone and brick, which are strong when squeezed. The price is that an arch pushes *outwards* at its feet, so it needs solid ground or heavy walls, called abutments, to push against. While an arch is being built it cannot stand on its own; its stones rest on a temporary wooden frame until the last, central stone, the keystone, locks them together.

**Cables.** A hanging cable is an upside-down arch: it carries load in pure tension. Suspension bridges hang their roads from cables draped over tall towers. The cables pull, the towers are squashed, and huge anchor blocks hold the cable ends in the ground.

**Trusses.** A truss is a frame of straight bars joined into triangles. Each bar is only pulled or pushed along its length, never bent, so a truss carries big loads with very little material. Look for trusses in railway bridges, roofs, cranes and electricity pylons.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice that the straw failed by bowing *sideways*, not by being crushed. A part in tension only has to be strong enough; a long part in compression must also be stiff enough not to buckle. That one difference shapes a great deal of engineering: designers of steel trusses often arrange them so that the longest bars are the ones being pulled.

</Narrator>

Why? As the square folds, every bar keeps its length; only the **angles** at the corners change. A triangle is different: its three side lengths allow only one shape, so its angles are locked. To change its shape you would have to stretch or squash a bar, and a good bar resists that very strongly.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Mathematicians proved this long before engineers used it: two triangles with the same three side lengths are always identical in shape and size (you can explore it in <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">congruence and similarity</TopicLink>). No such rule holds for four sides, which is why the square is free to fold.

</Narrator>

Add one diagonal to the square and it becomes two triangles, just as rigid. You can spot this trick everywhere: the diagonal brace on a farm gate, the triangles of a bicycle frame, the criss-cross bracing on a pylon or crane, the roof trusses of a house.

People in every part of the world have built structures that still teach engineers today.

| Structure | Where and when | How it carries load | What it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

In 1887, to show how the Forth Bridge would work, its builders staged a photograph of a "human cantilever". Two men sat on chairs with both arms stretched out sideways, each hand gripping a stick braced against the chair. Between their inner hands hung a short plank, and on it sat Kaichi Watanabe, a young Japanese engineer who had studied in Glasgow. Their arms were in tension, the sticks in compression, and piles of bricks at the outer ends stopped the men tipping forwards. The real bridge works the same way, with steel tubes as the sticks and steel ties as the arms.

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Beam shapes in Python">

Why does a plank on its edge bend so much less than the same plank lying flat? Engineers measure a beam's resistance to bending with a number worked out from the shape of its cross-section. For a solid rectangle it is

$$
\text{bending stiffness} = \frac{\text{width} \times \text{depth} \times \text{depth} \times \text{depth}}{12}.
$$

The depth appears **three times**, the width only once. So making a beam twice as deep makes it eight times stiffer, but making it twice as wide only makes it twice as stiff. The program below compares five shapes, each made from about the same amount of material. Press **Run**:

```python run
# How well do different beam shapes resist bending?
# All sizes are in millimetres. Every beam is made from about the same amount of material.

def stiffness(width, depth):
    """The bending stiffness of a solid rectangular beam: width x depth x depth x depth / 12."""
    return width * depth ** 3 / 12

# First, one plank, standing taller and taller (the width stays 60 mm):
print("depth   stiffness compared with the 10 mm plank")
for depth in [10, 20, 30]:
    print(f"{depth:3} mm  {stiffness(60, depth) / stiffness(60, 10):4.0f} times")

# Now five shapes, each made from about 600 square millimetres of material.
shapes = [
    # name,                                   material (mm2),     stiffness
    ("flat plank, 60 wide and 10 deep",       60 * 10,            stiffness(60, 10)),
    ("the same plank on its edge",            10 * 60,            stiffness(10, 60)),
    ("square bar, 24.5 by 24.5",              24.5 * 24.5,        stiffness(24.5, 24.5)),
    # A square tube is a 40 x 40 square with a 32 x 32 hole: subtract the hole.
    ("square tube, 40 by 40, walls 4 thick",  40 * 40 - 32 * 32,  stiffness(40, 40) - stiffness(32, 32)),
    # An I-beam is a 40 x 50 rectangle with two 17.5 x 40 gaps cut out of its sides.
    ("I-beam, 40 wide and 50 deep",           40 * 50 - 2 * 17.5 * 40,
                                                                  stiffness(40, 50) - 2 * stiffness(17.5, 40)),
]

flat = shapes[0][2]
print()
print("shape                                 material   times stiffer than the flat plank")
for name, material, stiff in shapes:
    times = stiff / flat
    print(f"{name:37} {material:5.0f} mm2  {times:5.1f}  " + "#" * round(times))
```

With the same 600 square millimetres of material, the plank on its edge is **36 times** stiffer than the flat plank, and the I-beam is **46 times** stiffer. The I-beam wins because it puts most of its material in two flanges, far from the middle, where the squeezing and stretching are greatest, and keeps only a thin web to join them. The square tube does the same trick in every direction, which is why bicycle frames and scaffolding are made of tubes.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The plank on its edge looks almost as good as the I-beam, so why not use planks? Press on the side of one and you will see: a tall, thin plank topples or twists sideways very easily. The I-beam's wide flanges stop that. Try changing the program: make the I-beam 60 mm deep with the same flanges, and see how much stiffer it gets.

</Narrator>

You can test the same idea with a sheet of paper. Lay it flat across a gap between two books and it sags under a single coin. Now fold it back and forth into a zigzag, like a paper fan, and lay it across the gap again. Exactly the same paper can now hold a whole row of coins, because the folds have given it depth. Corrugated cardboard and the ridged steel sheets on factory roofs use the same trick.

**Materials.** Choose one: sheets of paper and sticky tape; or dry spaghetti and white (PVA) glue or masking tape. Do not use a hot-glue gun unless an adult is doing the gluing, because the glue and the nozzle are hot enough to burn.

**Stay safe.** Dry spaghetti snaps into sharp little splinters that can fly up, so wear safety glasses or goggles while you build and test, and work with an adult. Test on a low table, put a tray or a folded towel under the cup, and keep your hands and feet out from under the bridge while it is loaded.

**Design.** Sketch two or three ideas first. Use triangles to stop the bridge folding, depth to stop it bending, and short pieces for parts that will be squashed. Glue joints well: most model bridges fail at a joint.

**Test.** Weigh your bridge on a kitchen scale. Hang the cup from the middle, then add coins gently, one at a time, and count until the bridge gives way. Watch closely, or film it on a phone: *where* did it fail, and was that part being pulled or squashed?

**Score.** To find the load, weigh ten of your coins to learn the mass of one, then multiply by the number of coins. The fairest score is the **strength-to-weight ratio**: the mass it held divided by the bridge's own mass. A 40 g bridge that held 600 g of coins scores 15: it carried fifteen times its own weight.

**Improve.** Make one change, guided by what broke, and test again. That loop of idea, build, test and improve is the heart of engineering, and the next lesson, <LessonLink slug="how-things-are-made">How things are made</LessonLink>, is all about it.

<Section id="real-world" kind="human" title="Structures in the real world">

Real structural engineers work just as you did, with bigger numbers. They work out every load a structure might meet, including a rare great storm or earthquake, and design with a **margin of safety**, so that it could carry more than it ever should have to. They calculate the force in every part by computer, test materials and models in laboratories, and inspect bridges and buildings for decades. Building codes in every country set the rules, because people crossing a bridge trust that someone has checked it.

Later in Lumenary ME you will learn to calculate those forces yourself: balancing forces and turning effects in <LessonLink slug="statics-and-equilibrium">Statics and equilibrium</LessonLink>, the force in every bar of a truss in <LessonLink slug="trusses-and-frames">Trusses and frames</LessonLink>, and how beams bend and columns buckle in <LessonLink slug="axial-torsion-and-bending">Axial load, torsion and bending</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="deflection-and-buckling">Deflection and buckling</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A strong structure has no forces in it."** A bridge standing still carries enormous forces all the time. It stays still because those forces are *balanced*, and because each part is strong enough for its share.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"More material always makes a stronger structure."** Shape matters as much as the amount of material. The same paper folded into a zigzag holds many more coins, and the same plank is 36 times stiffer standing on its edge. Extra material also adds weight, which the structure must then carry too.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Concrete is strong, so it can take any load."** Concrete is very strong when squeezed but cracks when pulled. Concrete beams and floors have steel bars cast inside them, placed where the tension will be.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Buildings and bridges never move."** They move a little all the time. Tall towers sway gently in the wind, and long bridges grow longer on hot days, which is why bridges have expansion joints: the toothed gaps you may have noticed in the road at each end. Structures are designed to move a little, safely, rather than to crack.

</Misconception>
