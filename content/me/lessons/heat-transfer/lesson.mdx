<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="How fast does heat move?">

<LessonLink slug="first-law-of-thermodynamics">The first law</LessonLink> tells you *how much* heat it takes to warm a tank of water; <LessonLink slug="second-law-and-entropy">the second law</LessonLink> tells you which way heat flows. Neither tells you *how fast*. Yet nearly every thermal design question is about rates and temperatures. How thick must the insulation on a house in Ulaanbaatar be, where winter nights reach −30 °C? How large a heat sink keeps a 300 W processor below 90 °C? How long does a casting take to cool? How many square metres of tubes does a power-station condenser need? How deep does winter frost reach into the ground, and so how deep must a water main be buried?

**Heat transfer** answers these. Heat moves in three ways, by conduction, convection and radiation, each with its own rate law. This lesson builds the three laws, combines them into networks of thermal resistances, solves conduction problems that formulas cannot by finite differences, and finishes with the two most common pieces of heat-transfer hardware: fins and heat exchangers.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Three ways heat travels">

Stand by a wood stove. The iron handle of its door gets hot because heat **conducts** through solid metal, passed from atom to atom by vibrations and, in metals, by free electrons. The warm air rising off the stove carries heat across the room by **convection**: a moving fluid picks up energy at a hot surface and takes it elsewhere. And your face feels warm even through still air, because the stove glows in the infrared, sending energy by **radiation**, which needs no material at all and is how the Sun's energy crosses 150 million kilometres of vacuum.

Most real problems mix all three. Heat leaving a heated room passes by convection (and radiation) to the inner face of the wall, by conduction through the layers of the wall, and by convection and radiation from the outer face to the cold air and sky.

<Analogy>

Heat flow through a chain of materials behaves like electric current through a chain of resistors, the analogy behind [Ohm's law](/en/ee/circuits-and-ohms-law/): temperature difference plays the part of voltage, heat flow the part of current, and each layer or surface has a **thermal resistance**. Resistances in series add; paths in parallel share the flow. The analogy is exact for steady conduction, and it is how engineers reason about every thermal design before they compute anything.

</Analogy>

<Narrator who="chloe">

The network tells you where to spend effort: the largest resistance controls the heat flow. To keep heat in, make the biggest resistance bigger, which is why insulation works and thicker brick barely helps. To get heat out, attack the biggest resistance too, and in a liquid-to-air device it is almost always the air-side convection, which is why a car radiator has fins on the air side and smooth tubes on the coolant side.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The three rate laws">

<Definition term="Fourier's law of conduction">

The **heat flux** $q''$ (heat flow per unit area, W/m²) conducted through a material is proportional to the temperature gradient and flows down it:

$$q'' = -k\,\frac{\mathrm dT}{\mathrm dx} \qquad\text{(in 3-D, } \mathbf q'' = -k\,\nabla T\text{)},$$

where $k$ is the **thermal conductivity**, in W/(m·K).

</Definition>

Typical values near room temperature, rounded: copper about 400 W/(m·K), aluminium alloys 120–240, carbon steel about 50, stainless steel about 15, concrete about 1.4, glass about 1, brick 0.6–1, wood 0.1–0.2, water 0.6, mineral wool and polymer foams 0.03–0.04, still air 0.026. Good insulators are mostly trapped air; the solid is there to stop the air moving.

<Definition term="Newton's law of cooling">

The heat flux from a surface at $T_s$ to a fluid at $T_\infty$ is

$$q'' = h\,(T_s - T_\infty),$$

where $h$ is the **convection heat-transfer coefficient**, in W/(m²·K).

</Definition>

Newton's law is really a definition of $h$, which is not a property of the fluid but of the whole flow: speed, geometry, fluid properties and whether the fluid boils or condenses. Orders of magnitude are worth memorising: a few to about 25 W/(m²·K) for air moving by itself (natural convection); tens to a few hundred for air blown by a fan; hundreds to tens of thousands for pumped water; and up to around $10^5$ for boiling and condensation.

<Definition term="Stefan–Boltzmann law">

A surface at absolute temperature $T_s$ with **emissivity** $\varepsilon$ ($0 \le \varepsilon \le 1$) emits a heat flux $\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4$, where $\sigma = 5.670 \times 10^{-8}$ W/(m²·K⁴). A small surface surrounded by large surroundings at $T_\text{sur}$ exchanges net

$$q'' = \varepsilon\,\sigma\left(T_s^4 - T_\text{sur}^4\right).$$

</Definition>

Combining Fourier's law with an energy balance on a small volume gives the **heat equation**, the partial differential equation every conduction solver solves:

$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha\,\nabla^2 T + \frac{\dot q}{\rho c_p}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c_p},$$

where $\dot q$ is any heat generated per unit volume (by an electric current, say) and $\alpha$ is the **thermal diffusivity**, in m²/s. Conductivity says how much heat flows for a given gradient; diffusivity says how fast a temperature change spreads. Its mathematics is in <TopicLink id="math.analysis.pde-intro">partial differential equations and Fourier series</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="resistances" kind="examples" title="Thermal resistance networks">

For steady one-dimensional heat flow, each rate law becomes "heat flow = temperature difference / resistance":

| Process | Thermal resistance (K/W) |
| --- | --- |
| Conduction through a slab of thickness $L$, area $A$ | $R = L/(kA)$ |
| Conduction through a cylindrical shell, radii $r_1$ to $r_2$, length $\ell$ | $R = \ln(r_2/r_1)/(2\pi k \ell)$ |
| Convection from area $A$ | $R = 1/(hA)$ |
| Radiation from area $A$ (linearised) | $R = 1/(h_r A)$, $h_r = \varepsilon\sigma(T_s + T_\text{sur})(T_s^2 + T_\text{sur}^2)$ |

Building engineers work per square metre and call the reciprocal of the total resistance the **U-value**, in W/(m²·K).

<Example title="An insulated cavity wall">

A wall is built (inside to outside) of 12.5 mm plasterboard ($k = 0.25$), 100 mm mineral wool ($k = 0.035$) and 102.5 mm brick ($k = 0.77$). Take $h = 8$ W/(m²·K) inside (convection and radiation together) and 25 outside. Per square metre the resistances are, in m²·K/W:

$$\tfrac18 + \tfrac{0.0125}{0.25} + \tfrac{0.100}{0.035} + \tfrac{0.1025}{0.77} + \tfrac{1}{25} = 0.125 + 0.050 + 2.857 + 0.133 + 0.040 = 3.205,$$

so $U = 0.31$ W/(m²·K). With 20 °C inside and 0 °C outside the flux is 6.2 W/m², and 40 m² of wall loses about 250 W. Without the mineral wool, $U = 2.87$ and the same wall loses 2.3 kW. The temperatures at each interface follow by subtracting $q''R$ layer by layer: 19.2 °C on the inner face of the plasterboard, 18.9 °C behind it, then a plunge to 1.1 °C across the insulation, and 0.25 °C on the outer face of the brick. Nearly all the temperature drop sits where nearly all the resistance is.

</Example>

The cylinder resistance hides a surprise. Insulating a pipe or wire adds conduction resistance but also enlarges the outer surface, lowering the convection resistance. For an insulation of conductivity $k$ and an outer coefficient $h$, the total is smallest at the **critical radius** $r_\text{cr} = k/h$. For mineral wool in still air that is about 4 mm, smaller than almost any pipe, so insulation always helps. For PVC on a thin electrical wire it is around 16 mm, so the insulation on a cable actually *increases* the heat it sheds, a happy accident for cable ratings.

</Section>

<Section id="finite-differences" kind="computation" title="Conduction by finite differences">

Formulas cover slabs, cylinders and a few idealised shapes. Real parts have corners, varying properties and changing boundary temperatures, so engineers solve the heat equation numerically. The simplest method replaces derivatives by differences on a grid of spacing $\Delta x$ and time step $\Delta t$. Write $T_i^n$ for the temperature at node $i$ and time level $n$. In 1-D, the second derivative becomes $(T_{i+1} - 2T_i + T_{i-1})/\Delta x^2$, and stepping forward in time gives the **explicit** (forward-time, centred-space) scheme

$$T_i^{n+1} = T_i^n + \mathrm{Fo}\,\left(T_{i+1}^n - 2T_i^n + T_{i-1}^n\right), \qquad \mathrm{Fo} = \frac{\alpha\,\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}.$$

The grid **Fourier number** $\mathrm{Fo}$ must not exceed $\tfrac12$. Rewrite the update as $T_i^{n+1} = \mathrm{Fo}\,T_{i+1}^n + (1 - 2\,\mathrm{Fo})\,T_i^n + \mathrm{Fo}\,T_{i-1}^n$: when $\mathrm{Fo} \le \tfrac12$ all three weights are non-negative and add to 1, so the new temperature is an average of old ones and can never overshoot. Above $\tfrac12$ the middle weight goes negative, a node that is hotter than its neighbours becomes colder than them at the next step, and the oscillation grows without limit.

### A cold snap in the ground

The ground surface, initially at 10 °C throughout, drops suddenly to −10 °C and stays there for 30 days. How deep does the 0 °C line reach? Treat the ground as a deep slab with a moist soil's diffusivity, $\alpha \approx 0.5 \times 10^{-6}$ m²/s. This problem also has an exact solution, $T = T_s + (T_i - T_s)\,\mathrm{erf}\!\big(x/(2\sqrt{\alpha t})\big)$, which lets us check the numbers.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

# A cold snap: the ground surface drops from +10 C to -10 C and stays there for 30 days.
alpha = 0.5e-6                    # thermal diffusivity of a moist soil, m^2/s (roughly)
T_i, T_s = 10.0, -10.0
depth, dx = 6.0, 0.05             # model 6 m of ground in 5 cm slices
x = np.arange(0.0, depth + dx / 2, dx)

def simulate(Fo, days):
    dt = Fo * dx**2 / alpha
    steps = int(round(days * 86400 / dt))
    T = np.full(x.size, T_i)
    T[0] = T_s                    # surface held at -10 C
    for _ in range(steps):        # explicit (FTCS) update of every interior node
        T[1:-1] = T[1:-1] + Fo * (T[2:] - 2 * T[1:-1] + T[:-2])
        # the deepest node stays at T_i: far enough down to be undisturbed
    return T, dt, steps

T, dt, steps = simulate(Fo=0.4, days=30)
print(f"time step {dt:.0f} s, {steps} steps")
print(" depth (m)   finite difference   exact (erf)")
for d in [0.0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0]:
    i = int(round(d / dx))
    exact = T_s + (T_i - T_s) * math.erf(d / (2 * math.sqrt(alpha * 30 * 86400)))
    print(f"   {d:5.2f}      {T[i]:8.3f}          {exact:8.3f}")

# where does the 0 C isotherm reach? interpolate between nodes
k = np.argmax(T > 0.0)
frost = x[k - 1] + (0.0 - T[k - 1]) / (T[k] - T[k - 1]) * dx
eta = 0.476936                    # erf(eta) = 0.5
print(f"\n0 C reaches {frost:.3f} m (finite difference); "
      f"exact {2 * eta * math.sqrt(alpha * 30 * 86400):.3f} m")

# The same scheme with too large a time step
for Fo in [0.5, 0.6]:
    T, dt, steps = simulate(Fo, days=3)
    print(f"Fo = {Fo}: after {steps} steps the largest |T| is {np.abs(T).max():.3g} C")
```

With $\mathrm{Fo} = 0.4$ (time steps of 2000 s, 1296 of them) the finite-difference temperatures agree with the exact solution to within 0.002 K, and both put the 0 °C line at 1.086 m after a month. At $\mathrm{Fo} = 0.5$ the scheme is on the edge but still bounded; at 0.6 the largest "temperature" is $10^{11}$ °C after 86 steps, a numerical explosion, not physics. Implicit schemes, which solve a linear system at every step, remove the limit, and the same idea, called the CFL condition, governs <LessonLink slug="computational-fluid-dynamics">computational fluid dynamics</LessonLink>.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Before using 1.09 m to bury a water main, check what the model left out. Freezing the water in soil releases latent heat, which slows the frost front a great deal; snow on the ground is an excellent insulator; and real surface temperatures swing daily and seasonally. So the pure-conduction answer is an overestimate for this cold snap, while a long, snowless winter could go deeper. Design frost depths come from local codes based on measured records, with margin added on top.

</Narrator>

### Two dimensions

In 2-D steady conduction without heat generation the heat equation becomes Laplace's equation, $\partial^2 T/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 T/\partial y^2 = 0$, and on a square grid with $\Delta x = \Delta y$ its finite-difference form says that **every interior temperature is the average of its four neighbours**. Writing that for every node gives one linear equation per node, solved here directly with `numpy.linalg.solve`. The problem: a long square bar with one face held at 100 °C and the other three at 0 °C, which has a Fourier-series solution to compare with, and a neat check: by symmetry, the four problems with one hot face each add up to a bar entirely at 100 °C, so the centre must be at exactly 25 °C.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Steady 2-D conduction in a long square bar: one face at 100 C, the other three at 0 C.
# Unknowns: the temperatures at the n x n interior nodes of a grid with spacing h = 1/(n+1).
n = 31
N = n * n
idx = lambda i, j: i * n + j                  # i: row (y), j: column (x)
A = np.zeros((N, N))
b = np.zeros(N)
T_top = 100.0
for i in range(n):
    for j in range(n):
        k = idx(i, j)
        A[k, k] = -4.0                        # five-point Laplacian: neighbours - 4 * centre = 0
        for di, dj in [(1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)]:
            ii, jj = i + di, j + dj
            if 0 <= ii < n and 0 <= jj < n:
                A[k, idx(ii, jj)] = 1.0
            elif ii == n:                     # the neighbour is on the hot top face
                b[k] -= T_top
            # neighbours on the other three faces are at 0 C and add nothing
T = np.linalg.solve(A, b).reshape(n, n)

def exact(x, y, terms=199):                   # Fourier-series solution of the same problem
    s = 0.0
    for m in range(1, terms + 1, 2):
        s += 4 * T_top / (m * np.pi) * np.sin(m * np.pi * x) * np.sinh(m * np.pi * y) / np.sinh(m * np.pi)
    return s

h = 1.0 / (n + 1)
print(f"{N} unknowns, grid spacing h = {h:.4f}")
print("   x      y     finite difference   Fourier series")
for x, y in [(0.5, 0.5), (0.5, 0.25), (0.5, 0.75), (0.25, 0.75), (0.25, 0.5), (0.125, 0.875)]:
    i, j = int(round(y / h)) - 1, int(round(x / h)) - 1
    print(f" {x:5.3f}  {y:5.3f}     {T[i, j]:8.3f}           {exact(x, y):8.3f}")

print("\nisotherms across the bar (top row is next to the 100 C face):")
for i in range(n - 2, -1, -4):
    print("  " + " ".join(f"{T[i, j]:3.0f}" for j in range(1, n, 4)))
```

The centre comes out at 25.000 °C, as symmetry demands, and elsewhere the 961-unknown solution agrees with the Fourier series to within about 0.03 K. The printed grid shows the heat spreading down from the hot face, with the steepest temperature gradients at the two top corners, where the hot face meets the cold ones. Finite element programs do the same thing on unstructured meshes of real parts (see <LessonLink slug="finite-element-method">the finite element method</LessonLink>), with sparse solvers that handle millions of unknowns.

</Section>

<Section id="convection" kind="definitions" title="Convection: coefficients and dimensionless numbers">

Convection is conduction into a moving fluid. Right at the wall the fluid is at rest (no slip, from <LessonLink slug="viscous-flow-and-pipes">viscous flow</LessonLink>), so the heat crosses a thin **thermal boundary layer** by conduction, and the faster the flow, the thinner the layer and the larger $h$. Because $h$ depends on so much, it is correlated through dimensionless groups.

<Definition term="Nusselt, Prandtl and Biot numbers">

- The **Nusselt number** $\mathrm{Nu} = hL/k_\text{fluid}$ is the convection coefficient made dimensionless with a length $L$ and the *fluid's* conductivity: how much better the flow transfers heat than pure conduction through a fluid layer of thickness $L$ would.
- The **Prandtl number** $\mathrm{Pr} = \nu/\alpha$ compares how fast momentum and heat diffuse in the fluid: about 0.7 for air, 7 for water at 20 °C, hundreds for oils.
- The **Biot number** $\mathrm{Bi} = hL_c/k_\text{solid}$, with $L_c$ the volume divided by the surface area, compares the resistance inside a *solid* with the convection resistance outside it.

</Definition>

A **correlation** gives Nu as a function of Re and Pr (for forced convection) or of the Rayleigh number (for natural convection, driven by buoyancy). Two classic ones for flow inside tubes: fully developed laminar flow has $\mathrm{Nu} = 3.66$ for a constant wall temperature and 4.36 for a constant heat flux; turbulent flow ($\mathrm{Re} \gt 10^4$, $0.7 \lt \mathrm{Pr} \lt 160$) is often estimated with the Dittus–Boelter correlation

$$\mathrm{Nu} = 0.023\,\mathrm{Re}^{0.8}\,\mathrm{Pr}^{n}, \qquad n = 0.4 \text{ when the fluid is heated, } 0.3 \text{ when cooled}.$$

For example, 0.2 kg/s of water at about 40 °C ($\mu = 0.653 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa·s, $k = 0.631$ W/(m·K), $\mathrm{Pr} = 4.32$) heated in a 20 mm tube has $\mathrm{Re} = 4\dot m/(\pi D\mu) \approx 19\,500$, $\mathrm{Nu} \approx 112$ and $h = \mathrm{Nu}\,k/D \approx 3500$ W/(m²·K).

<Narrator who="gideon">

Treat a correlation's answer as having error bars. Dittus–Boelter scatters by up to about ±25% against measurements, and newer fits such as Gnielinski's do better, to around ±10%, over a wider range. Thermal designs therefore carry margin: extra exchanger area, an allowance for fouling, a heat sink sized for the hottest day.

</Narrator>

### Lumped bodies

When $\mathrm{Bi} \lt 0.1$, conduction inside a body is so easy compared with convection outside that the body stays nearly uniform in temperature as it heats or cools. An energy balance, $\rho c V\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm dt = -hA(T - T_\infty)$, then gives an exponential approach to the fluid temperature with **time constant** $\tau = \rho c V/(hA)$. Small, conductive objects in gases qualify; a thick concrete block in water does not, and needs the conduction solution above.

<Narrator who="margaret">

This is why a temperature sensor lags. A thermocouple bead 0.8 mm across ($\rho = 8500$ kg/m³, $c = 400$ J/(kg·K), $k \approx 20$ W/(m·K)) in an air stream with $h = 200$ W/(m²·K) has $\mathrm{Bi} \approx 0.0013$, so it is lumped, and $\tau = \rho c D/(6h) \approx 2.3$ s. After a step change it reads 63% of the change in one time constant and 99% only after about five, some 11 s, so a test that changes faster than that needs a finer bead.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="radiation" kind="human" title="Radiation">

Every surface above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation, and at the temperatures of everyday engineering almost all of it is in the infrared. A perfect emitter, a **black body**, emits $\sigma T^4$; real surfaces emit a fraction $\varepsilon$ of that, and absorb the same fraction of the radiation falling on them (at the same wavelengths). Polished aluminium has $\varepsilon \approx 0.05$; most paints, whatever their visible colour, human skin, water and oxidised metals are around 0.9 or more in the infrared.

Radiation matters even at room temperature. A clothed person with 1.5 m² of surface at 30 °C and $\varepsilon = 0.95$, in a room whose walls are at 20 °C, radiates a net 86 W, comparable with the heat lost by convection. The linearised radiation coefficient is $h_r \approx 5.7$ W/(m²·K), about the same as natural convection in still air, which is why cold walls and windows make a room feel chilly even when the air is warm.

Between surfaces that see each other, how much radiation leaving surface 1 lands on surface 2 is a purely geometric quantity, the **view factor** $F_{12}$. Two rules make view factors tractable: *reciprocity*, $A_1F_{12} = A_2F_{21}$, and *summation*, $\sum_j F_{1j} = 1$ for an enclosure. For two large parallel plates the net flux is $\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)/(1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2 - 1)$, so two shiny surfaces facing each other barely exchange heat. A thin shiny sheet placed between them, a **radiation shield**, cuts the exchange further; dozens of aluminised plastic layers make the multilayer insulation that wraps spacecraft. Complex geometries are handled by view-factor tables in handbooks or by Monte Carlo ray tracing in software.

</Section>

<Section id="fins" kind="computation" title="Fins">

When the surface's convection resistance is the bottleneck, add surface: **fins**, the ribs on a motorcycle cylinder, the plates of a computer heat sink, the spines on a heat-pump coil. A fin is not at the base temperature everywhere: heat must conduct along it while convection drains heat from its sides, so its temperature falls towards the tip. An energy balance on a thin slice of a fin with cross-section $A_c$ and perimeter $P$ gives, for the excess temperature $\theta = T - T_\infty$,

$$\frac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dx^2} = m^2\theta, \qquad m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}}.$$

With an insulated tip, the solution is $\theta = \theta_b \cosh m(L - x)/\cosh mL$, the heat the fin sheds is $q = \sqrt{hPkA_c}\,\theta_b\tanh mL$, and its **fin efficiency** (actual heat over the heat it would shed if it were all at the base temperature) is $\eta_f = \tanh(mL)/(mL)$. The length $1/m$ is the natural scale: a fin much longer than about $2/m$ is mostly at air temperature and wasted.

The program solves the fin equation by finite differences (a tridiagonal linear system, with a mirror node for the insulated tip), checks it against the formula, and compares a 5 mm pin fin in aluminium and in stainless steel, with its base at 80 °C in air at 20 °C and $h = 40$ W/(m²·K).

```python run
import numpy as np

# A pin fin: diameter 5 mm, base at 80 C, air at 20 C, h = 40 W/(m^2 K), tip insulated.
D, h, T_b, T_inf = 0.005, 40.0, 80.0, 20.0
P, Ac = np.pi * D, np.pi * D**2 / 4           # perimeter and cross-section

def fin_fd(k, L, n=200):
    """Finite differences for theta'' = m^2 theta, theta(0) = theta_b, theta'(L) = 0."""
    m2 = h * P / (k * Ac)
    dx = L / n
    A = np.zeros((n + 1, n + 1))
    rhs = np.zeros(n + 1)
    A[0, 0], rhs[0] = 1.0, T_b - T_inf
    for i in range(1, n):
        A[i, i - 1], A[i, i], A[i, i + 1] = 1.0, -2.0 - m2 * dx**2, 1.0
    A[n, n - 1], A[n, n] = 2.0, -2.0 - m2 * dx**2   # mirror node: zero gradient at the tip
    theta = np.linalg.solve(A, rhs)
    # heat leaving the fin = convection from its whole surface (trapezoidal rule)
    q = h * P * dx * (theta.sum() - 0.5 * theta[0] - 0.5 * theta[-1])
    return theta, q

def fin_exact(k, L):
    m = np.sqrt(h * P / (k * Ac))
    q = np.sqrt(h * P * k * Ac) * (T_b - T_inf) * np.tanh(m * L)
    return q, np.tanh(m * L) / (m * L)

for name, k in [("aluminium", 200.0), ("stainless steel", 15.0)]:
    m = np.sqrt(h * P / (k * Ac))
    print(f"{name}: k = {k:.0f} W/(m K), m = {m:.1f} 1/m, 1/m = {1000 / m:.0f} mm")
    print("   L (mm)   q FD (W)  q exact (W)  tip T (C)  efficiency")
    for L in [0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20]:
        theta, q = fin_fd(k, L)
        qe, eff = fin_exact(k, L)
        print(f"   {L * 1000:5.0f}    {q:7.3f}    {qe:7.3f}     {theta[-1] + T_inf:6.1f}      {eff:5.2f}")
    print()
q_bare = h * Ac * (T_b - T_inf)
print(f"the 5 mm spot of bare base the fin replaces would lose only {q_bare:.3f} W")
```

The finite differences match the formula to the third decimal. An aluminium pin ($1/m = 79$ mm) keeps 89% efficiency at 50 mm and sheds 1.67 W, against 0.047 W from the bare spot it stands on: an effectiveness of about 35. Doubling it to 100 mm adds only half as much again, and at 200 mm the tip is within 10 K of the air. The stainless-steel pin ($1/m = 22$ mm) is finished by about 50 mm: doubling it to 100 mm adds 2%. Fin design is a trade between conductivity, length, spacing (fins too close choke the airflow) and mass, which is why heat sinks are aluminium or copper and why their fins are short and many.

</Section>

<Section id="exchangers" kind="examples" title="Heat exchangers and the log-mean temperature difference">

A **heat exchanger** moves heat from one fluid to another through a wall: the radiator of a car, the condenser of a power station, the evaporator of a heat pump, the plate exchanger that pasteurises milk. The main types are double-pipe (one tube inside another), **shell-and-tube** (a bundle of tubes in a cylindrical shell, with baffles to steer the shell-side flow), **plate** (corrugated plates clamped together, compact and easy to clean) and **cross-flow** finned coils for air. The fluids may run in the same direction (parallel flow), opposite directions (**counterflow**) or across each other.

The wall's local heat flux is $U(T_h - T_c)$, where the **overall coefficient** $U$ combines the two convection resistances, the wall's conduction and **fouling** resistances for the scale and deposits that build up in service. Because the temperature difference changes along the exchanger, the total duty needs the right average.

<Theorem name="Log-mean temperature difference">

For a parallel-flow or counterflow exchanger with constant $U$ and constant specific heats, the duty is

$$Q = U A\,\Delta T_\text{lm}, \qquad \Delta T_\text{lm} = \frac{\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2}{\ln(\Delta T_1/\Delta T_2)},$$

where $\Delta T_1$ and $\Delta T_2$ are the temperature differences between the two fluids at the two ends.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $C_h = \dot m_h c_{p,h}$ and $C_c = \dot m_c c_{p,c}$ for the heat-capacity rates and $\Delta T = T_h - T_c$. Across a strip of wall of area $\mathrm dA$, heat $\mathrm dQ = U\,\Delta T\,\mathrm dA$ leaves the hot fluid and enters the cold. Marching from end 1 to end 2, the hot fluid cools by $\mathrm dQ/C_h$; in parallel flow the cold fluid warms by $\mathrm dQ/C_c$, and in counterflow (cold fluid travelling the other way) it cools by $\mathrm dQ/C_c$ in the marching direction. Either way $\mathrm d(\Delta T) = -\mathrm dQ\,(1/C_h \pm 1/C_c) = -U\,\Delta T\,\mathrm dA\,(1/C_h \pm 1/C_c)$. Divide by $\Delta T$ and integrate over the whole area: $\ln(\Delta T_2/\Delta T_1) = -UA(1/C_h \pm 1/C_c)$. Integrating $\mathrm d(\Delta T)$ itself gives $\Delta T_2 - \Delta T_1 = -Q(1/C_h \pm 1/C_c)$. Dividing the second equation by the first eliminates the bracket: $Q = UA\,(\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2)/\ln(\Delta T_1/\Delta T_2)$.

</Proof>

<Example title="Sizing a water-to-water exchanger">

Cool 1.0 kg/s of hot water from 90 °C to 50 °C with 1.5 kg/s of water entering at 20 °C, with $U = 1500$ W/(m²·K) and $c_p = 4180$ J/(kg·K). The energy balance fixes the duty and the cold outlet; the LMTD then fixes the area for each arrangement. The program also checks the counterflow answer the way the proof works, by marching along the wall in small steps until the hot water has reached 50 °C.

</Example>

```python run
import numpy as np

# Water-to-water exchanger: cool 1.0 kg/s of hot water from 90 C to 50 C with 1.5 kg/s of water at 20 C.
cp = 4180.0                                   # J/(kg K)
mh, mc = 1.0, 1.5
Th_in, Th_out, Tc_in = 90.0, 50.0, 20.0
U = 1500.0                                    # overall coefficient, W/(m^2 K)
Ch, Cc = mh * cp, mc * cp                     # heat-capacity rates, W/K

Q = Ch * (Th_in - Th_out)                     # duty from the hot side
Tc_out = Tc_in + Q / Cc                       # energy balance gives the cold outlet
print(f"duty Q = {Q / 1e3:.1f} kW, cold water leaves at {Tc_out:.2f} C")

def lmtd(dT1, dT2):
    return (dT1 - dT2) / np.log(dT1 / dT2)

for name, dT1, dT2 in [("counterflow ", Th_in - Tc_out, Th_out - Tc_in),
                       ("parallel flow", Th_in - Tc_in, Th_out - Tc_out)]:
    dTlm = lmtd(dT1, dT2)
    print(f"{name}: end differences {dT1:5.2f} and {dT2:5.2f} K, LMTD {dTlm:5.2f} K,"
          f" area {Q / (U * dTlm):.2f} m^2")

# Check the counterflow answer by marching along the area, from the hot inlet end.
# Both streams cool in the direction of marching (the cold one flows the other way).
dA = 1e-4
A, Th, Tc = 0.0, Th_in, Tc_out
while Th > Th_out:
    dq = U * (Th - Tc) * dA                   # heat crossing this strip of wall
    Th -= dq / Ch
    Tc -= dq / Cc
    A += dA
print(f"marching: hot side reaches {Th_out:.0f} C after {A:.3f} m^2; cold side there is {Tc:.3f} C")
```

The duty is 167.2 kW and the cold water leaves at 46.67 °C. Counterflow keeps the two streams 30–43 K apart all the way (LMTD 36.3 K) and needs 3.07 m²; parallel flow starts with a 70 K difference but squeezes it to 3.3 K at the outlet (LMTD 21.9 K) and needs 5.09 m², two-thirds more. The marching check lands on 3.074 m² with the cold side at exactly 20 °C, confirming the formula. In counterflow the cold outlet could even be hotter than the hot outlet; in parallel flow it never can.

When the outlet temperatures are unknown (rating an existing exchanger rather than sizing a new one) the LMTD method needs iteration, and engineers use the equivalent **effectiveness–NTU** method instead: the effectiveness $\varepsilon = Q/Q_\text{max}$ compares the duty with the most that could ever be transferred, $C_\text{min}(T_{h,\text{in}} - T_{c,\text{in}})$ (here $\varepsilon = 0.57$), and is a known function of the number of transfer units $\mathrm{NTU} = UA/C_\text{min}$ for each flow arrangement. Multi-pass shell-and-tube and cross-flow exchangers use the LMTD with a correction factor, from charts or formulas in the handbooks.

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Heat transfer in professional practice">

- **Buildings.** Building codes in many countries set maximum U-values for walls, roofs and windows, calculated by standard methods (ISO 6946 for building components, via [ISO](https://www.iso.org/)), and the [ASHRAE](https://www.ashrae.org/) Handbook is a standard reference for the heat gains and losses of buildings. Thermal bridges, where a steel beam or a concrete slab edge short-circuits the insulation, are found with infrared cameras and 2-D or 3-D conduction models like the one above; <LessonLink slug="heating-cooling-and-buildings">heating, cooling and buildings</LessonLink> takes this further.
- **Electronics.** Every chip is a heat source in a network of thermal resistances: junction to case, case to heat sink, heat sink to air or liquid. Data centres in 2026 increasingly use liquid cooling for AI accelerators; see <LessonLink slug="thermal-management-for-computing">thermal management for computing</LessonLink> and [powering data centres](/en/ee/powering-data-centres/) in Lumenary EE.
- **Process plant and power.** Shell-and-tube exchangers are designed mechanically to the standards of [TEMA](https://tema.org/) and the pressure-vessel codes, and thermally with specialist rating software from several vendors, built on decades of research measurements. Fouling allowances, cleaning schedules and vibration of tubes in the shell-side flow are as important as the LMTD.
- **Simulation.** Conduction in solids is solved by finite elements in open-source codes such as [CalculiX](https://www.calculix.de/) and [Elmer](https://www.elmerfem.org/) and in commercial packages from Ansys, Dassault Systèmes (Abaqus), Siemens and COMSOL; conjugate heat transfer (solid and fluid together) uses <LessonLink slug="computational-fluid-dynamics">CFD</LessonLink>. Hot-section parts of gas turbines, cooled by internal passages and protected by ceramic thermal-barrier coatings (see [ceramics and glasses](/en/materials/ceramics-and-glasses/) in Lumenary Materials), are among the hardest heat-transfer designs in engineering.

Heat-transfer equipment can be dangerous: surfaces hot enough to burn, steam and pressurised hot water, and cryogenic liquids cold enough to injure. Industrial plant is guarded, insulated and lagged to keep touchable surfaces safe, and pressure parts are certified and inspected. Safe experiments at home are simple ones: timing two ice cubes as they melt, one on a metal baking tray and one on a wooden chopping board (the metal one wins, for the reason in the first misconception below), or logging a mug of warm water cooling with a kitchen thermometer.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

Isaac Newton published his law of cooling anonymously in 1701, from experiments on hot iron cooling in a draught. Joseph Fourier, prefect of the Isère department under Napoleon, wrote down the heat equation and invented Fourier series to solve it, in work submitted in 1807 and published in 1822. Josef Stefan found the fourth-power law from experiments in 1879, and his former student Ludwig Boltzmann derived it from thermodynamics in 1884. Wilhelm Nusselt, working in Germany around 1915, showed how dimensional analysis organises convection measurements; Ludwig Prandtl's boundary-layer theory explained them. The log-mean temperature difference was already in use in the nineteenth century for designing condensers, and the effectiveness–NTU method was developed in the 1950s for compact heat exchangers. Relaxation methods for grids like the ones above were worked by hand in the 1940s, and grid solutions of partial differential equations were among the first problems given to electronic computers.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Metal feels colder than wood because it is colder."** In a room both are at the same temperature. Metal conducts heat away from your hand much faster, so your skin cools more quickly where it touches it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"White surfaces radiate less heat."** Colour describes visible light. In the infrared, where room-temperature and warm objects radiate, white paint has an emissivity near 0.9, as high as black paint. To radiate less you need a shiny metal surface, not a light colour. (White does reflect more *sunlight*, which is a different part of the spectrum.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The convection coefficient is a property of the fluid."** It depends on the flow speed, the geometry, the fluid and whether it boils or condenses. The same water gives $h$ of a few hundred in a slow, wide channel and many thousands in a fast, narrow one.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A smaller time step always makes a simulation more accurate, so stability is only about accuracy."** For the explicit scheme, a time step above the limit $\mathrm{Fo} = \tfrac12$ is not just inaccurate: the solution explodes. Below it, accuracy also depends on the grid spacing, so shrinking $\Delta t$ alone cannot remove the spatial error.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"More fins, or longer fins, always mean more cooling."** Beyond a length of about $2/m$ a fin's tip is at air temperature and adds nothing but weight, and fins packed too closely choke the airflow between them, lowering $h$. Fins also help only on the side whose convection resistance controls the heat flow.

</Misconception>
