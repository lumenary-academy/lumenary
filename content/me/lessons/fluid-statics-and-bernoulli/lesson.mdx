<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Fluids at rest and in motion">

A dam in the Andes holds back a lake with a wall thinner at the top than at the bottom. A container ship carrying thousands of tonnes of steel floats, while a steel nut sinks. A car's brakes multiply the push of a foot many times through a few millilitres of fluid. A small tube pointing forward on an aircraft tells the pilot the airspeed. A plain constriction in a water main measures the flow through it with no moving parts.

All of these come from two ideas. The first is **hydrostatics**: in a fluid at rest, pressure depends only on depth. The second is **Bernoulli's equation**: in a steady flow without friction, pressure, speed and height trade off against each other, exactly as the energy of the roller coaster in <LessonLink slug="energy-and-efficiency">Energy and efficiency</LessonLink> traded height for speed. This lesson develops both and puts them to work: on gates and dams, manometers, floating barges, flow meters and airspeed probes. It ends with one of the most widely misunderstood stories in engineering, how a wing makes lift. Friction in flowing fluids, which Bernoulli's equation leaves out, is the subject of <LessonLink slug="viscous-flow-and-pipes">the next lesson</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="pressure" kind="definitions" title="Pressure and hydrostatics">

A **fluid** is a substance that deforms continuously under any shear stress, however small: it cannot resist being sheared at rest. So in a fluid at rest there is no shear stress at all, only **pressure**, a normal compressive stress. Pressure at a point acts equally in all directions (a small free-body diagram of a wedge of fluid proves it, since a wedge with unequal pressures on its faces would accelerate), and it acts perpendicular to any surface the fluid touches.

Pressure is measured relative to two references. **Absolute pressure** is measured from a perfect vacuum; **gauge pressure** is measured relative to the local atmosphere, which at sea level averages 101.325 kPa. A tyre gauge reading 220 kPa means an absolute pressure of about 320 kPa. Thermodynamic formulas such as $pv = RT$ need absolute pressure; structural calculations usually need gauge pressure, because the atmosphere pushes equally on both sides of a wall.

<Theorem name="The hydrostatic equation">

In a fluid at rest under gravity, with $z$ measured upwards, $\dfrac{dp}{dz} = -\rho g$. For a liquid of constant density, the pressure at a depth $h$ below a free surface at pressure $p_0$ is
$$
p = p_0 + \rho g h .
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take a vertical cylinder of fluid of cross-section $A$ between heights $z$ and $z + dz$. Its weight is $\rho g A\,dz$. The only vertical forces on it are the pressures on its ends, $p(z)A$ upwards and $p(z + dz)A$ downwards (pressures on its sides are horizontal). Equilibrium gives $p(z)A - p(z + dz)A - \rho g A\,dz = 0$, so $dp/dz = -\rho g$. With $\rho$ constant, integrating from the surface down a depth $h$ gives $p = p_0 + \rho g h$.

</Proof>

The pressure rises by about 9.8 kPa for every metre of fresh water, so about one atmosphere for every 10 m: a diver at 30 m is under four atmospheres in total. In air, $\rho$ is about 800 times smaller, and also falls with height, so the atmosphere's pressure halves roughly every 5.5 km.

Two consequences surprise most people. First, the pressure at a given depth does not depend on the shape of the container, only on the depth: the **hydrostatic paradox**, described by Simon Stevin in 1586. The base of a narrow pipe 10 m tall feels the same pressure as the base of a lake 10 m deep. Second, a pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of it (**Pascal's principle**). A hydraulic press or car brake applies a small force $F_1$ to a small piston of area $A_1$; the same pressure acts on a large piston of area $A_2$, which pushes with $F_2 = F_1 A_2/A_1$. Energy is still conserved: the large piston moves $A_1/A_2$ as far.

A hydraulic jack is a fine way to lift a car and a dangerous way to hold it up: a seal can leak or a valve be knocked, and the load comes down. That is why workshops require rated axle stands or a proper vehicle lift before anyone goes underneath, and why hydraulic presses carry guards and two-hand controls. A fluid that multiplies force also multiplies the consequences of a failure.

### Manometers

The hydrostatic equation turns a column of liquid into a pressure gauge. A **manometer** is a tube of liquid whose levels respond to the pressures at its two ends. The rule for reading one is simple: start at one end, add $\rho g h$ for each step down through a fluid, subtract it for each step up, and set the result equal to the pressure at the other end. Points at the same height in the *same continuous fluid at rest* are at the same pressure.

<Example title="Reading a manometer">

A water manometer on a gas supply shows a difference in levels of 200 mm. The gauge pressure of the gas is $\rho g h = 998 \times 9.81 \times 0.200 = 1958$ Pa, about 2 kPa: typical of a domestic gas supply, and far too small to read accurately on a dial gauge built for tyres. For large pressures a denser liquid shortens the column. Mercury, 13.6 times denser than water, was the classic choice (hence "millimetres of mercury" for blood pressure), but because its vapour is toxic it has largely been replaced by electronic pressure transducers, which are calibrated against deadweight testers and other primary standards.

</Example>

<Section id="surfaces" kind="computation" title="Forces on submerged surfaces">

Pressure on a flat submerged surface grows linearly with depth, so the total force is the pressure at the **centroid** of the surface times its area, $F = \rho g y_c A$ (in gauge pressure, with $y_c$ the depth of the centroid). That force acts not at the centroid but lower down, at the **centre of pressure**, because the deeper part pushes harder: for a vertical surface, $y_{cp} = y_c + I_c/(y_c A)$, where $I_c$ is the second moment of area of the surface about a horizontal axis through its centroid (the same $I$ you meet for beams in <LessonLink slug="axial-torsion-and-bending">axial load, torsion and bending</LessonLink>). The program checks both formulas by integrating the pressure strip by strip, and then looks at a dam.

```python run
import numpy as np

rho, g = 998.0, 9.81           # fresh water at 20 C (kg/m^3), gravity (m/s^2)

# 1. A vertical rectangular sluice gate, 3.0 m wide and 2.0 m tall, top edge 1.5 m below the surface.
#    Gauge pressure rho g y acts on every strip; add up the strips numerically.
width, top, height = 3.0, 1.5, 2.0
y = np.linspace(top, top + height, 20001)           # depth of each strip (m)
p = rho * g * y                                     # gauge pressure (Pa)
dF = p * width                                      # force per metre of depth (N/m)
F = np.sum(0.5 * (dF[1:] + dF[:-1]) * np.diff(y))   # trapezoidal rule
M = np.sum(0.5 * ((dF * y)[1:] + (dF * y)[:-1]) * np.diff(y))   # moment about the surface
y_cp = M / F                                        # depth of the centre of pressure

# The textbook formulas: F = rho g y_c A, and y_cp = y_c + I / (y_c A)
y_c, A = top + height / 2, width * height
I = width * height**3 / 12                          # second moment of area about the centroid
print(f"gate: F = {F / 1e3:.1f} kN (formula {rho * g * y_c * A / 1e3:.1f} kN)")
print(f"      centre of pressure {y_cp:.3f} m deep (formula {y_c + I / (y_c * A):.3f} m);"
      f" the centroid is at {y_c:.3f} m")

# 2. A dam holding back water 40 m deep: force and overturning moment per metre of length.
H = 40.0
F_dam = rho * g * H**2 / 2                          # triangle of pressure, from 0 to rho g H
print(f"\ndam, 40 m of water: {F_dam / 1e6:.2f} MN per metre of dam, acting {H / 3:.1f} m above the base;")
print(f"     overturning moment about the toe {F_dam * H / 3 / 1e6:.0f} MN m per metre")
print(f"     pressure at the base {rho * g * H / 1e3:.0f} kPa gauge = {rho * g * H / 101325:.1f} atmospheres")
```

The 6 m² gate carries 147 kN, the weight of about 15 tonnes, acting 133 mm below its centroid; its hinges and actuator must be sized for both the force and its line of action. The dam carries 7.83 MN on every metre of its length, and because the force grows with the *square* of the depth, the bottom third of the wall carries more than half of it. That is why gravity dams are triangular in section, thick at the base and thin at the crest, and why their design is checked not only for overturning but also for sliding and for water pressure seeping *under* the base, which pushes the dam up.

</Section>

<Section id="buoyancy" kind="examples" title="Buoyancy and the stability of floating bodies">

<Theorem name="Archimedes' principle">

A body wholly or partly immersed in a fluid at rest experiences an upward force, the **buoyant force**, equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces, acting through the centroid of the displaced volume, called the **centre of buoyancy** $B$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Imagine removing the body and filling its submerged volume with the surrounding fluid. That fluid would be in equilibrium, so the pressures on its surface must exactly support its weight, with a resultant acting through its centre of mass, the centroid of the volume. The pressures on the real body's surface are the same, because they depend only on depth. So the real body feels the same force: the weight of the displaced fluid, acting through the same point.

</Proof>

A floating body sinks until it displaces its own weight. A steel ship floats because its hull encloses a large volume of air below the waterline, so it displaces its own weight of water long before the hull is submerged. A body that cannot displace its own weight even when fully submerged, like a steel nut, sinks.

Floating is not enough: the body must also float the right way up. When a ship heels by a small angle, its weight still acts down through its **centre of gravity** $G$, but the underwater shape changes, so the centre of buoyancy moves sideways to $B'$, towards the side that has gone down. The vertical line through $B'$ meets the ship's centreline at the **metacentre** $M$. If $M$ is above $G$, weight and buoyancy form a couple that rights the ship; if $M$ is below $G$, the couple heels it further.

For small angles the geometry gives a beautifully simple result. The distance from $B$ to $M$ is $BM = I/V$, where $I$ is the second moment of area of the **waterplane** (the ship's footprint at the waterline) about its fore-and-aft centreline and $V$ is the displaced volume. Measuring heights from the keel $K$, the **metacentric height** is
$$
GM = KB + BM - KG ,
$$
and the righting arm at a small heel angle $\varphi$ is $GZ \approx GM \sin\varphi$. A positive $GM$ means stable. For a box-shaped barge of beam $b$ and draft $T$, $KB = T/2$ and $BM = b^2/(12\,T)$: wide and shallow is very stable.

The program loads a 40 m by 10 m barge with deck cargo whose centre of gravity is 6.5 m above the keel, and watches its stability as the cargo grows. (For a box shape the righting arm at larger angles follows the exact "wall-sided" formula $GZ = \sin\varphi\,(GM + \tfrac12 BM\tan^2\varphi)$, valid while the deck edge stays out of the water and the bottom stays in it.)

```python run
import numpy as np

# Stability of a rectangular barge in sea water, loaded with deck cargo.
rho = 1025.0                        # sea water, kg/m^3
L, B, D = 40.0, 10.0, 4.0           # length, beam, depth of hull (m)
m_hull, KG_hull = 300e3, 1.6        # empty barge: mass (kg), centre of gravity above keel (m)
KG_cargo = 6.5                      # centre of gravity of the cargo above the keel (m)

print(" cargo (t)   draft T (m)   KB (m)   BM (m)   KG (m)   GM (m)   GZ at 5 deg (m)")
for m_cargo in [0, 200e3, 400e3, 600e3, 800e3]:
    m = m_hull + m_cargo
    T = m / (rho * L * B)                           # Archimedes: displaced mass = barge mass
    KB = T / 2                                      # centroid of the rectangular underwater section
    BM = (L * B**3 / 12) / (L * B * T)              # I of the waterplane / displaced volume
    KG = (m_hull * KG_hull + m_cargo * KG_cargo) / m
    GM = KB + BM - KG
    phi = np.radians(5)
    GZ = np.sin(phi) * (GM + 0.5 * BM * np.tan(phi)**2)   # wall-sided formula
    flag = "" if GM > 0 else "   <- unstable: it will capsize or loll"
    print(f"{m_cargo / 1e3:8.0f} {T:12.2f} {KB:9.2f} {BM:8.2f} {KG:8.2f} {GM:8.2f} {GZ:12.2f}{flag}")
```

As cargo is added, two things go wrong at once: the centre of gravity rises, and the barge sits deeper, which *reduces* $BM$, because the same waterplane must now support a larger volume. The metacentric height falls from over 10 m to 0.03 m at 600 t, and becomes negative before 800 t. The barge would still float, with nearly 1.3 m of freeboard, and still capsize.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Real stability calculations add effects this model leaves out. Liquid sloshing in a partly filled tank moves towards the low side as the ship heels, which acts like raising $G$ (the **free-surface effect**); wind on high deck cargo, ice on the superstructure, and water trapped on deck all erode the margin. That is why ships carry an approved stability booklet, why loading is checked before departure against the criteria of the International Maritime Organization's Intact Stability Code, and why a ship with a small $GM$ is never "just a bit less stable": it is one gust away from trouble.

</Narrator>

<Section id="bernoulli" kind="definitions" title="Continuity and Bernoulli's equation">

Now let the fluid move. A **streamline** is a curve everywhere tangent to the velocity; in **steady flow** the streamlines stay fixed and each fluid particle follows one. Two laws govern steady flow along a tube of streamlines, such as a pipe.

<Definition term="continuity equation">

Mass is conserved, so in steady flow the mass flow rate is the same through every cross-section: $\dot m = \rho_1 A_1 V_1 = \rho_2 A_2 V_2$. For an incompressible fluid this is $Q = A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$, where $Q$ is the **volume flow rate** in m³/s: where the pipe narrows, the fluid speeds up.

</Definition>

<Theorem name="Bernoulli's equation">

For steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a streamline, with no shaft work and no heat transfer,
$$
p + \tfrac12 \rho V^2 + \rho g z = \text{constant} .
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Apply Newton's second law to a small particle of fluid moving along the streamline, of length $ds$ and cross-section $dA$. With no viscous stresses, the forces along the streamline are the pressure difference $-\frac{\partial p}{\partial s} ds\,dA$ and the component of weight $-\rho g\,dA\,ds\,\frac{dz}{ds}$. In steady flow its acceleration along the streamline is $V\,\frac{dV}{ds}$. So $\rho V\,dV = -dp - \rho g\,dz$, and integrating along the streamline with $\rho$ constant gives $p + \frac12\rho V^2 + \rho g z = \text{constant}$.

</Proof>

Each term is a pressure. $p$ is the **static pressure**, the pressure a gauge moving with the fluid would feel; $\frac12\rho V^2$ is the **dynamic pressure**; their sum is the **stagnation pressure** $p_0$, the pressure where the flow is brought to rest; and $\rho g z$ is the hydrostatic part. Dividing by $\rho g$ gives the same equation in metres of fluid, as **heads**: pressure head $p/\rho g$, velocity head $V^2/2g$ and elevation $z$, which is how pump and pipe engineers usually write it.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Bernoulli's equation is the steady-flow energy equation of <LessonLink slug="first-law-of-thermodynamics">the first law</LessonLink> for a special case. With no heat and no work, $h + V^2/2 + gz$ is constant; for an incompressible fluid without friction the internal energy does not change, so $h$ changes only by $\Delta p/\rho$, and what remains, multiplied by $\rho$, is Bernoulli. Seen this way, the equation is an energy budget per unit volume, and friction is what the next lesson adds as a "head loss".

</Narrator>

Every condition in the theorem matters, and most misuse of Bernoulli's equation comes from forgetting one:

- **steady** — a flow that is starting, stopping or pulsing needs an extra term;
- **incompressible** — fine for liquids, and for gases at low Mach number ($V$ below about a third of the speed of sound), as the computation below shows;
- **inviscid** — friction converts mechanical energy into internal energy, so in long pipes, through valves and in wakes the "constant" falls;
- **along one streamline** — comparing points on different streamlines is allowed only if all streamlines start from the same uniform reservoir (irrotational flow);
- **no pumps, fans or turbines in between** — they add or remove energy.

</Section>

<Section id="meters" kind="computation" title="Venturi meters, Pitot tubes and draining tanks">

### The Venturi meter

A **Venturi meter** is a smooth contraction in a pipe followed by a gentle expansion. Continuity says the fluid speeds up in the narrow **throat**; Bernoulli says its pressure must therefore drop. Measure the pressure difference and you know the flow. Combining $A_1V_1 = A_2V_2$ with $p_1 + \frac12\rho V_1^2 = p_2 + \frac12\rho V_2^2$ gives
$$
Q = C_d A_2 \sqrt{\frac{2\,(p_1 - p_2)}{\rho\,\bigl(1 - (A_2/A_1)^2\bigr)}} ,
$$
where the **discharge coefficient** $C_d$, measured in calibration, corrects for the small effects of friction and the velocity profile: about 0.98 to 0.995 for a well-made Venturi, and about 0.6 for a sharp-edged orifice plate, where the flow separates and much more energy is lost.

### The Pitot-static tube

A **Pitot tube** is a small tube pointing into the flow. Fluid is brought to rest at its open tip, so it senses the stagnation pressure $p_0$. Small holes in the side of an outer tube, parallel to the flow, sense the static pressure $p$. Bernoulli between the free stream and the tip gives $V = \sqrt{2(p_0 - p)/\rho}$. Aircraft, racing cars, wind tunnels and drones all use this; the program also checks how far the incompressible formula can be trusted for air, against the exact compressible relation.

```python run
import numpy as np

g = 9.81

# 1. A Venturi meter in a water main: 100 mm pipe, 50 mm throat.
#    A mercury-under-water manometer between inlet and throat reads 120 mm.
rho_w, rho_hg = 998.0, 13546.0                 # kg/m^3 at 20 C
d1, d2, h = 0.100, 0.050, 0.120
A1, A2 = np.pi * d1**2 / 4, np.pi * d2**2 / 4
dp = (rho_hg - rho_w) * g * h                  # manometer: the water columns above cancel
V2 = np.sqrt(2 * dp / (rho_w * (1 - (A2 / A1)**2)))   # Bernoulli + continuity
Q_ideal = A2 * V2
Cd = 0.98                                      # typical discharge coefficient of a Venturi
print(f"Venturi: dp = {dp / 1e3:.2f} kPa, throat velocity {V2:.2f} m/s,"
      f" flow {Q_ideal * 1000:.1f} L/s ideal, {Cd * Q_ideal * 1000:.1f} L/s with Cd = {Cd}")

# 2. Pitot-static tubes in air: speed from (stagnation - static) pressure.
rho_air, gam, p_s = 1.204, 1.4, 101325.0       # air at 20 C and sea-level pressure
a = np.sqrt(gam * p_s / rho_air)               # speed of sound, m/s
print(f"\nspeed of sound {a:.0f} m/s")
print("  true V (m/s)   Mach   p0 - p (Pa)   Bernoulli V (m/s)   error")
for V in [10.0, 30.0, 70.0, 100.0, 150.0, 200.0, 250.0]:
    M = V / a
    p0 = p_s * (1 + (gam - 1) / 2 * M**2) ** (gam / (gam - 1))   # compressible, isentropic
    V_b = np.sqrt(2 * (p0 - p_s) / rho_air)                      # incompressible Bernoulli
    print(f"{V:12.0f} {M:8.2f} {p0 - p_s:12.0f} {V_b:16.1f} {(V_b - V) / V:10.1%}")
```

The Venturi reads 14.77 kPa and a flow of 10.8 L/s, with no moving parts and very little permanent pressure loss, which is why Venturis are used in water mains, irrigation and process plants, following the procedures of ISO 5167. The Pitot table shows that incompressible Bernoulli is excellent at the speeds of cars, drones and light aircraft (errors well under 1% below Mach 0.2) and still usable at Mach 0.3, but at airliner speeds the error grows to several per cent. Air-data computers on aircraft therefore use the compressible relation.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Look at the size of the signal. At 10 m/s, a brisk breeze, the Pitot tube sees only 60 Pa, six-hundredths of a percent of atmospheric pressure; doubling the speed quadruples it. That square law is why airspeed probes struggle at low speed, why drone and wind-tunnel probes use sensitive differential transducers, and why a partly blocked Pitot tube, by ice or by an insect nest, has been a factor in serious aircraft accidents. Aircraft probes are heated and covered on the ground for that reason.

</Narrator>

### Draining a tank

Bernoulli between the free surface of a tank and a small hole a depth $h$ below gives the jet speed $V = \sqrt{2gh}$ (**Torricelli's law**, from the 1640s), the speed of a body falling through $h$. As the tank drains, $h$ falls and so does the flow, which makes the draining time a differential equation: $A_\text{tank}\,dh/dt = -C_d\,a\sqrt{2gh}$. It has an exact solution, but the program solves it numerically with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, as you would for a tank of any shape, and checks the answer.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A tank draining through a hole in its base (Torricelli + continuity), integrated in time.
g = 9.81
A_tank = 1.2                     # m^2: cross-section of the tank
d_hole, Cd = 0.030, 0.61         # sharp-edged 30 mm hole and its discharge coefficient
a_hole = np.pi * d_hole**2 / 4
h0 = 2.0                         # m: initial depth of water

def dhdt(h):
    """Continuity: A_tank dh/dt = -Cd a sqrt(2 g h); Bernoulli gives the jet speed."""
    return -Cd * a_hole * np.sqrt(2 * g * max(h, 0.0)) / A_tank

# Fourth-order Runge-Kutta with a fixed step, recording when the depth passes each mark.
dt, t, h = 0.5, 0.0, h0
marks = [1.5, 1.0, 0.5, 0.1]
print(" depth (m)   time, numerical (s)   time, exact (s)")
k_exact = Cd * a_hole * np.sqrt(2 * g) / (2 * A_tank)   # exact: sqrt(h) = sqrt(h0) - k t
while marks:
    k1 = dhdt(h)
    k2 = dhdt(h + dt / 2 * k1)
    k3 = dhdt(h + dt / 2 * k2)
    k4 = dhdt(h + dt * k3)
    h_new = h + dt / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)
    if h_new <= marks[0]:
        t_cross = t + dt * (h - marks[0]) / (h - h_new)          # linear interpolation in the step
        t_exact = (np.sqrt(h0) - np.sqrt(marks[0])) / k_exact
        print(f"{marks.pop(0):9.1f} {t_cross:16.0f} {t_exact:18.0f}")
    t, h = t + dt, h_new
print(f"\ntime to empty completely: {np.sqrt(h0) / k_exact:.0f} s ({np.sqrt(h0) / k_exact / 60:.1f} min);"
      f" the first half of the depth goes in {(np.sqrt(h0) - np.sqrt(h0 / 2)) / k_exact:.0f} s")
```

The tank takes 521 s to lose the first metre and 1256 s more to lose the second, because the jet slows as the head falls. The same reasoning sizes the outlets of water towers, fuel tanks and the emergency drains of process vessels.

<Section id="lift" kind="misconceptions" title="Bernoulli and the lift on a wing">

Many books, websites and even pilot manuals explain lift like this: air going over the curved top of a wing has further to travel than air going underneath; the two parcels must meet again at the trailing edge, so the top air goes faster, and by Bernoulli its pressure is lower. Almost every step is wrong.

<Misconception>

**"The air over the top must rejoin the air underneath at the trailing edge."** No law of physics requires this, and it does not happen. Wind-tunnel smoke pictures and computations show that air over the top of a lifting wing arrives at the trailing edge well *before* the air that went underneath. The real speed difference is much larger than "equal transit time" would predict, so the theory gets even the size of the lift badly wrong.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Lift needs a curved upper surface."** A flat plate at an angle to the flow makes lift; so do paper aeroplanes, symmetric aerobatic wings, and ordinary wings flying upside down at a suitable angle of attack. Camber helps a wing make lift efficiently, but the essential ingredient is that the wing turns the flow.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"It is either Bernoulli or Newton."** Both are true at once. A wing at an angle of attack, with a sharp trailing edge, deflects a large mass of air downwards (downwash); by Newton's third law the air pushes the wing up. The force is transmitted through the pressure field: the flow curving around the wing needs lower pressure above and higher pressure below, and in the nearly frictionless flow outside the thin boundary layer the speeds and pressures along each streamline obey Bernoulli's equation. Momentum and pressure are two accounts of the same force.

</Misconception>

What, then, decides how fast the air goes over the top? The wing's shape and angle, together with one condition that viscosity enforces: the flow must leave the sharp trailing edge smoothly, rather than whipping around it (the **Kutta condition**). That fixes a net circulation of air around the wing, and the lift per unit span is $L' = \rho V \Gamma$, where $\Gamma$ is that circulation (the **Kutta–Joukowski theorem**, 1902–1906). Today engineers compute lift with the panel methods and the full simulations of <LessonLink slug="computational-fluid-dynamics">computational fluid dynamics</LessonLink>, and confirm it in wind tunnels.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The classroom demonstration of blowing over a sheet of paper is often explained with Bernoulli, wrongly. The jet from your mouth leaves at roughly atmospheric pressure, and the still air under the paper did not come from the same reservoir, so Bernoulli cannot compare them. The paper rises because the jet follows its curved surface, and a flow can only curve if the pressure on the inside of the curve is lower. Check the conditions before you use the equation.

</Narrator>

### Two more misconceptions

<Misconception>

**"A wider dam, or a bigger lake behind it, means more pressure on the dam."** Pressure depends only on depth. A dam holding back a reservoir 40 m deep feels the same pressure whether the reservoir is a narrow gorge or a lake 50 km long; what grows with the length of the dam is the total force, because there are more metres of wall.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Wherever a fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower."** Only along a streamline, in steady flow without friction or added energy. A fan or pump makes the air or water both faster *and* higher in pressure, because it adds energy; a jet leaving a nozzle into the open air moves fast at atmospheric pressure.

</Misconception>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="In professional practice">

- **Pressure measurement** today is mostly electronic: piezoresistive and capacitive transducers, calibrated against deadweight testers that balance a known mass on a precisely made piston, a direct application of $p = F/A$. Manometers survive as simple, trustworthy references.
- **Flow metering** of water, gas and steam for billing and process control relies on differential-pressure meters (Venturis, nozzles and orifice plates) designed to ISO 5167 or ASME's MFC standards, alongside electromagnetic, ultrasonic and Coriolis meters.
- **Ship and offshore stability** is computed by naval architects with hydrostatics software over the full range of heel angles and loading conditions, and approved by classification societies against the Intact Stability Code.
- **Pressure testing.** Pressure vessels and pipelines are proof-tested before service, and the codes (such as the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code) prefer testing with water rather than gas: water is nearly incompressible, so it stores little energy, and a failure under hydrostatic test releases a leak rather than a blast. Such tests are done behind barriers by trained staff, never improvised.
- **Dams** are designed and monitored under national dam-safety regulations, with instruments measuring the water pressure under and inside the structure throughout its life.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

Archimedes of Syracuse stated the principle of buoyancy in the third century BC, in *On Floating Bodies*, which also analysed the stability of floating paraboloids, an early theory of the metacentre. Simon Stevin of Bruges described the hydrostatic paradox in 1586. Evangelista Torricelli built the mercury barometer in 1643 and derived the law of efflux from a hole; Blaise Pascal arranged for his brother-in-law Florin Périer to carry a barometer up the Puy de Dôme in 1648, showing that air pressure falls with height. Daniel Bernoulli published *Hydrodynamica* in 1738, relating pressure and speed, and Leonhard Euler derived the general equations of frictionless flow in the 1750s, from which Bernoulli's equation follows. Henri Pitot measured the flow of the Seine with his tube in 1732, Giovanni Battista Venturi studied flow through contractions in 1797, and Clemens Herschel turned the idea into the Venturi meter in 1887. Martin Kutta in Germany and Nikolai Zhukovsky (Joukowski) in Russia explained lift through circulation in the first years of powered flight.
