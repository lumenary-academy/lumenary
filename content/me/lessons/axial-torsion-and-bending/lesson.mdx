<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Three ways to carry a load">

Look at almost any machine or structure and you will find long, slender parts: the tie rods of a roof, the drive shaft between a motor and a pump, the joists under a floor, the axle of a bicycle, the boom of an excavator. A slender part can carry load in only three basic ways. It can be **pulled or pushed along its length** (axial load), **twisted about its length** (torsion), or **bent across its length** (bending). Most real parts do two or three at once, but each of the three has its own formula, and the formulas add.

In <LessonLink slug="stress-and-strain">Stress and strain</LessonLink> you learned what stress and strain are and how Hooke's law links them. This lesson turns that vocabulary into the working tools of a design office: how far a bar stretches, how much a shaft twists and how hard it is stressed, where a beam is most heavily loaded, and why steel beams are shaped like a capital I. By the end you will have computed the shear-force and bending-moment diagrams of the beam you met in <LessonLink slug="statics-and-equilibrium">Statics and equilibrium</LessonLink>, and chosen a steel section for it.

</Section>

<Section id="recipe" kind="intuition" title="One recipe, three answers">

Every formula in this lesson comes from the same three ingredients, used in the same order.

1. **Geometry of deformation.** Guess, from symmetry and experiment, how the part deforms. A bar under axial load stretches uniformly. A circular shaft twists so that each cross-section turns as a rigid disc. A beam bends so that cross-sections stay flat and simply tilt. Each guess gives the strain at every point in terms of one number: the stretch, the angle of twist, or the curvature.
2. **The material law.** Hooke's law turns strain into stress: $\sigma = E\varepsilon$ or $\tau = G\gamma$.
3. **Equilibrium.** Add up the stresses over a cross-section. Their total must equal the internal force or moment that statics says the cut carries: the axial force $N$, the torque $T$, or the bending moment $M$.

The result each time is a formula of the form *stress = load × position ÷ a property of the cross-section*, and *deformation = load × length ÷ (material stiffness × the same property)*. The property is the area $A$ for axial load, the polar second moment $J$ for torsion, and the second moment of area $I$ for bending.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Only step 1 is a guess, and it is a guess about how the part moves, not about forces. That tells you where to trust these formulas when you design: along the length of a slender part, yes; at holes, shoulders, keyways and the points where loads go in, no, because there the cross-sections do not deform as neatly as the guess assumes. Those are the places a designer gives generous fillets and checks more carefully.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="axial" kind="definitions" title="Axial load">

A bar of length $L$, cross-sectional area $A$ and Young's modulus $E$ carries an axial force $N$ (tension positive). Its strain is $\varepsilon = \sigma/E = N/(EA)$ everywhere, so it stretches by

$$\delta = \frac{NL}{EA}.$$

<Definition term="axial stiffness">

A bar behaves as a spring of stiffness $k = EA/L$: force divided by stretch. Bars joined end to end (**in series**) carry the same force and their stretches add; bars side by side between rigid plates (**in parallel**) stretch by the same amount and their forces add. If the force or the area varies along the bar, add up the stretch of each small piece: $\delta = \int_0^L N(x)\,/\,(E A(x))\,dx$.

</Definition>

The spring picture is more than an analogy. It is exactly how the <LessonLink slug="finite-element-method">finite element method</LessonLink> begins: chop a structure into springs, write each one's stiffness, and assemble.

<Example title="A concrete column with steel bars">

A short concrete column, 300 mm square, is reinforced with four steel bars of 20 mm diameter and carries 1200 kN. Take $E_c = 30$ GPa for the concrete and $E_s = 200$ GPa for the steel. How is the load shared?

Statics alone cannot say: there is one equation ($N_c + N_s = 1200$ kN) and two unknowns. This is the axial version of a **statically indeterminate** problem, and the missing equation is **compatibility**: the steel is bonded to the concrete, so both shorten by the same amount and have the same strain $\varepsilon$. Then $N_c = E_c A_c \varepsilon$, $N_s = E_s A_s \varepsilon$, and

$$\varepsilon = \frac{N}{E_c A_c + E_s A_s}.$$

The steel area is $A_s = 4 \times \pi \times 10^2 = 1257\ \text{mm}^2$ and the concrete area is the rest, $A_c = 88\,743\ \text{mm}^2$. So $E_cA_c + E_sA_s = 2.66 \times 10^9 + 0.25 \times 10^9 = 2.91 \times 10^9$ N and $\varepsilon = 4.12 \times 10^{-4}$. The stresses are $\sigma_c = E_c\varepsilon = 12.4$ MPa and $\sigma_s = E_s\varepsilon = 82.4$ MPa. The steel, 1.4% of the area, carries 104 kN, 8.6% of the load, because it is more than six times as stiff. A 3 m column shortens by $\varepsilon L = 1.24$ mm.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

The "same strain" assumption hides a time scale. Concrete *creeps*: under a sustained load it keeps shortening for years, and it shrinks as it dries. The steel resists both, so over time load migrates from the concrete into the bars, and the steel stress can end up two or three times the short-term value. Design codes for concrete account for this; a one-line Hooke's-law calculation does not.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="torsion" kind="definitions" title="Torsion of circular shafts">

A shaft transmits power by turning while it carries a torque $T$. If it turns at angular speed $\omega$ (in rad/s), the power is

$$P = T\omega, \qquad \omega = \frac{2\pi n}{60} \text{ for } n \text{ in revolutions per minute}.$$

So a given power at low speed needs a large torque, which is why the slow output shaft of a gearbox is much thicker than the fast input shaft.

<Theorem name="Torsion of a circular shaft">

A solid or hollow circular shaft of length $L$ and shear modulus $G$, carrying a torque $T$, twists through an angle

$$\varphi = \frac{TL}{GJ},$$

and the shear stress at radius $r$ is $\tau = Tr/J$, largest at the outer surface. Here $J$ is the **polar second moment of area**: $J = \pi D^4/32$ for a solid shaft of diameter $D$, and $J = \pi(D^4 - d^4)/32$ for a tube with inner diameter $d$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Geometry.* By symmetry, each cross-section of a circular shaft turns as a rigid disc and stays flat; radii stay straight. If the section at $x$ has turned by $\varphi(x)$, a thin tube of material at radius $r$ is sheared: a line drawn along it, originally parallel to the axis, tilts by the angle $\gamma = r\,d\varphi/dx$. *Material.* Hooke's law in shear gives $\tau = G\gamma = G r\,d\varphi/dx$, growing linearly from zero on the axis. *Equilibrium.* The shear stresses on the cross-section must add up to the torque: $T = \int_A \tau\, r\, dA = G\,\frac{d\varphi}{dx}\int_A r^2\,dA = GJ\,\frac{d\varphi}{dx}$, where $J = \int_A r^2\,dA$. So $d\varphi/dx = T/(GJ)$, constant along a uniform shaft, which integrates to $\varphi = TL/(GJ)$, and substituting back gives $\tau = Tr/J$. For a disc, $J = \int_0^{D/2} r^2 \, 2\pi r\,dr = \pi D^4/32$; a tube subtracts the hole.

</Proof>

<Example title="A pump shaft">

A 30 kW electric motor turning at 1470 rpm drives a pump through a solid steel shaft of diameter 30 mm and length 1.2 m ($G = 80$ GPa). The angular speed is $\omega = 2\pi \times 1470/60 = 153.9$ rad/s, so the torque is $T = 30\,000/153.9 = 195$ N m. The polar second moment is $J = \pi \times 30^4/32 = 79\,520\ \text{mm}^4$, so the largest shear stress is

$$\tau = \frac{Tr}{J} = \frac{195 \times 10^3 \times 15}{79\,520} = 36.8\ \text{MPa},$$

and the shaft twists by $\varphi = TL/(GJ) = 0.0368$ rad, or 2.1°, from end to end. For a solid shaft it is handy to write $\tau = 16T/(\pi D^3)$: the stress falls with the *cube* of the diameter.

</Example>

The stress is zero on the axis and greatest at the surface, so the material near the centre of a solid shaft does little work. A tube puts its material where the stress is high. The block below sizes shafts for the pump, all working at the same 40 MPa, from solid ($d/D = 0$) to thin-walled.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A 30 kW motor turning at 1470 rpm drives a pump through a steel shaft.
P, rpm = 30e3, 1470.0                       # W, rev/min
G, rho = 80e3, 7850e-9                      # MPa (N/mm^2), kg/mm^3
tau_allow = 40.0                            # MPa, a working shear stress with margin for fatigue

omega = 2 * np.pi * rpm / 60                # rad/s
T = P / omega * 1e3                         # N mm
print(f"torque T = P / omega = {T / 1e3:.0f} N m")

print("\n d/D    D (mm)   d (mm)   mass (kg/m)   twist (deg/m)")
for k in [0.0, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9]:
    # tau = T (D/2) / J with J = pi D^4 (1 - k^4) / 32, solved for D
    D = (16 * T / (np.pi * tau_allow * (1 - k**4))) ** (1 / 3)
    J = np.pi * D**4 * (1 - k**4) / 32
    mass = rho * np.pi * D**2 * (1 - k**2) / 4 * 1000          # per metre
    twist = np.degrees(T * 1000 / (G * J))                    # over 1 m
    print(f" {k:3.1f}   {D:6.1f}   {k * D:6.1f}   {mass:9.2f}     {twist:8.3f}")
```

A solid shaft needs a diameter of 29.2 mm and weighs 5.25 kg per metre. A tube with $d/D = 0.8$ is 34.8 mm across and weighs 2.68 kg per metre: the same strength for 51% of the steel, and it is stiffer in twist too. That is why vehicle propeller shafts, bicycle frames and aircraft control rods are tubes. The limit is the wall: very thin tubes wrinkle and buckle locally under torsion, and they are harder to join to gears and couplings.

<Narrator who="margaret">

A piece of chalk makes the stress state visible, safely, at a kitchen table. In pure torsion the surface is in pure shear, so by Mohr's circle the principal stresses are $+\tau$ and $-\tau$ on planes at 45° to the axis. Twist a stick of chalk until it snaps: brittle chalk fails in tension, so it breaks along a 45° helix. A ductile steel shaft, which fails in shear, breaks straight across instead.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="shear-moment" kind="examples" title="Shear force and bending moment diagrams">

A beam is a slender part loaded across its length. Cut it at a distance $x$ from the left end and draw the left piece as a free body. To keep it in equilibrium, the right piece must exert on it a vertical force and a couple:

<Definition term="shear force and bending moment">

The **shear force** $V(x)$ is the vertical internal force at a cut, and the **bending moment** $M(x)$ is the internal couple. With the convention used here, $V(x)$ is the sum of all the vertical forces acting on the beam to the left of the cut (upwards positive), and $M(x)$ is the sum of their moments about the cut, positive when it makes the beam **sag** (concave upwards, top squeezed, bottom stretched). Plots of $V$ and $M$ along the beam are its **shear-force diagram** and **bending-moment diagram**.

</Definition>

Two relations make the diagrams easy to sketch. If $q(x)$ is the distributed load per unit length (upwards positive), then

$$\frac{dV}{dx} = q(x), \qquad \frac{dM}{dx} = V(x).$$

Both are simply the equilibrium of a slice of beam of length $dx$: vertical forces give $dV = q\,dx$, and moments about one face give $dM = V\,dx$. So a concentrated force makes $V$ jump by the force; a uniform load makes $V$ slope and $M$ curve as a parabola; and wherever $V$ passes through zero, $M$ has a maximum or minimum. That gives a designer a fast sketch: draw the shear diagram straight from the loads and find where it changes sign. That is where the largest bending moment, the number that sizes the beam, will be, and its value is the area under the shear diagram up to that point.

Here is the beam from <LessonLink slug="statics-and-equilibrium">Statics and equilibrium</LessonLink>: 6 m long, pinned at A, on a roller at B 5 m along, with an 8 kN load tilted 30° from the vertical at 2 m and 2 kN/m over the last 3 m. Statics gave the reactions $A_y = 4.76$ kN and $B_y = 8.17$ kN. The program computes $V$ and $M$ at any cut by adding up the forces to its left, exactly as the definition says.

```python run
import numpy as np

# The beam from the statics lesson: pin at A (x = 0), roller at B (x = 5 m), overhang to 6 m.
# Vertical loads (kN, upwards positive): the tilted 8 kN load's vertical part at x = 2 m and
# 2 kN/m from x = 3 m to the end. (The load's 4 kN horizontal part only stretches the first 2 m.)
L, xB = 6.0, 5.0
point = [(2.0, -8.0 * np.cos(np.radians(30)))]           # (position m, force kN)
w, x1, x2 = -2.0, 3.0, 6.0                                # spread load (kN/m) and where it acts

# Reactions from statics: moments about A, then vertical equilibrium
W = w * (x2 - x1)
By = -(sum(x * P for x, P in point) + W * (x1 + x2) / 2) / xB
Ay = -(sum(P for _, P in point) + W) - By
point += [(0.0, Ay), (xB, By)]
print(f"reactions: Ay = {Ay:.2f} kN, By = {By:.2f} kN")

def V_and_M(x):
    """Shear force and bending moment at a cut at x, from everything to the left of the cut."""
    V = M = 0.0
    for xp, P in point:
        if xp <= x:
            V += P
            M += P * (x - xp)
    a = min(max(x - x1, 0.0), x2 - x1)                     # length of spread load left of the cut
    V += w * a
    M += w * a * (x - x1 - a / 2)                          # its resultant acts at its middle
    return V, M

eps = 1e-9
print("\n  x (m)    V (kN)   M (kN m)   bending moment diagram (each # = 0.5 kN m)")
for x in [0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 - eps, 2 + eps, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5 - eps, 5 + eps, 5.5, 6 - eps]:
    V, M = V_and_M(x)
    M = 0.0 if abs(M) < 1e-6 else M
    bar = "#" * int(round(abs(M) / 0.5))
    print(f"  {x:5.2f}   {V:6.2f}   {M:7.2f}    {'+' if M > 0.01 else '-' if M < -0.01 else ' '}{bar}")

xs = np.linspace(0.0, L - eps, 6001)
M = np.array([V_and_M(x)[1] for x in xs])
V = np.array([V_and_M(x)[0] for x in xs])
print(f"\nlargest positive moment {M.max():5.2f} kN m at x = {xs[M.argmax()]:.2f} m")
print(f"largest negative moment {M.min():5.2f} kN m at x = {xs[M.argmin()]:.2f} m")
print(f"largest shear force     {abs(V).max():5.2f} kN just left of B")
i = np.where((M[:-1] > 0) & (M[1:] <= 0))[0][0]
print(f"the moment changes sign at x = {xs[i]:.2f} m (the point of contraflexure)")
```

<Section id="bending-stress" kind="definitions" title="Bending stress and the second moment of area">

Now look inside the beam. Experiments, and symmetry for a straight beam under a constant moment, show that **plane cross-sections remain plane**: they tilt but do not warp. When the beam bends into an arc of radius $\rho$, fibres on the inside of the curve shorten and fibres on the outside lengthen. Somewhere between there is a surface that keeps its length, the **neutral surface**; where it meets a cross-section is the **neutral axis**.

<Theorem name="The flexure formula">

For a beam of linear-elastic material bending about a principal axis of its cross-section, the neutral axis passes through the **centroid** of the section, the curvature is

$$\frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{M}{EI},$$

and the normal stress at height $y$ above the neutral axis is

$$\sigma = -\frac{My}{I}, \qquad I = \int_A y^2\,dA,$$

where $I$ is the **second moment of area** of the cross-section about the neutral axis. The largest stress is at the fibre farthest from the neutral axis, at distance $c$: $\sigma_\text{max} = M c / I = M / S$, where $S = I/c$ is the **elastic section modulus**.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Geometry.* A fibre at height $y$ above the neutral surface lies on an arc of radius $\rho - y$ when the beam sags with radius $\rho$, so its strain is $\varepsilon = ((\rho - y)\theta - \rho\theta)/(\rho\theta) = -y/\rho$. *Material.* $\sigma = E\varepsilon = -Ey/\rho$: the stress varies linearly across the depth. *Equilibrium.* There is no axial force, so $\int_A \sigma\,dA = -(E/\rho)\int_A y\,dA = 0$, which says that $y$ is measured from the centroid: the neutral axis passes through it. The stresses must add up to the bending moment: $M = -\int_A \sigma y\,dA = (E/\rho)\int_A y^2\,dA = EI/\rho$. So $1/\rho = M/(EI)$, and substituting $E/\rho = M/I$ into $\sigma = -Ey/\rho$ gives $\sigma = -My/I$.

</Proof>

For a sagging (positive) moment the top fibres ($y \gt 0$) are in compression and the bottom fibres in tension, as the sponge beam in <LessonLink slug="structures">Structures</LessonLink> showed.

### Computing I: the parallel-axis theorem

For a rectangle of width $b$ and depth $h$, bending about the axis through its middle, $I = bh^3/12$ and $S = bh^2/6$. The cube is why depth matters so much more than width. Real sections are built from rectangles, and the **parallel-axis theorem** moves each piece's own second moment to the section's centroid:

$$I = \sum_i \left( I_i + A_i d_i^2 \right),$$

where $I_i$ is piece $i$'s second moment about its own centroid, $A_i$ its area and $d_i$ the distance from its centroid to the section's. The $A_i d_i^2$ terms reward area placed far from the neutral axis, and they dominate in an I-section.

<Example title="Choosing a section for the beam">

The beam above must carry $M = 9.51$ kN m. Try an I-section 160 mm deep with flanges 80 mm wide and 7 mm thick and a web 5 mm thick. Its area is $2(80 \times 7) + 146 \times 5 = 1850\ \text{mm}^2$. Treating it as a solid $80 \times 160$ rectangle minus two $37.5 \times 146$ gaps, $I = (80 \times 160^3 - 75 \times 146^3)/12 = 7.86 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^4$, so $S = I/80 = 98\,200\ \text{mm}^3$ and

$$\sigma_\text{max} = \frac{M}{S} = \frac{9.51 \times 10^6\ \text{N mm}}{98\,200\ \text{mm}^3} = 97\ \text{MPa},$$

comfortably below the yield strength of a common structural steel (235–355 MPa). The program compares it with other shapes made from the same 1850 mm² of steel.

</Example>

```python run
import numpy as np

def section(rects):
    """Area, centroid height and second moment of area about the centroid of a section
    made of rectangles (width b, height h, y of the rectangle's own centroid), in mm."""
    A = sum(b * h for b, h, y in rects)
    yc = sum(b * h * y for b, h, y in rects) / A
    I = sum(b * h**3 / 12 + b * h * (y - yc) ** 2 for b, h, y in rects)   # parallel-axis theorem
    return A, yc, I

def I_beam(h, b, tf, tw):
    """A symmetric I-section: two flanges b x tf and a web tw thick, overall depth h (mm)."""
    return [(b, tf, tf / 2), (tw, h - 2 * tf, h / 2), (b, tf, h - tf / 2)]

# The largest moment in our beam, 9.51 kN m, and a steel with 7850 kg/m^3
M = 9.51e6                                                 # N mm
shapes = {                                                 # width x depth
    "I-section 160 x 80 (tf 7, tw 5)": I_beam(160, 80, 7, 5),
    "solid square 43 x 43":            [(43, 43, 21.5)],
    "flat bar 92.5 x 20, flat":        [(92.5, 20, 10)],
    "flat bar 20 x 92.5, on edge":     [(20, 92.5, 46.25)],
    "square tube 100 x 100 x 4.8":     [(100, 4.8, 2.4), (4.8, 90.4, 50), (4.8, 90.4, 50), (100, 4.8, 97.6)],
}
print(f"{'section':34} {'A (mm2)':>8} {'I (cm4)':>8} {'S (cm3)':>8} {'stress (MPa)':>13} {'kg/m':>6}")
for name, rects in shapes.items():
    A, yc, I = section(rects)
    c = max(yc, max(y + h / 2 for b, h, y in rects) - yc)   # distance to the farthest fibre
    S = I / c
    print(f"{name:34} {A:8.0f} {I / 1e4:8.1f} {S / 1e3:8.1f} {M / S:13.0f} {A * 7850e-6:6.1f}")

# Check the I-section by brute force: slice it into thin horizontal strips and add up y^2 dA
h, b, tf, tw = 160, 80, 7, 5
y = np.linspace(-h / 2, h / 2, 160001)
width = np.where(np.abs(y) > h / 2 - tf, b, tw)
dy = y[1] - y[0]
print(f"\nI by strips = {np.sum(y**2 * width) * dy / 1e4:.1f} cm4")
```

Every section weighs 14.5 kg per metre, yet the stresses range from 97 MPa to over 1500 MPa. The I-section's second moment, 786 cm⁴, is 28 times that of the solid square and six times that of the same steel as a plank on its edge. The square tube is a respectable second at 172 MPa, and it has an advantage the table does not show: it is stiff in every direction and strong in torsion, while the I-section is weak about its vertical axis and twists easily. The brute-force sum of $y^2\,dA$ over thin strips agrees with the parallel-axis theorem to four figures.

What about the 4 kN horizontal push from the tilted load? It puts the first 2 m of the beam in axial tension, and stresses from different loadings simply add: $\sigma = N/A - My/I$. Here $N/A = 4000/1850 = 2.2$ MPa against a bending stress of 97 MPa, so it is worth a sentence in the calculation and no more. In a column carrying a heavy load slightly off centre, the two terms are comparable and both matter.

</Section>

<Section id="beam-shear" kind="human" title="Shear stress in beams">

Bending is not the whole story. A beam also carries the shear force $V$, and that produces shear stress on its cross-sections and, less obviously, on horizontal planes along its length.

<Analogy>

Lay three planks on top of one another across a gap and stand in the middle: they bend separately, and their ends slide past one another. Glue them together and the stack becomes far stiffer, because the glue stops the sliding. The glue is carrying **horizontal shear**. Every solid beam carries the same shear on internal planes, which is why glued-laminated timber depends on its glue lines, and why the welds or bolts joining a steel girder's flanges to its web must be designed for it.

</Analogy>

The size of that shear follows from equilibrium. Take a slice of beam of length $dx$ and look only at the part of it above some height $y$. The bending stresses on its two ends differ, because the moment changes by $dM = V\,dx$ along the slice; the difference must be balanced by a shear stress $\tau$ on its bottom face, of width $b$. Working it through gives Zhuravskii's formula:

$$\tau(y) = \frac{V\,Q(y)}{I\,b(y)}, \qquad Q(y) = \int_{A'} y'\,dA',$$

where $Q(y)$ is the first moment, about the neutral axis, of the area $A'$ above height $y$. By the symmetry of shear stress, the same $\tau$ acts vertically on the cross-section at that height.

For a rectangle the distribution is a parabola: zero at the top and bottom faces, which are free surfaces, and greatest at the neutral axis, where it is $\tau_\text{max} = 1.5\,V/A$, half as much again as the average. In an I-section, $Q$ hardly changes across the web while $b$ is small there, so the web carries almost all the shear at a nearly uniform stress. The program computes both for the largest shear force in our beam, 6.17 kN.

```python run
import numpy as np

def shear_flow_profile(y, width, V):
    """Shear stress tau = V Q / (I b) across a symmetric section sampled at heights y (mm)."""
    dy = y[1] - y[0]
    I = np.sum(y**2 * width) * dy
    # Q(y): first moment, about the neutral axis, of the area above height y
    Q = np.cumsum((y * width)[::-1])[::-1] * dy
    return V * Q / (I * width), I

V = 6.17e3                                                 # N, the largest shear in our beam
h, b, tf, tw = 160.0, 80.0, 7.0, 5.0
y = np.linspace(-h / 2, h / 2, 16001)
w_I = np.where(np.abs(y) > h / 2 - tf, b, tw)
tau_I, I = shear_flow_profile(y, w_I, V)

A = 2 * b * tf + (h - 2 * tf) * tw                         # 1850 mm2
w_R = np.full_like(y, A / h)                               # a rectangle of the same depth and area
tau_R, I_R = shear_flow_profile(y, w_R, V)

print("  y (mm)   I-section: tau (MPa)                         rectangle: tau (MPa)")
for yy in [80, 74, 73.01, 72.99, 60, 40, 20, 0]:
    k = np.argmin(abs(y - yy))
    print(f"  {yy:6.2f}   {tau_I[k]:5.2f} {'#' * int(tau_I[k] * 4):36} {tau_R[k]:5.2f}")

web = np.abs(y) < h / 2 - tf
dy = y[1] - y[0]
print(f"\nI-section: tau max = {tau_I.max():.2f} MPa; V / (web area) = {V / ((h - 2*tf) * tw):.2f} MPa")
print(f"share of the shear force carried by the web: {np.sum((tau_I * w_I)[web]) * dy / V:.1%}")
print(f"rectangle: tau max = {tau_R.max():.2f} MPa = {tau_R.max() / (V / A):.3f} x V/A")
```

At the junction of flange and web ($y = 73$ mm) the computed stress jumps from 0.42 to 6.73 MPa, because the width $b$ drops from 80 mm to 5 mm. Across the web it rises gently to 8.82 MPa at the neutral axis, close to the simple estimate $V/(\text{web area}) = 8.45$ MPa, and the web carries 96% of the shear force. The rectangle of the same area peaks at exactly $1.5\,V/A = 5.00$ MPa. Either way, the shear stress here is a tenth of the bending stress, which is typical of long beams. Shear governs in short, heavily loaded beams, near supports, in beams with very thin webs, and in timber, which is weak in shear along the grain.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Do not trust the formula's value in the flanges. It assumes that $\tau$ is uniform across the width $b$, which is reasonable in a narrow web but not across a wide, thin flange whose top surface is free of stress. What actually flows in the flanges is *horizontal* shear, running outwards from the web, and that flow is why an asymmetric channel section twists unless it is loaded through a point outside it, its shear centre.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="In professional practice">

**Section tables.** Nobody computes $I$ for a standard rolled section by hand. Steel producers and national steel associations publish tables of dimensions and section properties for their standard shapes: IPE and HE sections in Europe, W shapes in the United States (in the AISC *Steel Construction Manual*), ISMB sections in India, H-sections in Japan and China. The designer picks the lightest section whose section modulus and shear area are large enough. The worked example above did that by hand with invented, round-number dimensions.

**Design codes.** The formulas of this lesson are the core of every structural design code, wrapped in rules that the formulas leave out: partial safety factors on loads and strengths, local buckling of thin flanges and webs, lateral-torsional buckling of beams whose compression flange is not held sideways, combined bending and axial load, and connections. For steel these include the Eurocodes' EN 1993 in Europe, the [AISC](https://www.aisc.org/) specification in the United States, GB 50017 in China and IS 800 in India; timber and concrete have their own. Standards are linked, not reproduced, because the current edition is always the authority.

**Shafts.** A real shaft carries bending (from gear forces and belt pulls) and torsion together, has keyways, shoulders and grooves that concentrate stress, and turns, so every point on its surface sees its bending stress reverse once per revolution. Shaft design is therefore a fatigue problem, which <LessonLink slug="failure-and-fatigue">Failure and fatigue</LessonLink> takes up, and the subject of <LessonLink slug="machine-elements">Machine elements</LessonLink>.

**Measuring.** Strain gauges bonded at ±45° to a shaft's axis measure the principal strains of pure shear, which is how many torque sensors on test benches and in electric vehicles' drivetrains work. Gauges on the top and bottom flanges of a bridge girder measure its bending moment directly.

**Why the materials behave so.** Young's modulus and the shear modulus, which set every deflection and twist here, come from the stiffness of atomic bonds; yield strength comes from how dislocations move. Lumenary Materials explains both in [elasticity, plasticity and strengthening](/en/materials/elasticity-plasticity-and-strengthening/).

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="From Galileo's cantilever to Zhuravskii's bridges">

Galileo Galilei asked, in his *Two New Sciences* of 1638, how strong a cantilever beam is. He assumed the whole cross-section was in tension, pivoting about its bottom edge, and so overestimated the strength of a rectangular beam by a factor of three, though he correctly found that strength grows with the square of the depth. Edme Mariotte in France, in the 1680s, and Antoine Parent, in 1713, realised that the upper fibres are compressed while the lower ones are stretched. Charles-Augustin Coulomb gave a correct analysis of bending in 1773 and, in 1784, studied the torsion of thin wires for his torsion balance. Claude-Louis Navier's lectures of 1826 gave the flexure formula in its modern form, with the neutral axis through the centroid. In 1855 the Russian railway engineer Dmitrii Zhuravskii, designing timber truss bridges for the Saint Petersburg–Moscow railway, derived the shear stress formula to explain why the beams split along their length; in the same year Adhémar Barré de Saint-Venant published his theory of the torsion of bars of any cross-section, showing why non-circular sections warp.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The torsion formula works for any shaft."** $\tau = Tr/J$ holds only for circular sections, solid or hollow. Square and rectangular bars warp when twisted, and the largest stress is at the middle of the long side, not at the corners. Open thin-walled sections are dramatically weaker: slit a tube along its length and its torsional stiffness can fall by a factor of hundreds.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The largest shear stress in a beam is at its top or bottom face."** Those faces are free surfaces, so the shear stress there is zero. In a rectangular beam it is largest at the neutral axis, where the bending stress is zero: the two stresses peak in different places.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The neutral axis is always at mid-height."** It passes through the centroid, which is at mid-height only for sections symmetric about a horizontal axis. In a T-section it lies close to the flange, so the stem's far edge carries the larger stress.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"More material always makes a beam stronger."** Where the material is matters more than how much there is. The same 1850 mm² of steel gave stresses from 97 MPa to over 1500 MPa above, depending only on its shape and orientation.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"σ = My/I tells you the moment at which the beam breaks."** It is valid only while the material is elastic. Once the outer fibres yield, a ductile beam can carry more moment, up to the *plastic moment*, which for a rectangle is 1.5 times the moment at first yield; brittle beams may fail at a different moment again. Codes use these differences deliberately.

</Misconception>

</Section>
