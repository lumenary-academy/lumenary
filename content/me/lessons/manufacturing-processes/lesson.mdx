<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="One part, many ways to make it">

Take a small aluminium housing, the size of your fist, with a bore for a bearing and four mounting holes. A prototype workshop would machine it from a solid bar in an afternoon. A small firm making a few hundred might sand-cast it and machine the bore. A car-parts supplier making half a million a year would die-cast it, in a steel die costing as much as a house, and might replace it with a glass-filled plastic moulding if the loads allow. Each answer is right for its quantity, and each changes the shape: a die-cast part has thin, even walls and draft angles; a machined part has square pockets the size of the cutter.

<LessonLink slug="how-things-are-made">How things are made</LessonLink> sorted processes into four families. This lesson takes the main ones apart: the numbers that govern machining, the heat flow that limits casting and moulding, the mechanics of forming and joining, a cost model for choosing between them, and the statistics that tell you whether a process can hold the tolerances the <LessonLink slug="engineering-design-and-cad">drawing</LessonLink> asks for. Additive manufacturing has the <LessonLink slug="additive-manufacturing">next lesson</LessonLink> to itself.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Process selection is a three-way negotiation between **shape**, **material** and **quantity**, with cost as the referee. Change any one and the best process can change, which is why manufacturing engineers belong in design reviews from the first sketch.

</Narrator>

<Section id="machining" kind="definitions" title="Machining: speeds, feeds and tool life">

**Machining** removes material as chips with a hard cutting edge. On a **lathe** the work turns and a single-point tool moves along it, making round parts; on a **milling machine** a rotating multi-edged cutter moves across the work, making flat faces, slots and pockets; **drilling**, **boring**, **reaming** and **tapping** make and finish holes; **grinding** uses abrasive grains for fine finishes and hard materials. Almost all production machining is now CNC, and a **machining centre** changes its own tools from a magazine.

<Definition term="cutting speed, feed and material removal rate">

The **cutting speed** $v_c$ is the speed of the work surface past the edge, $v_c = \pi D n$ (with $D$ the work diameter in turning, the cutter diameter in milling). In turning, the **feed** $f$ is the tool's advance per revolution and the **depth of cut** $a_p$ the radial depth; the **material removal rate** is $Q = v_c\,f\,a_p$. In milling, the **feed per tooth** $f_z$ gives the table feed $v_f = f_z\,z\,n$ for a cutter with $z$ teeth, and $Q = a_e\,a_p\,v_f$. The cutting power is roughly $P = u\,Q$, where the **specific cutting energy** $u$ is a property of the work material: of order 0.5–1 J/mm³ for aluminium alloys and 2–5 J/mm³ for carbon and alloy steels.

</Definition>

Cutting speed is chosen for the **tool**, not the machine. Tool makers publish recommended speeds and feeds for each tool grade and work material. Faster cutting removes metal sooner but wears the edge out much faster, by abrasion, diffusion and chemical attack at temperatures that reach several hundred degrees at the edge. F. W. Taylor, in years of experiments at Midvale and Bethlehem Steel in Pennsylvania published in 1907, found the relation

$$v_c\,T^{k} = C,$$

where $T$ is the tool life in minutes and the exponent $k$ is about 0.1–0.15 for high-speed steel and 0.2–0.3 for carbide. With $k = 0.25$, doubling the speed cuts tool life sixteen-fold. Between "slow and cheap on tools" and "fast and cheap on machine time" lies a speed of minimum cost per part.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Turning one pass along a steel shaft with a carbide insert.
D, L = 60.0, 200.0             # workpiece diameter and length of cut, mm
vc = 180.0                     # cutting speed, m/min
f, ap = 0.25, 2.0              # feed (mm/rev) and depth of cut (mm)
u = 2.5                        # specific cutting energy of a medium-carbon steel, J/mm^3 (W s/mm^3)

n = 1000 * vc / (np.pi * D)    # spindle speed, rev/min
vf = f * n                     # feed rate, mm/min
t = L / vf                     # time for the pass, min
MRR = vc * 1000 * f * ap       # material removal rate, mm^3/min
print(f"turning: spindle {n:.0f} rev/min, feed rate {vf:.0f} mm/min, pass time {t * 60:.0f} s")
print(f"  material removal rate {MRR / 1000:.0f} cm^3/min, cutting power {u * MRR / 60 / 1000:.2f} kW")

# Face milling: a 63 mm cutter with 5 inserts takes a 40 mm wide, 1.5 mm deep cut.
Dc, z, fz, vc_m = 63.0, 5, 0.12, 200.0
ae, ap_m = 40.0, 1.5
n_m = 1000 * vc_m / (np.pi * Dc)
vf_m = fz * z * n_m
print(f"milling: spindle {n_m:.0f} rev/min, table feed {vf_m:.0f} mm/min, "
      f"MRR {ae * ap_m * vf_m / 1000:.1f} cm^3/min, power {u * ae * ap_m * vf_m / 60 / 1000:.2f} kW")

# Tool life (Taylor): v T^k = C. Faster cutting wears the tool out much sooner.
k, C = 0.25, 450.0             # illustrative constants for carbide on this steel (v in m/min, T in min)
def tool_life(v):
    return (C / v) ** (1 / k)

# Cost per part for one finishing pass at speed v (costs in EUR, illustrative)
Cm = 1.5                       # machine and operator, EUR per minute
Ct = 8.0                       # cost of one cutting edge, EUR
t_ch, t_load = 2.0, 1.0        # minutes to change an edge, and to load and unload a part
def cost(v):
    tm = np.pi * D * L / (1000 * v * f)          # cutting time per part, min
    return Cm * (tm + t_load) + (Cm * t_ch + Ct) * tm / tool_life(v)

print("\n  v (m/min)  tool life (min)  parts per edge  cost per part (EUR)")
for v in [100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 400]:
    tm = np.pi * D * L / (1000 * v * f)
    print(f"  {v:8d}   {tool_life(v):12.1f}   {tool_life(v) / tm:12.1f}    {cost(v):10.2f}")
vs = np.linspace(60, 450, 3901)
best = vs[np.argmin(cost(vs))]
T_opt = (1 / k - 1) * (t_ch + Ct / Cm)
print(f"cheapest speed found by scanning: {best:.0f} m/min (tool life {tool_life(best):.0f} min)")
print(f"minimum-cost formula: T_opt = (1/k - 1)(t_ch + Ct/Cm) = {T_opt:.1f} min -> v = {C / T_opt**k:.0f} m/min")
```

At 180 m/min the 60 mm shaft turns at 955 rev/min and one 200 mm pass takes 50 s, removing 90 cm³ of steel a minute with 3.75 kW at the edge. The face mill removes 36.4 cm³/min with 1.52 kW. The cost table falls from EUR 3.80 per part at 100 m/min (where the edge would last 410 minutes) to a minimum of about EUR 2.95 near 200 m/min, and rises again as edges wear out in minutes. The scan and the classical minimum-cost formula agree: the cheapest speed is 208 m/min, where each edge lasts 22 minutes.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The minimum is shallow: anywhere from about 170 to 250 m/min costs within 3% of the best. That is good news on the shop floor, where the Taylor constants are never known precisely, but the formula's lesson holds: the more expensive the machine time compared with tool changes, the faster you should cut.

</Narrator>

<Section id="casting-forming" kind="human" title="Casting, forming and sheet metal">

### Casting

Casting pours molten metal into a mould cavity shaped like the part. **Sand casting** packs sand around a pattern and is cheap in tooling, suitable for anything from a valve body to a ship's propeller. **Investment casting** coats a wax pattern in ceramic, melts the wax out and pours into the shell, giving fine detail and good finish; it is how turbine blades and many medical implants are made. **High-pressure die casting** injects aluminium, magnesium or zinc into a steel die in a fraction of a second, producing thin-walled housings by the hundred thousand.

The physics is heat flow. Metal shrinks as it solidifies, so the last part to freeze must be fed with liquid from a reservoir, a **riser**, or it will contain a shrinkage cavity. Nicolae Chvorinov, studying castings in Czechoslovakia in 1940, found that solidification time grows with the square of the casting's **modulus**, its volume-to-surface ratio: $t_s = B\,(V/A)^2$, where $B$ depends on the metal and the mould. A riser works only if it freezes after the casting it feeds.

### Forming and forging

**Bulk forming** reshapes solid metal by plastic deformation. **Rolling** makes plate, sheet and rails; **extrusion** pushes billets through a die to make long profiles, like aluminium window frames; **forging** squeezes or hammers a billet, usually hot, between dies. Forging makes the strongest and toughest parts (crankshafts, connecting rods, aircraft landing-gear and turbine discs) because it closes internal porosity and aligns the metal's grain flow along the part's outline. **Cold working** at room temperature gives better finish and accuracy and strengthens the metal by work hardening, the process explained in [elasticity, plasticity and strengthening](/en/materials/elasticity-plasticity-and-strengthening/), at the price of higher forces.

### Sheet metal

Sheet-metal parts, from a car's door to a computer's case, are **blanked** (sheared to an outline), **pierced**, **bent** and **drawn** (stretched into a cup or panel) in presses. Bending must allow for **springback**: the elastic part of the strain recovers when the punch lifts, so dies overbend. The blank for a bent part is calculated from the length of its **neutral axis**, which in a bend lies a fraction $K$ (typically 0.3–0.5) of the thickness from the inside surface. Laser and punch-press cutting with CNC press brakes make sheet metal one of the cheapest ways to produce accurate enclosures in small batches as well as large ones.

</Section>

<Section id="joining-moulding" kind="human" title="Welding, joining and injection moulding">

### Welding and joining

**Fusion welding** melts the edges of two parts, usually with filler metal, so they solidify as one. **Arc welding** (manual metal arc, MIG/MAG with a wire fed through the torch, TIG with a tungsten electrode) is the workhorse of steel structures, ships and pipelines; **resistance spot welding** joins car-body panels, several thousand welds per car, in a fraction of a second each; **laser** and **electron-beam welding** make deep, narrow welds with little distortion. Every fusion weld has a **heat-affected zone** whose microstructure, and so strength and toughness, differ from the parent metal, and welds shrink as they cool, distorting the parts and leaving residual stresses. Welds are also where most fatigue cracks in steel structures begin, which is why the fatigue rules for welded joints in design codes are so conservative.

**Solid-state** methods avoid melting. **Friction stir welding**, invented at TWI in Cambridge in 1991, stirs the softened metals together with a spinning tool and joins aluminium alloys that are hard to fusion-weld; it is used for rocket fuel tanks, rail carriages and ship panels. **Brazing** and **soldering** use a filler that melts at a lower temperature than the parts, **adhesive bonding** joins dissimilar materials and spreads load over large areas, and **mechanical fasteners** (bolts, rivets, clinches) allow disassembly and repair.

### Injection moulding

Most plastic parts are injection moulded: plastic pellets are melted by a heated screw, injected into a closed steel mould under high pressure, held under pressure to pack in material as it shrinks, cooled until rigid, and ejected. A cycle takes from a few seconds to a minute, and one mould may make millions of parts. The cycle is dominated by **cooling**, which is again heat conduction: for a wall of thickness $s$ cooled from both faces, solving the heat equation of <LessonLink slug="heat-transfer">heat transfer</LessonLink> and keeping the first term gives

$$t_c \approx \frac{s^2}{\pi^2\alpha}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{4}{\pi}\,\frac{T_\text{melt} - T_\text{mould}}{T_\text{eject} - T_\text{mould}}\right),$$

with $\alpha$ the plastic's thermal diffusivity. The design rules follow from the physics: keep walls thin and **uniform** (thick sections cool slowly and sink), add **ribs** for stiffness instead of thickness, give every wall a **draft** angle so the part releases, and round the corners so the melt flows.

```python run
import numpy as np

# 1. Injection moulding: the part must cool until its centre is below the ejection temperature.
#    For a wall of thickness s cooled from both faces (first term of the slab conduction solution):
#    t_c = s^2 / (pi^2 alpha) * ln( (4/pi) (T_melt - T_mould) / (T_eject - T_mould) )
alpha = 0.09                         # thermal diffusivity of ABS, mm^2/s
T_melt, T_mould, T_eject = 240.0, 60.0, 95.0
print("wall (mm)   cooling time (s)")
for s in [1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0]:
    tc = s**2 / (np.pi**2 * alpha) * np.log(4 / np.pi * (T_melt - T_mould) / (T_eject - T_mould))
    print(f"{s:7.1f}     {tc:8.1f}")

# 2. Sand casting: Chvorinov's rule, solidification time t = B (V/A)^2.
B = 2.5                              # mould constant, s/mm^2 (illustrative: measured for each metal and mould)
def modulus_box(a, b, c):
    return a * b * c / (2 * (a * b + b * c + a * c))
M_plate = modulus_box(200, 100, 20)  # a 200 x 100 x 20 mm aluminium plate
side = 400_000 ** (1 / 3)              # a cube with the same volume, 400 cm^3
M_cube = modulus_box(side, side, side)
print(f"\nplate 200x100x20 mm: V/A = {M_plate:.2f} mm, freezes in about {B * M_plate**2:.0f} s")
print(f"cube of the same volume ({side:.1f} mm side): V/A = {M_cube:.2f} mm, about {B * M_cube**2:.0f} s")

# A cylindrical riser (height = diameter, V/A = D/6) must freeze after the casting it feeds:
# rule of thumb, riser modulus at least 1.2 x casting modulus.
D_riser = 6 * 1.2 * M_plate
print(f"riser: D = H >= {D_riser:.1f} mm, freezes in about {B * (D_riser / 6)**2:.0f} s")
```

A 2 mm wall of ABS needs about 8.5 s to cool; a 4 mm wall needs 33.8 s, four times as long, because the time grows with the square of the thickness. Making a part's walls 2 mm instead of 4 mm cuts the cooling time, the largest part of the cycle, by three quarters, and the machine cost per part with it. The aluminium plate's modulus is 7.69 mm and it freezes in about 148 s; a cube of the same volume, with less surface, takes 377 s. A riser for the plate must be at least 55.4 mm in diameter and height to stay liquid longer (213 s).

<Narrator who="gideon">

Both formulas are first estimates that assume uniform, one-dimensional heat flow and constant properties. Corners, bosses and ribs cool differently, real moulds have cooling channels at varying distances, and $B$ must be measured. Foundries and moulders therefore confirm gating, risers and cycle times with solidification and mould-flow simulation before cutting steel.

</Narrator>

<Section id="selection" kind="computation" title="Choosing a process: cost per part">

Start with what the part needs. **Shape** rules out many processes (a hollow, re-entrant shape cannot be pressed from sheet); **material** rules out others (titanium is not die-cast; high-strength steel is not injection moulded); **size**, **tolerance** and **surface finish** narrow the field further. Michael Ashby's process-selection charts, which plot processes against mass, section thickness, tolerance and economic batch size, make this screening quick. Among the survivors, cost decides. A useful model for the cost of each part is

$$c = \underbrace{\frac{C_\text{tool}\,\lceil n/n_\text{life}\rceil}{n}}_{\text{tooling, shared}} + c_\text{material} + c_\text{process} + c_\text{finishing},$$

where $n$ is the number of parts and $n_\text{life}$ how many parts one die or mould can make before it is worn out. Tooling dominates small batches; material and cycle time dominate large ones. The program applies it to the aluminium housing from the motivation, with illustrative figures of a realistic size, and to a glass-filled polyamide moulding of the same part, in case the plastic meets the requirements.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Cost per part of a small housing (about 0.25 kg finished), made five ways.
# All figures are illustrative (EUR), of a realistic size, not quotes from any supplier.
#   tooling: patterns, dies, moulds, fixtures and programming, paid once per tool
#   tool_life: parts one tool can make before it must be replaced
#   material, process, finishing: cost per part of material (with scrap), of the forming
#   or cutting step (machine-hour rate x cycle time), and of secondary machining
processes = {
    #                         tooling  tool_life   material  process  finishing
    "CNC from aluminium bar": (  400,  10**9,       3.00,    21.60,    0.00),
    "sand casting":           ( 1500,  5_000,       1.20,     4.00,    7.20),
    "investment casting":     ( 6000,  50_000,      1.50,     9.00,    2.40),
    "high-pressure die cast": (40000,  100_000,     0.80,     2.00,    2.70),
    "injection moulded PA-GF":(25000,  500_000,     0.40,     0.70,    0.00),
}

def cost_per_part(name, n):
    tooling, life, mat, proc, fin = processes[name]
    tools_needed = int(np.ceil(n / life))       # a worn-out die must be replaced
    return tooling * tools_needed / n + mat + proc + fin

quantities = [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]
print("parts made       " + "".join(f"{q:>11,}" for q in quantities))
for name in processes:
    print(f"{name:24s}" + "".join(f"{cost_per_part(name, q):11.2f}" for q in quantities))
print("cheapest         " + "".join(f"{min(processes, key=lambda p: cost_per_part(p, q)).split()[0]:>11s}" for q in quantities))

# Where do the metal processes cross over? Try every quantity up to 20 000, then a coarser grid.
metals = ["CNC from aluminium bar", "sand casting", "investment casting", "high-pressure die cast"]
grid = np.concatenate([np.arange(1, 20_001), np.unique(np.round(np.logspace(4.31, 6, 300)).astype(int))])
winner = [min(metals, key=lambda p: cost_per_part(p, q)) for q in grid]
print("\nif the part must be aluminium:")
print(f"  from {grid[0]:>7,} parts: {winner[0]}")
for i in range(1, len(grid)):
    if winner[i] != winner[i - 1]:
        print(f"  from {grid[i]:>7,} parts: {winner[i]}")
```

<Section id="quality" kind="computation" title="Metrology and quality control">

A tolerance on a drawing is a promise; **metrology**, the science of measurement, checks whether the factory keeps it. Workshops measure with calipers, micrometers, bore gauges, height gauges on surface plates, and gauge blocks as references; quality labs use **coordinate measuring machines** (CMMs) that probe hundreds of points and compare them with the CAD model, optical and laser scanners, and profilometers for surface roughness. Every measurement has an **uncertainty**, and a measuring system must be checked (a gauge repeatability and reproducibility study) before its results are trusted: a rule of thumb asks for an instrument at least ten times finer than the tolerance it checks. Measurements are traceable, through a chain of calibrations, to national metrology institutes and to the SI metre.

Measuring every part tells you which parts are bad. Measuring a sample regularly tells you whether the **process** is good, which is cheaper and catches problems before parts go wrong. Walter Shewhart at Bell Telephone Laboratories introduced **control charts** in the 1920s; W. Edwards Deming took statistical quality control to Japanese industry after 1950, where it became part of the Toyota Production System and of the quality movement that followed.

<Definition term="process capability indices">

For a stable process with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and specification limits LSL and USL,

$$C_p = \frac{\text{USL} - \text{LSL}}{6\sigma}, \qquad C_{pk} = \frac{\min(\text{USL} - \mu,\; \mu - \text{LSL})}{3\sigma}.$$

$C_p$ compares the tolerance with the process spread; $C_{pk}$ also penalises a process that is off centre. $C_{pk} \ge 1.33$ is a common requirement, and 1.67 or more for safety-critical features.

</Definition>

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

# A turned bearing seat: 25.000 +/- 0.020 mm. Measure 25 subgroups of 5 consecutive parts.
LSL, USL = 24.980, 25.020
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
x = rng.normal(25.006, 0.005, size=(25, 5))      # the process runs slightly large
x[20:] += 0.009                                   # from subgroup 21 on, the tool has worn

def phi(z):
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

def capability(mu, sigma):
    cp = (USL - LSL) / (6 * sigma)
    cpk = min(USL - mu, mu - LSL) / (3 * sigma)
    ppm = 1e6 * (phi((LSL - mu) / sigma) + 1 - phi((USL - mu) / sigma))
    return cp, cpk, ppm

stable = x[:20]                                   # the first 20 subgroups, before the wear
mu = stable.mean()
sigma = stable.std(ddof=1, axis=1).mean() / 0.9400   # mean subgroup s / c4 (n = 5): within-subgroup sigma
cp, cpk, ppm = capability(mu, sigma)
print(f"process mean {mu:.4f} mm, sigma {sigma * 1000:.2f} um")
print(f"Cp = {cp:.2f}, Cpk = {cpk:.2f}, expected out of tolerance {ppm:.0f} ppm")
cp0, cpk0, ppm0 = capability(25.000, sigma)
print(f"if centred on 25.000: Cp = Cpk = {cpk0:.2f}, {ppm0:.2f} ppm")

# X-bar control chart: limits from the stable period, then watch every subgroup.
means = x.mean(axis=1)
ucl, lcl = mu + 3 * sigma / math.sqrt(5), mu - 3 * sigma / math.sqrt(5)
print(f"\nX-bar chart: centre {mu:.4f}, limits {lcl:.4f} to {ucl:.4f} mm")
for i, m in enumerate(means, start=1):
    pos = int(round((m - 24.995) / 0.025 * 50))
    flag = "  <- out of control" if (m > ucl or m < lcl) else ""
    print(f"{i:3d} {m:.4f} " + " " * pos + "o" + flag)

print("\n Cpk    out of tolerance (centred process, both tails)")
for c in [0.67, 1.00, 1.33, 1.67, 2.00]:
    print(f"{c:5.2f}   {2e6 * (1 - phi(3 * c)):10.3f} ppm")
print(f"one tail at 4.5 sigma (Cpk = 1.5): {1e6 * (1 - phi(4.5)):.1f} ppm")
```

The process is precise ($\sigma = 4.49$ µm, $C_p = 1.49$) but runs 6 µm large, so $C_{pk} = 1.03$ and about 1000 parts per million would be out of tolerance. Re-centring it, a simple tool offset, raises $C_{pk}$ to 1.49 and cuts the rejects to about 8 per million. The control chart, with limits computed from the first twenty subgroups, flags every subgroup from 21 on, as soon as the tool wear shifts the mean, long before many parts are out of tolerance. The last table explains the "six sigma" target of the 1980s: a process whose tolerance is $\pm 6\sigma$ but which may drift by $1.5\sigma$ has $C_{pk} = 1.5$ and produces about 3.4 defects per million.

<Narrator who="gideon">

$C_{pk}$ means something only for a process that is **in statistical control** and roughly normal. Computing it from the whole run above, wear and all, would mix two processes and describe neither. Check the control chart first, and be wary of capability figures quoted from a few dozen parts: with 25 parts, an estimated $C_{pk}$ of 1.33 could easily be 1.1 or 1.6 in truth.

</Narrator>

<Section id="safety" kind="human" title="Safety in workshops and factories">

Manufacturing concentrates energy: spinning chucks and cutters, presses delivering hundreds of tonnes, molten metal at over 600 °C (aluminium) to over 1500 °C (steel), welding arcs bright with ultraviolet, robots that move faster than people can react. Workplaces control these hazards in a fixed order of preference: **eliminate** the hazard if possible, **engineer** it away (guards, interlocks that stop a machine when a door opens, light curtains and two-hand controls on presses, fume extraction, enclosed robot cells), use **procedures and training** (lock-out before maintenance, permits for hot work), and finally **personal protective equipment** (eye protection, hearing protection, aluminised clothing in foundries, welding helmets and gloves). Safety regulators such as the UK's HSE and the US OSHA publish guidance for each process, and machines sold in many countries must meet machinery-safety standards before they can be put on the market.

These processes belong in properly equipped workshops and factories, run by trained people. What a learner can safely do is design for them: model a part in CAD, generate and **simulate** its tool paths in a CAM program such as FreeCAD's CAM workbench (which shows the material being removed on screen), check a moulded design's wall thickness and draft, and then, in a school or makerspace workshop with supervision, make simple parts with hand tools or a 3D printer used as its maker intends.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How it is done in industry">

In a modern plant the processes in this lesson are linked by data. CAM programs come from the same model as the drawing; CMMs report measurements straight into statistical process control software; machine tools report spindle loads and vibration so that worn tools are changed before they break. ISO 9001 quality systems require that processes are controlled and capability is demonstrated before production starts, and car and aerospace makers add formal approval procedures for every new part and supplier. Lean manufacturing, which grew out of the Toyota Production System, attacks waste in waiting, transport and inventory as hard as statistical methods attack variation. The <LessonLink slug="advanced-manufacturing">frontier lesson on advanced manufacturing</LessonLink> picks up where this one ends: hybrid machines that add and subtract material, digital twins of whole production lines, and machine learning on process data.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Machining is always the most accurate process."** Machining is accurate, but a good die casting or moulding can hold tight tolerances on many features at once, part after part. The usual practice is to cast or mould near net shape and machine only the features that need it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Cutting faster is always more productive."** Past the minimum-cost speed, tool changes and broken edges cost more than the time saved; Taylor's law makes tool life collapse quickly with speed.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If every part is inspected, quality is assured."** Inspection finds defects after they are made, and inspectors miss some. Controlling the process, so that it rarely makes a bad part, is cheaper and more reliable.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A high Cp means few rejects."** Only if the process is centred. A process with $C_p = 1.5$ running near one limit can reject thousands per million; $C_{pk}$ is the index that counts.

</Misconception>
