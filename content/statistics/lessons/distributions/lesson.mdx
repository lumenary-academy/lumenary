<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="One number is not enough">

Akosua runs a bakery in Accra. On an average morning she sells 30 loaves of tea bread. How many should she bake?

Thirty is the obvious answer, and a poor one. Demand varies from morning to morning: some days 24 people want a loaf, some days 37. If she bakes exactly the average, she will turn customers away on close to half of all mornings, as we will calculate below. If she bakes 60, she will rarely run out but will throw bread away almost every day. A sensible answer needs more than the average. It needs to know *how often* demand reaches 35, 40 or 45 loaves: the whole pattern of variation. That pattern is a **distribution**.

The same need appears everywhere statistics is used. A maternity ward in Manila plans staffing from the number of births per night, which varies. A machine in Stuttgart turns steel shafts whose diameters vary by a few micrometres around the target, and the question is what fraction will fall outside tolerance. A seismologist in Santiago asks how long the wait until the next large earthquake might be. In each case the useful answer is not one number but a description of which values are possible and how likely each one is.

This lesson introduces the language for that description (random variables, mass and density functions, expectation, variance and quantiles) and the four distributions that a working statistician reaches for first. Each comes with a **story**, a description of the kind of process that produces it, because the story is how you decide whether a distribution fits your problem. And each comes with a check: we will simulate it and compare the simulation with the formulas, a habit you will use throughout Lumenary Statistics.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The question Akosua is really asking is not "what is the typical demand?" but "what demand is exceeded only rarely?". That is a question about the upper tail of a distribution, and no average, however carefully computed, can answer it.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Numbers that come from chance">

In <LessonLink slug="chance-and-probability">Chance and probability</LessonLink> you met probabilities of events: the chance of rolling a six, or of rain tomorrow. Statistics usually cares about *numbers* produced by chance: how many of 20 seeds sprout, how many calls arrive in a minute, how tall the next person measured will be, how long until the next bus. A **random variable** is such a number: a quantity whose value is decided by a chance process. We write it with a capital letter, $X$, and its observed value with a small one, $x$.

Random variables come in two kinds.

- **Discrete** ones take separate values you can list: 0, 1, 2, 3, … seeds; 0, 1, 2, … calls. Each value has a probability, and we can draw them as bars whose heights add up to 1.
- **Continuous** ones can take any value in an interval: a height of 171.3 cm, or 171.32, or 171.3247. The chance of any one exact value is zero, so instead we describe how probability is *spread* along the line with a curve, the **density**. Probability is then the **area** under the curve over an interval, and the total area is 1.

A good way to picture any distribution is as the histogram you would get if you could repeat the chance process forever. Roll the process ten times and the histogram is ragged; a thousand times and its shape emerges; a million times and it settles onto the bars or the curve. You met histograms in <LessonLink slug="describing-data">Describing data</LessonLink> as the shape of a data set; a distribution is the shape of a *process*. We cannot repeat Akosua's mornings a million times, but a computer can repeat a model of them, which is why simulation is the statistician's laboratory.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="probability mass function">

A discrete random variable $X$ has a **probability mass function** (pmf) $p(k) = P(X = k)$, with $p(k) \ge 0$ and $\sum_k p(k) = 1$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="probability density function">

A continuous random variable $X$ has a **probability density function** (pdf) $f(x) \ge 0$ with total area $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$, such that for any interval
$$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$
The value $f(x)$ is not a probability: it is probability *per unit* of $x$, and it may be larger than 1.

</Definition>

<Definition term="cumulative distribution function">

The **cumulative distribution function** (cdf) of any random variable is $F(x) = P(X \le x)$. It rises from 0 to 1. Its complement $1 - F(x) = P(X > x)$ is called the **survival function**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="expectation, variance and standard deviation">

The **expectation** (mean) of $X$ is $\mu = E[X] = \sum_k k\,p(k)$ for a discrete variable and $\int x f(x)\,dx$ for a continuous one: the long-run average of many independent repetitions. The **variance** is $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2]$, the average squared distance from the mean, and the **standard deviation** is $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$, in the same units as $X$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="quantile">

For $0 < q < 1$, the **$q$-quantile** of $X$ is the smallest value $x$ with $F(x) \ge q$. The 0.5-quantile is the **median**; the 0.95-quantile is the 95th **percentile**. Software calls the function $q \mapsto x$ the *quantile function*, *inverse cdf* or *ppf* (percent point function).

</Definition>

Expectation is the "expected value" of a game from <LessonLink slug="chance-and-probability">Chance and probability</LessonLink>, extended to any random variable. Three rules do most of the everyday work. For constants $a$ and $b$, $E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$ and $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$: shifting moves the centre but not the spread, and rescaling stretches both. For any random variables, $E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$; and if $X$ and $Y$ are **independent**, $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$. Lumenary Math proves these in <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-theory">Probability theory</TopicLink>; here we use them.

</Section>

<Section id="four-distributions" kind="examples" title="Four distributions and their stories">

### The binomial: counting successes in a fixed number of tries

**Story.** Run $n$ independent trials, each a success with the same probability $p$, and count the successes. Then $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$, with
$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}, \qquad E[X] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1 - p).$$

The mean and variance follow from writing $X$ as a sum of $n$ independent 0-or-1 variables, each with mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$, and using the rules above.

<Example title="A germination test">

Wanjiru, an agronomist in Nakuru, tests a batch of bean seed by planting 20 seeds; the supplier claims 80% germinate. If the claim is right and seeds sprout independently, the count is Binomial(20, 0.8): expect $20 \times 0.8 = 16$ sprouts, with standard deviation $\sqrt{20 \times 0.8 \times 0.2} \approx 1.79$. All 20 sprouting has probability $0.8^{20} \approx 0.012$, and 13 or fewer has probability about 0.087, so a result of 13 is unusual but not shocking. <LessonLink slug="hypothesis-testing">Hypothesis testing</LessonLink> turns that "unusual but not shocking" into a careful argument.

</Example>

The story carries conditions: a **fixed** number of trials, **independence**, and the **same** $p$ for every trial. Seeds from one diseased pod violate independence; mixing two batches with different germination rates violates "same $p$". Either way the counts will vary more than the binomial predicts.

### The Poisson: counting events in a window

**Story.** Events happen one at a time, independently, at a constant average rate, and we count how many fall in a fixed window of time or space: calls to a helpline per minute, typing errors per page, flaws per metre of cable. Then $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the average count per window:
$$P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda.$$

The Poisson is the binomial's limit when there are very many opportunities, each very unlikely: split a minute into $n$ tiny slices, each holding a call with probability $\lambda/n$, and let $n$ grow. Siméon-Denis Poisson derived it in 1837, and in 1898 Ladislaus Bortkiewicz showed how well it describes rare events by tabulating the number of soldiers killed by horse kicks in Prussian cavalry corps over twenty years: deaths were rare, independent and about equally likely in any corps-year.

The equality of mean and variance is the Poisson's fingerprint, and a practical check: if your counts have a variance far bigger than their mean, the Poisson story does not hold.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The Poisson model assumes a constant rate and independent events. Real counts often break both: emergency admissions rise on winter weekends, and one burst water main generates a cluster of calls. Then the variance exceeds the mean (statisticians say the data are **overdispersed**), and a Poisson model will understate how often extreme counts occur.

</Narrator>

### The normal: many small effects added together

**Story.** A quantity is the sum of many small, independent influences, none of which dominates: a measurement error made of dozens of tiny disturbances, or an adult height shaped by many genes and many meals. Then the quantity is approximately **normal** (also called Gaussian), with density
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad E[X] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^2.$$

Why sums of small effects become normal is the central limit theorem, the subject of <LessonLink slug="sampling-distributions-and-the-clt">the next lesson</LessonLink>. Every normal distribution is a shifted, stretched copy of the **standard normal** $Z \sim N(0, 1)$: if $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ then $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$, the **z-score**, is standard normal. So one table of the standard normal serves every normal distribution, and every normal puts about 68% of its probability within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three.

### The exponential: waiting for the next event

**Story.** Events arrive as in the Poisson story at rate $\lambda$ per unit time, and we measure the waiting time until the next one. Then $T \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)$, with
$$f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t} \ (t \ge 0), \qquad P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad E[T] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(T) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}.$$

The two stories are two views of one process. If calls arrive at 3 per minute, the count in each minute is Poisson(3) and the gaps between calls are Exponential(3), with mean $1/3$ of a minute.

<Theorem name="The exponential distribution is memoryless">

If $T \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)$, then for all $s, t \ge 0$,
$$P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > t).$$
Having already waited $s$ minutes tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By the definition of conditional probability, and because the event $T > s + t$ is contained in $T > s$,
$$P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = \frac{P(T > s + t)}{P(T > s)} = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(T > t).$$

</Proof>

Memorylessness is exactly right for radioactive decay, and badly wrong for a light bulb that wears out or for a bus that runs to a timetable. It is the precise sense in which the exponential describes events that "just happen" with no build-up.

| Distribution | Story | Values | Mean | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Binomial$(n, p)$ | successes in $n$ independent trials | $0, 1, \ldots, n$ | $np$ | $np(1-p)$ |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$ | events in a fixed window, constant rate | $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ |
| Normal$(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | sum of many small independent effects | all real numbers | $\mu$ | $\sigma^2$ |
| Exponential$(\lambda)$ | waiting time to the next event | $t \ge 0$ | $1/\lambda$ | $1/\lambda^2$ |

<Section id="simulate" kind="computation" title="Simulate, then check">

A formula you have derived is a claim, and a simulation is a cheap experiment to test it. NumPy's random `Generator` draws from all four distributions. We fix the **seed** so that the program prints the same numbers every time, which makes the output reproducible and lets you compare your run with this page.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
N = 200_000

# Four distributions: draw N values from each and compare with the formulas.
cases = [
    ("Binomial(n=20, p=0.3)", rng.binomial(20, 0.3, N), 20 * 0.3, 20 * 0.3 * 0.7),
    ("Poisson(lam=4)", rng.poisson(4.0, N), 4.0, 4.0),
    ("Normal(mu=170, sd=8)", rng.normal(170.0, 8.0, N), 170.0, 8.0**2),
    ("Exponential(rate=0.5)", rng.exponential(1 / 0.5, N), 1 / 0.5, 1 / 0.5**2),
]
print(f"{'distribution':22} {'mean: sim':>10} {'formula':>8} {'var: sim':>10} {'formula':>8}")
for name, x, mean, var in cases:
    print(f"{name:22} {x.mean():10.3f} {mean:8.3f} {x.var():10.3f} {var:8.3f}")

# The probability mass function of Binomial(10, 0.3), simulated and exact, as a text bar chart.
n, p = 10, 0.3
x = rng.binomial(n, p, N)
print("\nk  simulated  exact")
for k in range(n + 1):
    sim = np.mean(x == k)
    exact = math.comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p) ** (n - k)
    print(f"{k:2} {sim:9.4f} {exact:7.4f}  " + "#" * round(100 * exact))
```

Every simulated mean and variance sits within a whisker of its formula, and the simulated binomial frequencies match $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ to about two decimal places. The bar chart also shows the shape: Binomial(10, 0.3) piles up around 3 and has a longer tail to the right than to the left.

<Narrator who="margaret">

How close should the simulation be? The average of $N$ independent draws has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, so for 200,000 draws from Poisson(4), whose $\sigma$ is 2, the simulated mean should typically land within about $2/\sqrt{200\,000} \approx 0.0045$ of 4. It printed 4.002, comfortably inside. A gap ten times larger would mean a bug, in the formula or in the code.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="quantiles" kind="examples" title="Quantiles answer planning questions">

Back to the bakery. Suppose Akosua's morning demand is Poisson with mean 30: many independent customers, each unlikely to come on any given morning, at a steady average rate. (That is a modelling assumption; checking it against her sales records would be the first job of a real analysis.) To turn customers away on at most one morning in twenty, she needs the smallest number of loaves $k$ with $P(\text{demand} \le k) \ge 0.95$: the 95th percentile of demand.

SciPy's distribution objects answer such questions directly. Their method names are worth learning, because the same names appear across the scientific Python ecosystem: `pmf` or `pdf` for the height, `cdf` for $P(X \le x)$, `sf` (survival function) for $P(X > x)$, `ppf` for the quantile, and `rvs` for random draws.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# A bakery sells, on average, 30 loaves of tea bread each morning. If demand is Poisson(30),
# how many loaves must it bake to sell out on at most 1 morning in 20?
demand = stats.poisson(30)
k95 = demand.ppf(0.95)                      # ppf = "percent point function" = quantile
print("95th percentile of Poisson(30):", int(k95))
print("P(demand <= 38) =", round(demand.cdf(38), 4), "  P(demand <= 39) =", round(demand.cdf(39), 4))

# The same answer by simulating 100,000 mornings.
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
mornings = rng.poisson(30, 100_000)
print("simulated 95th percentile:", np.quantile(mornings, 0.95))
print("share of mornings that would sell out with 39 loaves:", np.mean(mornings > 39))

# Familiar quantiles of the standard normal, and the median of an exponential wait.
z = stats.norm()
for q in [0.5, 0.84, 0.95, 0.975, 0.995]:
    print(f"standard normal: {q:.3f} quantile = {z.ppf(q):6.3f}")
wait = stats.expon(scale=1 / 0.5)           # SciPy uses scale = 1/rate
print("exponential (rate 0.5): mean =", wait.mean(), " median =", round(wait.median(), 3))
```

Thirty-nine loaves. With 38 she would fall short on $1 - 0.9352 \approx 6.5\%$ of mornings; with 39, on about 4.6%. The 100,000 simulated mornings agree, and baking only the average of 30 would leave customers disappointed on about 45% of mornings. The extra nine loaves are the price of reliability, and they come straight from the spread of the distribution: a Poisson with mean 30 has standard deviation $\sqrt{30} \approx 5.5$, and the 95th percentile of a roughly normal quantity sits about 1.645 standard deviations above the mean, $30 + 1.645 \times 5.48 \approx 39.0$.

The table of standard normal quantiles is worth memorising: 1.645 (95th percentile), 1.96 (97.5th) and 2.576 (99.5th) will reappear in every lesson that follows. Notice too that the exponential's median, $\ln 2 / \lambda \approx 1.386$ minutes, is well below its mean of 2: in a right-skewed distribution a few long waits pull the mean up, while the median stays with the typical case.

</Section>

<Section id="normal-approximation" kind="human" title="The normal approximation, and when it fails">

In 1733 Abraham de Moivre, a French Huguenot mathematician who made a modest living in London tutoring mathematics and advising gamblers and insurers, noticed that the binomial bars for large $n$ trace out a bell-shaped curve. In modern terms: when $n$ is large,
$$\text{Binomial}(n, p) \approx N\big(np,\; np(1-p)\big).$$
To approximate $P(X \ge k)$ for a count, we use $P(Y \ge k - 0.5)$ for the normal $Y$: the **continuity correction**, which accounts for each bar covering the interval from $k - 0.5$ to $k + 0.5$.

Before computers, this approximation was how anyone computed binomial probabilities for large $n$. Today SciPy gives exact binomial tails in microseconds, so the approximation matters for a different reason: it explains *why* so many statistics are roughly normal, and it is the basis of quick mental calculations and of many standard tests. You need to know when to trust it.

```python run
import math
from scipy import stats

def approximations(n, p, k):
    """P(X >= k) for X ~ Binomial(n, p): exact, normal approximation, Poisson approximation."""
    exact = stats.binom.sf(k - 1, n, p)                # sf(k - 1) = P(X > k - 1) = P(X >= k)
    mu, sd = n * p, math.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
    normal = stats.norm.sf((k - 0.5 - mu) / sd)       # with the continuity correction
    poisson = stats.poisson.sf(k - 1, mu)
    return exact, normal, poisson

print("   n      p    k   P(X>=k) exact  normal approx  Poisson approx")
for n, p, k in [(100, 0.5, 55), (100, 0.5, 65), (100, 0.5, 70), (100, 0.5, 75),
                (40, 0.05, 4), (40, 0.05, 6), (40, 0.05, 8), (2000, 0.001, 6)]:
    e, nm, po = approximations(n, p, k)
    print(f"{n:4} {p:6.3f} {k:4}  {e:13.3g}  {nm:13.3g}  {po:14.3g}")

# Why the normal struggles with Binomial(40, 0.05): it is lopsided and piled up against zero.
n, p = 40, 0.05
mu, sd = n * p, math.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
print(f"\nBinomial({n}, {p}): mean {mu}, sd {sd:.3f}")
for k in range(9):
    pk = stats.binom.pmf(k, n, p)
    print(f"{k} {pk:.4f} " + "#" * round(100 * pk))
print("a normal curve with this mean and sd puts", round(stats.norm.cdf(-0.5, mu, sd), 3),
      "of its probability below -0.5, on impossible negative counts")
```

Two things stand out.

1. **Symmetric and large: excellent near the centre, worse in the far tails.** For Binomial(100, 0.5), the normal approximation is essentially exact for $P(X \ge 55)$, but at $P(X \ge 75)$ it is too large by about 70% in relative terms ($4.79 \times 10^{-7}$ against $2.82 \times 10^{-7}$). The absolute error is tiny, but if your decision depends on how rare something is (a one-in-a-million event), relative error is what matters.
2. **Skewed: poor even at moderate distances.** Binomial(40, 0.05) has mean 2 and is piled up against zero. The normal curve with the same mean and standard deviation puts 3.5% of its probability on impossible negative counts, and it underestimates $P(X \ge 8)$ by a factor of about 20. The Poisson approximation, Poisson$(np)$, does far better for rare events, and for Binomial(2000, 0.001) it is almost exact.

The usual rule of thumb asks for $np \ge 10$ and $n(1 - p) \ge 10$ before using the normal approximation (some books say 5). Treat it as a minimum for approximations near the centre, not as a licence for tail probabilities.

<Narrator who="gideon">

A rule of thumb about $np$ assumes the binomial story holds in the first place. If the trials are not independent (pupils in the same classroom, patients in the same hospital), the true spread is larger than $np(1-p)$, and no approximation to the binomial, however good, describes the data.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="Distributions in professional practice">

**Choose by story, then check against data.** A statistician picks a candidate distribution because its story matches how the data were generated, then checks the fit: a histogram against the fitted curve, a comparison of mean and variance for counts, and a **quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot**, which plots the sorted data against the quantiles the model predicts, so that a good fit lies on a straight line and heavy tails bend away at the ends. George Box's often-quoted remark that all models are wrong but some are useful is the working attitude: no real process is exactly binomial or normal, and the question is whether the model is good enough for the decision at hand.

**Beyond the four.** Count data with extra variation are often modelled with the **negative binomial**. Quantities that are products of many factors, such as incomes or the sizes of particles after crushing, are often closer to **lognormal**. Some phenomena, such as the sizes of cities, insurance losses or the popularity of web pages, have **heavy tails** in which extreme values are far more common than any normal model allows; ignoring that has repeatedly led risk models to understate the chance of large losses. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/) describes dozens of distributions with the situations they model.

**Software conventions.** In Python, `scipy.stats` provides each distribution as an object with the methods used above. In R, the same four operations are four functions sharing a prefix: `d` for density or mass, `p` for the cdf, `q` for quantiles, `r` for random draws. This R code is shown for comparison and is not run here:

```r
# R, not run here: the bakery and seed examples
qpois(0.95, lambda = 30)            # 39
pbinom(13, size = 20, prob = 0.8)   # P(13 or fewer sprout), about 0.087
rexp(5, rate = 0.5)                 # five random waiting times
```

**Reproducible randomness.** Simulations in research and industry use seeded generators so that results can be reproduced and audited. NumPy's default generator is PCG64; record the seed with the results, and never choose a seed by trying several until the answer looks good, which quietly turns a simulation into a forking path.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Most data are normally distributed."** Many are not: counts near zero, waiting times, incomes, reaction times and file sizes are all right-skewed. The normal is common for *sums and averages* of many independent pieces (the next lesson explains why), not for raw data in general. Look at the data before assuming a shape.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The density at a point is the probability of that value."** For a continuous variable every single value has probability zero; the density is probability per unit length. A normal distribution with standard deviation 0.1 has density about 3.99 at its centre, which is fine, because only areas are probabilities.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The mean is the most likely value."** For Poisson(0.5) the mean is 0.5, a value that can never occur; the most likely count is 0 (probability 0.61). For the exponential, the density is highest at 0 although the mean is $1/\lambda$. The mean is a balance point, not a forecast of the typical case.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Random events are spread out evenly."** Look at the arrivals in the figure above: five calls in the first minute (three of them within a few seconds) and one in the last. Clusters and gaps are what independence looks like. People who see a cluster of rare events, such as several cases of an illness in one village, often conclude that something must have caused it; sometimes something did, but chance alone produces more clusters than intuition expects.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I've waited so long that the next one must be due."** For a memoryless process, the expected remaining wait is the same however long you have already waited. The intuition is right only when the process has memory: buses that keep to a timetable, or a machine part that wears out.

</Misconception>

</Section>
