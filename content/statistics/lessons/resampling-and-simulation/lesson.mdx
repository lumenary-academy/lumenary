<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="When the formula runs out">

A water charity in Malawi records how many days it takes to repair each broken village hand pump. For the last 24 repairs the mean is 5.8 days, and the board asks how precise that figure is. For a mean there is a formula, $s/\sqrt{n}$, and a $t$ interval to go with it — though both lean on a normal approximation that is shaky for 24 strongly skewed values. But the board's next questions have no textbook formula at all. How precise is the *median* repair time? The 90th percentile, which says how long the unluckiest villages wait? The ratio of this year's mean to last year's?

Each of these questions asks the same thing: **how much would my estimate change if I could repeat the data collection?** Classical statistics answers it by mathematics, deriving the sampling distribution of each statistic, often only approximately and only for some statistics. In 1979 Bradley Efron proposed answering it by computation instead: if we cannot draw new samples from the population, draw them from the best picture of the population we have — the sample itself. That idea, the **bootstrap**, is one of a family of **resampling** methods that replace derivations with repeated computation on the data:

- the **bootstrap** estimates the uncertainty of almost any estimate;
- **permutation tests** test hypotheses by reshuffling labels;
- **cross-validation** estimates how well a prediction method will do on new data;
- **Monte Carlo studies** test the methods themselves, on simulated data where the truth is known.

You have already used two of them: the percentile bootstrap in <LessonLink slug="estimation-and-confidence-intervals">Estimation and confidence intervals</LessonLink> and a permutation test in <LessonLink slug="comparing-groups">Comparing groups</LessonLink>. This lesson takes each method in depth — why it works, its better variants, and how to test it — and then spends as much care on when it fails.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Behind every interval and test is a question about an imaginary repetition: what if we had collected the data again? Formulas imagine the repetition analytically; resampling imagines it on the computer. Neither can do better than the assumption about *how* the repetition would go, so that is the thing to think hardest about.

</Narrator>

</Section>

In the bootstrap world the "population" is the **empirical distribution** $\hat F$, which puts probability $1/n$ on each observed value, and its "true parameter" is $\hat\theta$, which we know. Drawing a sample of size $n$ from $\hat F$ means drawing $n$ values from the data **with replacement**: some observations appear twice or more, others not at all. On average a resample contains $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \approx 1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$ of the distinct original observations. Computing the statistic on many resamples shows how $\hat\theta^*$ varies around $\hat\theta$, and we use that as a stand-in for how $\hat\theta$ varies around $\theta$. This substitution of $\hat F$ for $F$ is the **plug-in principle** you met with the percentile interval; for smooth statistics and reasonably large samples, theory confirms that it works.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Bootstrap estimates and intervals">

<Definition term="nonparametric bootstrap">

Given data $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ and an estimate $\hat\theta = s(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, draw $B$ **resamples** $x^{*b}$, each of $n$ values chosen with replacement from the data, and compute the **bootstrap replicates** $\hat\theta^{*b} = s(x^{*b})$, $b = 1, \ldots, B$. The **bootstrap standard error** is the standard deviation of the replicates, and the **bootstrap estimate of bias** is $\overline{\hat\theta^{*}} - \hat\theta$.

</Definition>

Three kinds of 95% interval are built from the replicates, with $q_{p}$ denoting the $p$-quantile of the $\hat\theta^{*b}$:

- **Percentile interval:** $[q_{0.025},\, q_{0.975}]$. Simple, and it respects the range of the parameter (a bootstrap interval for a proportion never leaves $[0, 1]$).
- **Basic interval:** $[2\hat\theta - q_{0.975},\, 2\hat\theta - q_{0.025}]$. It assumes that $\hat\theta - \theta$ is distributed like $\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta$ and inverts that relationship; for skewed statistics it is reflected the opposite way from the percentile interval.
- **BCa interval** (bias-corrected and accelerated): the percentile interval with its quantile levels adjusted. A **bias correction** $z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\big(\text{fraction of } \hat\theta^{*b} < \hat\theta\big)$ measures how far the replicates sit off-centre, and an **acceleration** $a$, estimated from the jackknife (the $n$ estimates obtained by leaving out one observation at a time), measures how fast the standard error changes with the parameter, which is what makes sampling distributions skewed. The interval is $[q_{\alpha_1}, q_{\alpha_2}]$ with
$$\alpha_{1,2} = \Phi\!\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{0.025,\,0.975}}{1 - a\,(z_0 + z_{0.025,\,0.975})}\right),$$
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cdf and $z_p$ its quantiles. With $z_0 = a = 0$ it reduces to the percentile interval.

BCa endpoints are **second-order accurate**: under regularity conditions the coverage error of a one-sided BCa bound shrinks like $1/n$, against $1/\sqrt{n}$ for percentile and basic bounds. That is a statement about large samples; the simulation study below tests what it means for 24 observations.

</Section>

<Section id="bootstrap-code" kind="computation" title="The bootstrap at work">

The program resamples the 24 repair times 10,000 times, computes the bootstrap standard error and bias, the three bootstrap intervals and the $t$ interval, and implements BCa by hand before checking it against `scipy.stats.bootstrap`.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Synthetic data: days taken to repair 24 broken village hand pumps.
days = np.array([3.3, 5.5, 7.3, 2.1, 8.3, 5.5, 8.4, 5.6, 11.4, 2.1, 18.6, 11.7,
                 2.8, 7.6, 6.4, 1.1, 7.2, 2.8, 3.7, 2.8, 3.2, 4.7, 4.8, 1.3])
n, B = len(days), 10_000
rng = np.random.default_rng(1979)

theta_hat = days.mean()
idx = rng.integers(0, n, size=(B, n))           # B resamples of size n, with replacement
boot = days[idx].mean(axis=1)                   # the statistic on every resample

se = boot.std(ddof=1)
print(f"sample mean {theta_hat:.3f} days; bootstrap SE {se:.3f}; formula s/sqrt(n) {days.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n):.3f}")
print(f"bootstrap estimate of bias: {boot.mean() - theta_hat:+.3f}")

lo, hi = np.quantile(boot, [0.025, 0.975])
print(f"percentile interval   [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}]")
print(f"basic interval        [{2 * theta_hat - hi:.2f}, {2 * theta_hat - lo:.2f}]")
t = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1) * days.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"t interval            [{theta_hat - t:.2f}, {theta_hat + t:.2f}]")

# BCa: correct the percentile interval for bias (z0) and for skewness (acceleration a).
z0 = stats.norm.ppf(np.mean(boot < theta_hat))
jack = np.array([np.delete(days, i).mean() for i in range(n)])   # leave-one-out estimates
d = jack.mean() - jack
a = (d**3).sum() / (6 * ((d**2).sum()) ** 1.5)
z = stats.norm.ppf([0.025, 0.975])
adj = stats.norm.cdf(z0 + (z0 + z) / (1 - a * (z0 + z)))
lo_bca, hi_bca = np.quantile(boot, adj)
print(f"BCa interval          [{lo_bca:.2f}, {hi_bca:.2f}]   (z0 = {z0:.3f}, a = {a:.3f}, quantiles used {adj.round(4)})")

res = stats.bootstrap((days,), np.mean, n_resamples=B, method="BCa", random_state=np.random.default_rng(1))
print(f"scipy.stats.bootstrap BCa [{res.confidence_interval.low:.2f}, {res.confidence_interval.high:.2f}]")
```

The bootstrap standard error, 0.799 days, is slightly smaller than the formula's 0.816. That is not an error: the bootstrap world's population has variance $\frac{1}{n}\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$, with divisor $n$ rather than $n - 1$, so it reproduces $s\sqrt{(n-1)/n}/\sqrt{n}$. The bias estimate, −0.009 days, is pure noise, as it should be for a sample mean, which is unbiased.

The intervals are more interesting. The repair times are skewed to the right — one pump took 18.6 days — so the sampling distribution of their mean is skewed to the right too, and an honest interval should reach further above 5.76 than below it. The $t$ interval is symmetric by construction, and the basic interval leans the *wrong* way. The BCa interval, $[4.48, 7.73]$, leans the right way: its positive $z_0$ and acceleration move both quantile levels up, to 4.8% and 99.0%. SciPy's BCa interval agrees to within a few hundredths, the size of the Monte Carlo variation between two sets of 10,000 resamples.

<Narrator who="margaret">

How many resamples are enough? A standard error settles down after a few hundred, but interval endpoints live in the tails, where few replicates fall. With $B = 10\,000$, the 2.5% quantile is pinned down by about 250 replicates below it, and rerunning with different seeds moves the BCa endpoints by a few hundredths of a day. With $B = 1\,000$ they would wander about three times as far. Computation is cheap, so use thousands, and report the seed.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="permutation" kind="examples" title="Permutation tests">

<LessonLink slug="comparing-groups">Comparing groups</LessonLink> built a permutation test by shuffling labels thousands of times. Here we ask why that works, and how to do it exactly. An agronomy station in Ethiopia randomly assigns 8 of 16 maize plots to a cover-crop treatment, and measures yields in tonnes per hectare (the data are synthetic). The cover-crop plots average 0.50 t/ha more. Could that be chance?

If the cover crop does nothing at all, each plot's yield would have been the same whichever group it was assigned to; the labels "cover crop" and "control" are then arbitrary, and every way of choosing 8 of the 16 plots was equally likely to be the one we got. So we can compute the difference in means for **every possible assignment** and ask how unusual the real one is. This is the logic R. A. Fisher gave in *The Design of Experiments* (1935): the randomisation that was actually performed is the basis of the test.

<Theorem name="Permutation tests are exact under exchangeability">

Suppose that under the null hypothesis the joint distribution of the data is unchanged by permuting the group labels (the data are **exchangeable**). Let $T_0$ be the observed test statistic and $T_1, \ldots, T_M$ its values under all $M$ relabellings other than the observed one, and define the p-value as the fraction of all $M + 1$ values that are at least as extreme as $T_0$. Then for every $\alpha$, $P(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha$ under the null hypothesis.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Under exchangeability, the observed labelling is, in distribution, just one of the $M + 1$ equally likely labellings, so the rank of $T_0$ among $T_0, \ldots, T_M$ is uniformly distributed (with ties broken against rejection). The p-value is at most $\alpha$ only when $T_0$ ranks among the $\lfloor \alpha(M + 1) \rfloor$ most extreme values, which has probability at most $\lfloor \alpha(M+1) \rfloor / (M+1) \le \alpha$.

</Proof>

When there are too many relabellings to enumerate, we draw $B$ of them at random. The same argument holds if we count the observed labelling among them and use $p = (1 + \#\{\text{random } T_b \text{ at least as extreme}\})/(B + 1)$. Belinda Phipson and Gordon Smyth showed in 2010 that omitting the "+1", which many programs once did, understates p-values and can report the impossible value zero.

```python run
import itertools
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Synthetic field trial: maize yield (tonnes per hectare) on 16 plots, 8 randomly given a cover crop.
control = np.array([3.1, 2.8, 3.5, 2.6, 3.0, 3.3, 2.9, 2.4])
cover = np.array([3.4, 3.9, 3.2, 3.6, 2.9, 4.1, 3.5, 3.0])
pooled = np.concatenate([cover, control])
observed = cover.mean() - control.mean()
print(f"observed difference in means: {observed:.3f} t/ha")

# Exact: every way of choosing which 8 of the 16 plots were "cover crop" (16 choose 8 = 12,870).
total = pooled.sum()
diffs = np.array([pooled[list(c)].sum() / 8 - (total - pooled[list(c)].sum()) / 8
                  for c in itertools.combinations(range(16), 8)])
p_exact = np.mean(np.abs(diffs) >= abs(observed) - 1e-12)      # the 1e-12 allows for rounding error
print(f"exact two-sided permutation p-value: {p_exact:.4f}  ({np.sum(np.abs(diffs) >= abs(observed) - 1e-12)} of {len(diffs)} splits)")

# Monte Carlo: 9,999 random relabellings, counting the observed labelling as one of them.
rng = np.random.default_rng(1935)
B = 9_999
perm = np.array([rng.permutation(pooled) for _ in range(B)])
mc = perm[:, :8].mean(axis=1) - perm[:, 8:].mean(axis=1)
p_mc = (1 + np.sum(np.abs(mc) >= abs(observed) - 1e-12)) / (B + 1)
print(f"Monte Carlo permutation p-value:     {p_mc:.4f}")

res = stats.permutation_test((cover, control), lambda a, b: a.mean() - b.mean(),
                             permutation_type="independent", n_resamples=np.inf)
print(f"scipy.stats.permutation_test (exact): {res.pvalue:.4f}")
print(f"Welch t-test:                         {stats.ttest_ind(cover, control, equal_var=False).pvalue:.4f}")
```

All $\binom{16}{8} = 12\,870$ splits can be enumerated in under a second: 352 of them give a difference at least as large as 0.50 in either direction, so the exact p-value is 0.027. The random-permutation estimate, SciPy's exact computation and Welch's $t$ test (0.023) all tell the same story. When they agree, the permutation test's value is reassurance; when they disagree, usually because of small samples or outliers, the permutation test is the one whose assumptions are easier to defend — *if* the data are exchangeable under the null.

</Section>

<Section id="cross-validation" kind="examples" title="Cross-validation is resampling too">

The bootstrap asks how much an estimate varies; **cross-validation** asks how well a prediction method will do on data it has not seen. In $K$-fold cross-validation, the data are split into $K$ roughly equal **folds**; each fold in turn is held out, the method is fitted to the other $K - 1$, and its errors on the held-out fold are recorded. The average of these errors estimates the method's prediction error. Stephen Bates, Trevor Hastie and Robert Tibshirani have shown that it estimates the average error of the *fitting procedure* over training sets like ours, rather than the error of the particular model we end up with, and that the usual standard errors for it are too small.

Cross-validation is honest only if the held-out fold plays no part in **any** step of the fitting. The classic trap is to select variables using all the data and then cross-validate only the final model. The program reproduces that trap on pure noise: 50 cases, 2,000 random features, labels unrelated to any of them, and a simple nearest-centroid classifier using the 20 features most correlated with the labels. Every classifier has a true error rate of 50%.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2002)
n, p, keep, K = 50, 2_000, 20, 5

def nearest_centroid_error(Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte):
    """Classify each test row by the closer of the two class means in the training data."""
    c0, c1 = Xtr[ytr == 0].mean(axis=0), Xtr[ytr == 1].mean(axis=0)
    pred = (((Xte - c1) ** 2).sum(axis=1) < ((Xte - c0) ** 2).sum(axis=1)).astype(int)
    return np.mean(pred != yte)

def top_features(X, y, k):
    """The k features most correlated (in absolute value) with the labels."""
    Xc, yc = X - X.mean(axis=0), y - y.mean()
    corr = (Xc * yc[:, None]).sum(axis=0) / np.sqrt((Xc**2).sum(axis=0) * (yc**2).sum())
    return np.argsort(-np.abs(corr))[:k]

wrong, right = [], []
for rep in range(20):
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, p))                     # pure noise: no feature carries any signal
    y = rng.permutation(np.repeat([0, 1], n // 2))  # labels unrelated to X
    folds = np.arange(n) % K
    rng.shuffle(folds)
    chosen_once = top_features(X, y, keep)          # WRONG: select using all the data
    e_wrong, e_right = [], []
    for k in range(K):
        tr, te = folds != k, folds == k
        e_wrong.append(nearest_centroid_error(X[tr][:, chosen_once], y[tr], X[te][:, chosen_once], y[te]))
        chosen = top_features(X[tr], y[tr], keep)   # RIGHT: select inside each training fold
        e_right.append(nearest_centroid_error(X[tr][:, chosen], y[tr], X[te][:, chosen], y[te]))
    wrong.append(np.mean(e_wrong)); right.append(np.mean(e_right))

print("estimated error rate when the true error rate of any classifier is 0.50")
print(f"  selection outside cross-validation: mean {np.mean(wrong):.3f}  (range {min(wrong):.2f} to {max(wrong):.2f})")
print(f"  selection inside cross-validation:  mean {np.mean(right):.3f}  (range {min(right):.2f} to {max(right):.2f})")
```

Selecting the features outside the cross-validation produces an estimated error of 4%: a "discovery" of near-perfect prediction from noise, in every one of the 20 simulated data sets. Moving the selection inside each fold gives 50%, the truth. The leak is that the held-out cases helped choose the features, so they are no longer new to the model. The same leak occurs with scaling, imputation of missing values, oversampling, and tuning of hyperparameters; in software, a **pipeline** object that bundles every step and is refitted inside each fold is the standard defence (see the Data Science elective's [machine learning for tabular data](/en/cs/electives/data-science/machine-learning-for-tabular-data/)). Notice also how much the honest estimate varies — from 0.34 to 0.68 with 50 cases — another reason to report cross-validated errors with humility.

</Section>

<Section id="monte-carlo-studies" kind="computation" title="Monte Carlo studies: testing methods where the truth is known">

How do we know whether a method — an interval, a test, a sampler — works? We cannot check it on real data, where the truth is unknown. But we can on simulated data, where we chose the truth. A **Monte Carlo study** (or simulation study) generates many data sets from a known model, applies the method to each, and measures its performance: bias, coverage, error rates, power. Tim Morris, Ian White and Michael Crowther's guide organises a study by its **aims**, **data-generating mechanisms**, **estimands**, **methods** and **performance measures** (ADEMP), and asks that the Monte Carlo standard error of every performance measure be reported.

The program runs two such studies. The first draws 1,000 samples of 24 values from a lognormal population shaped like the repair times, whose true mean is known, and checks how often each 95% interval covers it. The second tests a Bayesian computation by **simulation-based calibration** (SBC), proposed by Sean Talts and colleagues in 2018: draw a "true" parameter from the prior, simulate data from it, run the inference, and record the **rank** of the true value among the posterior draws. If the computation is correct, the true value is just another draw from the posterior, so its rank is uniformly distributed. We test the correct beta–binomial posterior and a buggy one that counts every observation twice.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(2019)

# Study 1: how often do 95% intervals for a mean cover the truth, for skewed data?
# Population: lognormal with log-mean 1.5 and log-sd 0.8 (like the repair times); true mean known.
mu, sigma, n, reps, B = 1.5, 0.8, 24, 1_000, 1_000
true_mean = np.exp(mu + sigma**2 / 2)
z = stats.norm.ppf([0.025, 0.975])
hits = {"t": 0, "percentile": 0, "BCa": 0}
above = {"t": 0, "percentile": 0, "BCa": 0}      # misses with the whole interval below the truth
widths = {"t": 0.0, "percentile": 0.0, "BCa": 0.0}
for _ in range(reps):
    x = rng.lognormal(mu, sigma, n)
    m = x.mean()
    boot = x[rng.integers(0, n, size=(B, n))].mean(axis=1)
    h = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1) * x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    jack = (x.sum() - x) / (n - 1)
    d = jack.mean() - jack
    a = (d**3).sum() / (6 * ((d**2).sum()) ** 1.5)
    z0 = stats.norm.ppf(np.mean(boot < m))
    ivs = {"t": (m - h, m + h),
           "percentile": tuple(np.quantile(boot, [0.025, 0.975])),
           "BCa": tuple(np.quantile(boot, stats.norm.cdf(z0 + (z0 + z) / (1 - a * (z0 + z)))))}
    for k, (lo, hi) in ivs.items():
        hits[k] += lo <= true_mean <= hi
        above[k] += hi < true_mean
        widths[k] += hi - lo
print(f"Coverage of nominal 95% intervals for a lognormal mean, n = {n}, {reps} simulated data sets")
for k in hits:
    print(f"  {k:10} coverage {hits[k] / reps:.3f}   average width {widths[k] / reps:.2f}"
          f"   misses: interval too low {above[k]}, too high {reps - hits[k] - above[k]}")
print(f"  (Monte Carlo standard error of each coverage: about {np.sqrt(0.95 * 0.05 / reps):.3f})")

# Study 2: simulation-based calibration of a Bayesian computation (beta-binomial, n = 20).
def sbc_ranks(posterior, sims=2_000, draws=99):
    ranks = np.empty(sims, dtype=int)
    for s in range(sims):
        theta = rng.beta(2, 2)                      # 1. draw a "true" parameter from the prior
        k = rng.binomial(20, theta)                 # 2. simulate data from it
        post = posterior(k, draws)                  # 3. run the inference being tested
        ranks[s] = np.sum(post < theta)             # 4. rank of the truth among the posterior draws
    return ranks

correct = lambda k, L: rng.beta(2 + k, 2 + 20 - k, L)
buggy = lambda k, L: rng.beta(2 + 2 * k, 2 + 2 * (20 - k), L)   # counts every observation twice
for name, post in [("correct posterior", correct), ("overconfident bug", buggy)]:
    counts = np.bincount(sbc_ranks(post) // 10, minlength=10)  # ranks 0..99 in 10 bins
    p = stats.chisquare(counts).pvalue
    print(f"\n{name}: rank counts in 10 bins (200 expected in each), chi-square p = {p:.3g}")
    for i, c in enumerate(counts):
        print(f"  ranks {10 * i:2}-{10 * i + 9:2} {c:4} " + "#" * (c // 10))
```

The first study is humbling. With 24 skewed observations, no method delivers 95%: all three cover about 90% of the time, well outside the Monte Carlo error of 0.007. The direction of the misses is telling: the $t$ interval missed 95 times by lying entirely below the truth and only 3 times above it, because samples that miss the rare large values have both small means and small standard deviations. BCa balances its misses better (71 and 28), as its design intends, but it cannot conjure up information about a tail that the sample did not see. The practical lesson is that "second-order accurate" is a promise about large samples, and for skewed data with small $n$ a Monte Carlo study like this one, tailored to your situation, is worth running before you trust any interval.

The second study shows what SBC can detect. The correct posterior gives a flat histogram ($p = 0.69$ for a chi-square test of uniformity). The buggy posterior, too narrow because it treats 20 observations as 40, leaves the true value outside the bulk of its draws far too often: the histogram is U-shaped, with $p \approx 10^{-61}$. Other shapes have other meanings: a hump in the middle means the posterior is too wide, and a slope means it is biased in one direction. SBC is now a standard check for new samplers and for complex models fitted with Stan or PyMC, because it tests the whole pipeline — model code, sampler and summaries — at once.

<Section id="failures" kind="human" title="When resampling fails">

Resampling methods feel assumption-free. They are not, and their assumptions are worth knowing as precisely as a formula's.

- **Extremes.** For the sample maximum, the bootstrap fails outright. A resample includes the largest observation with probability $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \approx 0.632$, so about 63% of the bootstrap replicates equal the sample maximum exactly. For a population with an upper limit, such as a uniform distribution, the true sampling distribution of the maximum is continuous and lies entirely below that limit; the bootstrap distribution, a spike at the sample maximum with scattered values below it, looks nothing like it. The same problem affects other statistics that depend on a few extreme values, and estimates at the boundary of the parameter space.
- **Heavy tails.** If the population has infinite variance, the bootstrap distribution of the mean does not converge to the right limit. With finite but large variance it merely converges slowly, as the coverage study showed.
- **Dependence.** Resampling individual observations assumes they are independent. For time series it destroys the autocorrelation and understates the uncertainty; the **block bootstrap** resamples whole stretches of consecutive observations instead. For clustered data — pupils in schools, measurements on the same patient — resample whole clusters. Cross-validation needs the same care: folds that split a patient's records, or put tomorrow's data in the training set and yesterday's in the test set, leak information.
- **Non-exchangeability.** A permutation test of equal means assumes that, under the null, the groups are interchangeable. If they have different variances and different sizes, they are not, and the test can reject a true null too often or too rarely. Permuting a studentised statistic, such as Welch's $t$, restores approximate validity.
- **The data themselves.** Resampling a biased sample reproduces its bias exactly. No amount of computation repairs a sample that left out the people, places or times that matter.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The bootstrap treats the sample as the population, so it cannot see anything the sample did not contain. With 24 repair times it has never seen a 40-day repair, and its intervals act as if such repairs were impossible. Before trusting a resampling result, ask whether the sample could plausibly have missed something that matters for your question.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="Resampling in practice">

**History.** Maurice Quenouille's jackknife (1949), named by John Tukey, was the first resampling method for bias and variance. Efron's 1979 bootstrap generalised it and became practical as computers spread; his 1987 BCa interval and the books by Efron and Robert Tibshirani (1993) and by Anthony Davison and David Hinkley (1997) set out the theory. Permutation tests go back to Fisher and to Edwin Pitman in the 1930s, when enumerating relabellings was done by hand for tiny samples. Cross-validation was formalised by Mervyn Stone and Seymour Geisser in the 1970s, and is today the default way to compare prediction methods.

**Tools.** In Python, `scipy.stats` has `bootstrap` (percentile, basic and BCa), `permutation_test` and `monte_carlo_test`, and scikit-learn has cross-validation splitters (including `GroupKFold` and `TimeSeriesSplit`) and pipelines. In R, the classic `boot` package does the same, shown here for comparison (not run here):

```r
# R, not run here: bootstrap intervals for the mean repair time with the boot package
library(boot)
b <- boot(days, function(x, i) mean(x[i]), R = 10000)
boot.ci(b, type = c("perc", "basic", "bca"))
```

**Habits.** Resample in the same way the data were generated: individuals if they were sampled independently, clusters if clusters were sampled, blocks for time series, and within strata for stratified designs. Use thousands of resamples and record the seed. Keep every data-driven step inside cross-validation. Report Monte Carlo error. And when a method is new to you or your data are unusual, run a small simulation study before trusting it — it is the statistician's equivalent of testing code.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The bootstrap creates new data."** It reuses the data you have, to estimate how an estimate would vary. It cannot increase the information in the sample, and it cannot reveal values the sample never contained.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Bootstrap intervals are exact, or always better than formulas."** They are approximations whose accuracy improves with $n$. For 24 skewed values, the percentile and BCa intervals covered about 90% of the time, like the $t$ interval. For the sample maximum the bootstrap is simply wrong.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A permutation test makes no assumptions."** It assumes exchangeability under the null hypothesis, and it tests a specific null — usually that the groups' distributions are identical, not merely that their means are equal.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The cross-validated error is the error of my final model."** It estimates the average error of the fitting procedure on training sets of that size, and it is honest only if every step of the procedure, including variable selection and tuning, was repeated inside each fold.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"None of 10,000 random permutations was as extreme as the data, so $p = 0$."** The observed labelling is itself one of the possible labellings, so the p-value is at least $1/10\,001$. Report $p < 0.0001$, or better, run more permutations if the exact size matters.

</Misconception>
