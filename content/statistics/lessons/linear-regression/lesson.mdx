<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A line through a cloud of points">

Chimwemwe is an agronomist working with smallholder maize farmers near Lilongwe, in Malawi. Fertiliser is expensive, and the question farmers put to her is practical: *if I apply more nitrogen, how much more maize will I harvest?* To answer it she runs a trial on one farm: 24 plots, eight rates of nitrogen from 0 to 140 kilograms per hectare, three plots at each rate, with the rates assigned to plots at random. At harvest she weighs the grain from each plot. (The numbers in this lesson are synthetic, generated by a program, but they behave like real trial data.)

Plotted, the 24 yields form a cloud that rises from left to right. No line passes through every point: plots that received the same nitrogen differ by half a tonne per hectare or more, because soil, water and pests vary from plot to plot. Statistics begins by accepting that variation. The question is not "which line passes through the points?" but "which line summarises how yield *tends* to change with nitrogen, how precisely do the data pin it down, and how far can we trust it?"

That is **linear regression**, the most widely used model in statistics. It is how researchers estimate how blood pressure changes with salt intake, how wages change with years of schooling, how a sensor's reading drifts with temperature. It is also the gateway to the rest of this level: <LessonLink slug="multiple-regression-and-diagnostics">multiple regression</LessonLink> adds predictors, <LessonLink slug="generalised-linear-models">generalised linear models</LessonLink> handle yes-or-no outcomes and counts, and <LessonLink slug="experimental-design-and-anova">analysis of variance</LessonLink> turns out to be regression in disguise.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Before fitting any line, ask what it is for. Describing these 24 plots, predicting the next plot, and telling a farmer what nitrogen will do on her own field are three different questions. The arithmetic of the fit is the same for all three; what you are entitled to conclude is not.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="What makes a line the best one?">

A straight line is $\hat y = b_0 + b_1 x$: an **intercept** $b_0$, the height of the line at $x = 0$, and a **slope** $b_1$, the change in $\hat y$ when $x$ increases by one unit. For any candidate line, each plot has a **residual**: its observed yield minus the yield the line predicts at its nitrogen rate, $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$. Points above the line have positive residuals, points below it negative ones.

A good line makes the residuals small overall, and the classic way to make "small overall" precise is to minimise the **sum of squared residuals**:

$$\text{SSE}(b_0, b_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2.$$

Why vertical distances? Because the task is to predict $y$ from $x$: the nitrogen rate was set by the experimenter and is known exactly, and the unpredictable part lives in the yield. Why squares? Squaring makes every miss count positively and punishes large misses more than small ones, and, decisively, it turns the minimisation into two linear equations, so the answer has a formula. Other choices exist (minimising *absolute* residuals gives a line that resists outliers, the start of quantile regression), but least squares is the default and the foundation of this level.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The model and the least-squares estimates">

A line fitted to data is a description. To make inferences, about what would happen on other plots or in another season, we need a **model**: a statement about how the data were generated.

<Definition term="simple linear regression model">

The responses $Y_1, \dots, Y_n$, observed at known values $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of a predictor, satisfy

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i,$$

where the **errors** $\varepsilon_i$ are independent, with mean 0 and the same variance $\sigma^2$. For exact small-sample inference we add that they are normally distributed. The unknown **parameters** are $\beta_0$, $\beta_1$ and $\sigma$.

</Definition>

Greek letters stand for the truth we do not know; the Latin letters $b_0, b_1$ (also written $\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1$) are estimates computed from data. The assumptions are often remembered as **LINE**: a **L**inear mean, **I**ndependent errors, **N**ormal errors, **E**qual variance. They are not equally important: a wrong shape for the mean, or dependent errors, can ruin the conclusions, while mild non-normality matters little once $n$ is moderate, thanks to the central limit theorem from <LessonLink slug="sampling-distributions-and-the-clt">sampling distributions</LessonLink>.

<Definition term="fitted values and residuals">

Given estimates $b_0, b_1$, the **fitted value** of observation $i$ is $\hat y_i = b_0 + b_1 x_i$ and its **residual** is $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$. Residuals are the observable stand-ins for the unobservable errors $\varepsilon_i$.

</Definition>

Write $\bar x, \bar y$ for the means, and $S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$, $S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$.

<Theorem name="Least-squares estimates">

If the $x_i$ are not all equal, exactly one line minimises the sum of squared residuals. Its slope and intercept are

$$b_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x.$$

Equivalently $b_1 = r\, s_y / s_x$, where $r$ is the correlation and $s_x, s_y$ the standard deviations. The line passes through the point of means $(\bar x, \bar y)$, and its residuals sum to zero and are uncorrelated with $x$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

SSE is a sum of squares of linear functions of $(b_0, b_1)$, hence a smooth convex function, and its minimum is where both partial derivatives vanish:

$$\frac{\partial\,\text{SSE}}{\partial b_0} = -2\sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial\,\text{SSE}}{\partial b_1} = -2\sum x_i (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i) = 0.$$

The first equation says $\sum e_i = 0$, which rearranges to $b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x$. The second says $\sum x_i e_i = 0$. Subtracting $\bar x \sum e_i = 0$ from it gives $\sum (x_i - \bar x) e_i = 0$, and substituting $e_i = (y_i - \bar y) - b_1(x_i - \bar x)$ gives $S_{xy} - b_1 S_{xx} = 0$. Since the $x_i$ are not all equal, $S_{xx} > 0$, so $b_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$. The Hessian of SSE is $2\begin{pmatrix} n & \sum x_i \\ \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2 \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $4 n S_{xx} > 0$ and positive diagonal, so this stationary point is the unique minimum. Finally, $r = S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx} S_{yy}}$ and $s_y/s_x = \sqrt{S_{yy}/S_{xx}}$, whose product is $S_{xy}/S_{xx}$; and $\sum (x_i - \bar x) e_i = 0$ is exactly zero covariance between $x$ and the residuals.

</Proof>

The two conditions in the proof are the **normal equations**, and they have a geometric meaning worth carrying into the rest of the course. Stack the data as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$: the responses $\mathbf y$, and the columns of the **design matrix** $X = [\,\mathbf 1 \;\; \mathbf x\,]$. Every candidate line gives a vector of fitted values $X\mathbf b$, a point in the plane spanned by $\mathbf 1$ and $\mathbf x$. Least squares picks the point of that plane closest to $\mathbf y$, which is the **orthogonal projection** of $\mathbf y$ onto it: the residual vector $\mathbf e = \mathbf y - X\mathbf b$ is perpendicular to both columns, $X^\top \mathbf e = \mathbf 0$. Solving gives

$$\mathbf b = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf y,$$

a formula that works unchanged when $X$ has more columns, as it will in the next lesson. Column spaces, projections and rank are covered in Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">linear algebra</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="fitting" kind="computation" title="Fitting the line in code">

The program below generates the synthetic trial, fits the line from the formulas, and checks the answer against two library routines. It also prints the average residual at each nitrogen rate, which turns out to be the most interesting line of output.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Synthetic field trial: 24 maize plots, nitrogen fertiliser N (kg/ha), grain yield (t/ha).
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
N = np.repeat([0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 140], 3).astype(float)
true_curve = lambda n: 1.2 + 4.0 * (1 - np.exp(-n / 110))      # how this synthetic world works
yield_t = true_curve(N) + rng.normal(0, 0.35, N.size)

# Least squares from the formulas
xbar, ybar = N.mean(), yield_t.mean()
Sxx = np.sum((N - xbar) ** 2)
Sxy = np.sum((N - xbar) * (yield_t - ybar))
b1 = Sxy / Sxx
b0 = ybar - b1 * xbar
resid = yield_t - (b0 + b1 * N)
R2 = 1 - np.sum(resid ** 2) / np.sum((yield_t - ybar) ** 2)
print(f"slope     b1 = {b1:.4f} t/ha per kg/ha of N  ({1000 * b1:.1f} kg of grain per kg of N)")
print(f"intercept b0 = {b0:.3f} t/ha")
print(f"R^2 = {R2:.3f}   residuals sum to zero: {abs(resid.sum()) < 1e-9}")

# The same fit two other ways
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(N), N])
coef = np.linalg.lstsq(X, yield_t, rcond=None)[0]
fit = stats.linregress(N, yield_t)
print("np.linalg.lstsq:", np.round(coef, 4), "  scipy linregress:", round(fit.intercept, 4), round(fit.slope, 4))

# Mean residual at each dose: is a straight line the right shape?
print("N:            ", "  ".join(f"{n:5.0f}" for n in np.unique(N)))
print("mean residual:", "  ".join(f"{resid[N == n].mean():+5.2f}" for n in np.unique(N)))

for n in [60, 140, 300]:
    print(f"N = {n:3d}: the line predicts {b0 + b1 * n:4.2f} t/ha; this world's true mean is {true_curve(n):4.2f}")
```

Reading the output:

- **The slope.** Each extra kilogram of nitrogen per hectare goes with 0.0202 t/ha more grain, which reads better as **20.2 kg of grain per kg of nitrogen**: if a kilogram of nitrogen costs more than about 20 kg of maize, extra fertiliser does not pay at these rates. Notice the wording "goes with". Here the rates were randomised, so reading the slope as the *effect* of nitrogen (on average, on these plots, this season) is justified. With observational data, as the <LessonLink slug="multiple-regression-and-diagnostics">next lesson</LessonLink> shows, a slope compares different units rather than describing what happens when you change one.
- **The intercept.** 1.45 t/ha is the line's yield with no nitrogen, meaningful here because $N = 0$ was in the trial. If the predictor were adult height in centimetres, the intercept would be the prediction for a person 0 cm tall. Interpret an intercept only when $x = 0$ lies within the data; otherwise centre the predictor ($x - \bar x$) so that the intercept becomes the prediction at the average.
- **Agreement.** The formulas, `np.linalg.lstsq` and `scipy.stats.linregress` agree to the digits printed, and the residuals sum to zero, as the theorem promised.
- **The mean residual at each rate.** It is negative at 0 (−0.54), positive in the middle (up to +0.34 at 60), and negative again at 120 and 140. The points bow above the line in the middle and fall below it at both ends: the true response is curved, rising steeply and then levelling off. Over this range the line is still a useful summary, because the curvature is modest compared with the plot-to-plot scatter. But the residuals have issued a warning about trusting the line *outside* the range, and the last three lines of output show why. We return to them below.

</Section>

<Section id="fit-quality" kind="human" title="How good is the fit? Residuals and R²">

The variation of the yields around their mean, $\text{SST} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2$, splits into two parts.

<Theorem name="Decomposition of variation">

For a least-squares line with an intercept,

$$\underbrace{\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{SST (total)}} = \underbrace{\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{SSR (explained by the line)}} + \underbrace{\sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}_{\text{SSE (residual)}}.$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $y_i - \bar y = (\hat y_i - \bar y) + e_i$, square, and sum. The cross term is $2\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y) e_i = 2(b_0 - \bar y) \sum e_i + 2 b_1 \sum x_i e_i$, and the two normal equations make both sums zero. Geometrically this is Pythagoras' theorem: $\hat{\mathbf y} - \bar y \mathbf 1$ lies in the column space of $X$, and $\mathbf e$ is perpendicular to it.

</Proof>

The **coefficient of determination** $R^2 = \text{SSR}/\text{SST} = 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}$ is the fraction of the variation in $y$ accounted for by the line. In simple regression it equals $r^2$, the squared correlation. For the trial $R^2 = 0.888$: the line accounts for about 89% of the plot-to-plot variation in yield, and the remaining 11% is scatter around the line.

R² is useful and much abused. Here is what it does *not* tell you.

- **Whether the line is the right shape.** Our data are curved and R² is 0.888. In 1973 Francis Anscombe published four small datasets with identical means, variances, correlation, fitted line and R²: a straight-line cloud, a smooth curve, a perfect line spoiled by one outlier, and a vertical stack of points whose whole slope comes from one far-off point. Only a picture tells them apart.
- **How precise predictions are.** R² depends on how spread out the $x$ values happen to be: the same relationship measured over rates 60 to 80 only would give a much smaller R², with the same scatter around the line. For prediction, the **residual standard error** $s = \sqrt{\text{SSE}/(n-2)}$, in the units of $y$, is the honest summary of how far typical points fall from the line.
- **Whether the relationship matters, or is causal.** A treatment that lowers blood pressure by 5 mmHg on average may explain only a few per cent of the variation between patients and still prevent many strokes; two quantities that both grow over time can have a high R² and no causal link.

The working statistician's first check is a **residual plot**: residuals against fitted values, or against $x$. A structureless horizontal band is what the model expects; a curve means the mean is not linear (our bow); a funnel means the variance is not constant; a few points far from the band are cases to investigate, not to delete quietly. The <LessonLink slug="multiple-regression-and-diagnostics">next lesson</LessonLink> makes these checks systematic, with leverage and influence.

</Section>

<Section id="inference" kind="computation" title="How sure are we about the slope?">

Run the trial again next season on other plots and the slope would come out a little different. The slope is a statistic with a sampling distribution, exactly like the sample mean in <LessonLink slug="estimation-and-confidence-intervals">estimation and confidence intervals</LessonLink>.

<Theorem name="Sampling distribution of the slope">

Under the model, $b_1$ is unbiased for $\beta_1$ and $\operatorname{Var}(b_1) = \sigma^2 / S_{xx}$. If the errors are normal, then with $s^2 = \text{SSE}/(n-2)$,

$$T = \frac{b_1 - \beta_1}{s/\sqrt{S_{xx}}} \sim t_{n-2}.$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Because $\sum (x_i - \bar x) = 0$, we can write $b_1 = \sum c_i Y_i$ with fixed weights $c_i = (x_i - \bar x)/S_{xx}$. These weights satisfy $\sum c_i = 0$ and $\sum c_i x_i = 1$, so $E[b_1] = \sum c_i(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i) = \beta_1$. By independence, $\operatorname{Var}(b_1) = \sigma^2 \sum c_i^2 = \sigma^2 / S_{xx}$, and a linear combination of independent normal variables is normal. The remaining step, that $(n-2)s^2/\sigma^2$ has a $\chi^2_{n-2}$ distribution independent of $b_1$, comes from the projection picture: the residual vector lies in the $(n-2)$-dimensional subspace perpendicular to the columns of $X$, and for normal errors projections onto perpendicular subspaces are independent. Dividing the standardised normal by the square root of the scaled chi-squared gives the $t$ distribution (see <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistics">statistical inference</TopicLink> in Lumenary Math).

</Proof>

The formula $\text{SE}(b_1) = \sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$ carries a lesson for design: the slope is pinned down better when the $x$ values are more spread out, and when there are more of them. To measure a slope, measure across a wide range. That is why the trial used rates from 0 to 140 rather than from 60 to 80.

From the theorem come the standard tools: a 95% **confidence interval** $b_1 \pm t_{0.975,\,n-2}\,\text{SE}(b_1)$, and a **test** of $H_0\colon \beta_1 = 0$ with statistic $t = b_1/\text{SE}(b_1)$, as in <LessonLink slug="hypothesis-testing">hypothesis testing</LessonLink>. For predictions at a new value $x_0$ there are two different intervals, and confusing them is a classic error:

- a **confidence interval for the mean response**, with standard error $s\sqrt{1/n + (x_0 - \bar x)^2/S_{xx}}$, says where the *average* yield of many plots given $x_0$ lies;
- a **prediction interval** for one new observation, with standard error $s\sqrt{1 + 1/n + (x_0 - \bar x)^2/S_{xx}}$, says where *a single* new plot's yield is likely to fall. The extra 1 is the new plot's own scatter, and no amount of data makes it go away.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
N = np.repeat([0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 140], 3).astype(float)
yield_t = 1.2 + 4.0 * (1 - np.exp(-N / 110)) + rng.normal(0, 0.35, N.size)

n = N.size
Sxx = np.sum((N - N.mean()) ** 2)
b1 = np.sum((N - N.mean()) * (yield_t - yield_t.mean())) / Sxx
b0 = yield_t.mean() - b1 * N.mean()
s = np.sqrt(np.sum((yield_t - b0 - b1 * N) ** 2) / (n - 2))   # residual standard error
se_b1 = s / np.sqrt(Sxx)
t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n - 2)
t_stat = b1 / se_b1
p = 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t_stat), df=n - 2)
print(f"s = {s:.3f} t/ha   SE(b1) = {se_b1:.5f}   t = {t_stat:.1f} on {n - 2} df   p = {p:.1e}")
print(f"95% CI for the slope: {b1 - t_crit * se_b1:.4f} to {b1 + t_crit * se_b1:.4f}")

for x0 in [100, 300]:
    mean0 = b0 + b1 * x0
    se_mean = s * np.sqrt(1 / n + (x0 - N.mean()) ** 2 / Sxx)
    se_new = s * np.sqrt(1 + 1 / n + (x0 - N.mean()) ** 2 / Sxx)
    print(f"N = {x0}: mean yield {mean0:.2f}, 95% CI {mean0 - t_crit * se_mean:.2f} to {mean0 + t_crit * se_mean:.2f}; "
          f"95% prediction interval for one new plot {mean0 - t_crit * se_new:.2f} to {mean0 + t_crit * se_new:.2f}")

# Does the formula tell the truth? Pretend the fitted line is the true one and rerun the trial 10,000 times.
sims = 10_000
Y = b0 + b1 * N + rng.normal(0, s, (sims, n))              # each row is one imagined trial
slopes = (Y - Y.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) @ (N - N.mean()) / Sxx
intercepts = Y.mean(axis=1) - slopes * N.mean()
s_sim = np.sqrt(np.sum((Y - intercepts[:, None] - slopes[:, None] * N) ** 2, axis=1) / (n - 2))
covered = np.abs(slopes - b1) <= t_crit * s_sim / np.sqrt(Sxx)
print(f"sd of the 10,000 slopes: {slopes.std(ddof=1):.5f}   formula sigma/sqrt(Sxx): {s / np.sqrt(Sxx):.5f}")
print(f"95% intervals that caught the true slope: {covered.mean():.1%}")
```

The slope is 0.0202 with a standard error of 0.00153, so the 95% confidence interval runs from 0.0170 to 0.0233 t/ha per kg/ha: roughly 17 to 23 kg of grain per kg of nitrogen. The $t$ statistic of 13.2, with its tiny $p$-value, says only that the slope is not zero, which no agronomist doubted; the interval is the informative part. At $N = 100$ the *mean* yield is pinned down to within about 0.18 t/ha either way, yet a *single* new plot could plausibly yield anything from 2.74 to 4.20 t/ha.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The second half of that program is the check I trust most. It reruns the trial 10,000 times in a world where the fitted line is the truth: the 10,000 slopes have a standard deviation of 0.00154, against 0.00153 from $\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$, and 94.9% of the intervals catch the true slope. When a formula and a simulation agree, you understand both a little better.

</Narrator>

<Narrator who="gideon">

That 94.9% was earned in a world built to satisfy the model. In a real field, neighbouring plots share soil and drainage, so their errors can be correlated even when the rates were randomised; and here the true response is curved, so "the" slope is only the best straight-line summary over 0 to 140 kg/ha. Independence and the shape of the mean matter far more than normality. Check them before quoting an interval to three decimal places.

</Narrator>

<Section id="galton" kind="history" title="Galton, heights and regression to the mean">

The word *regression* comes from a puzzle. In the 1880s the English scientist Francis Galton collected the heights of more than 900 adult children and their parents, from about 200 families. Children of very tall parents were, on average, tall, but less tall than their parents; children of very short parents were short, but less short. In an 1886 paper he called this "regression towards mediocrity", and the name stuck to the whole method, even though the phenomenon has nothing specially to do with heredity. (Galton's family data are in the [HistData package for R](https://cran.r-project.org/package=HistData). Galton also founded eugenics, a movement built on false and harmful ideas about human worth. Statistics owes him some of its tools, and owes it to its students to say so plainly.)

The explanation is in the least-squares formula. Measure both variables in **standard units** (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation). Then $b_1 = r\,s_y/s_x$ becomes simply $r$, and the regression line reads

$$\hat z_y = r\, z_x.$$

Unless the correlation is perfect, $|r| < 1$, so the predicted value is always *closer to the mean*, in standard units, than the value it was predicted from. That is all regression to the mean is: a consequence of imperfect correlation, with no cause required. Whenever two measurements are imperfectly correlated (a test and a retest, this year's crop and next year's, a parent's height and a child's), the extreme cases on one will, on average, be less extreme on the other.

Here is a world with no cause at all. Each synthetic pupil has a fixed skill, and sits two tests a week apart; each score is skill plus luck on the day, and nothing happens between the tests.

```python run
import numpy as np

# 10,000 synthetic pupils sit two tests a week apart. Nothing happens in between.
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
skill = rng.normal(60, 10, 10_000)              # what each pupil really knows
test1 = skill + rng.normal(0, 8, skill.size)    # plus luck on the day
test2 = skill + rng.normal(0, 8, skill.size)    # fresh luck, same skill

r = np.corrcoef(test1, test2)[0, 1]
slope = np.polyfit(test1, test2, 1)[0]
print(f"correlation r = {r:.3f}; slope of test2 on test1 = {slope:.3f}; theory 100/164 = {100 / 164:.3f}")
print(f"spread: sd(test1) = {test1.std():.2f}, sd(test2) = {test2.std():.2f}")

top = test1 >= np.quantile(test1, 0.9)
bottom = test1 <= np.quantile(test1, 0.1)
print(f"top 10% on test 1:    mean {test1[top].mean():.1f} -> {test2[top].mean():.1f} on test 2")
print(f"bottom 10% on test 1: mean {test1[bottom].mean():.1f} -> {test2[bottom].mean():.1f} on test 2")

top2 = test2 >= np.quantile(test2, 0.9)
print(f"top 10% on test 2:    mean {test2[top2].mean():.1f} on test 2, but {test1[top2].mean():.1f} on test 1")
```

The top tenth on test 1 averaged 82.1 and then 73.6; the bottom tenth rose from 37.7 to 46.5. The correlation is 0.603, close to the theoretical $\operatorname{Var}(\text{skill})/\operatorname{Var}(\text{score}) = 100/164$. Two further lines of output dispose of common misreadings. The spread does not shrink (standard deviations 12.64 and 12.83), so the pupils are not all drifting towards average. And the effect runs backwards in time too: the top tenth on test 2 averaged only 73.6 on test 1.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The question to ask of any group picked for being extreme is: extreme because of what? The top pupils on test 1 were selected partly for skill and partly for good luck on the day. Skill comes back for test 2; the luck is drawn afresh, and fresh luck is ordinary on average.

Regression to the mean is behind many false conclusions. Speed cameras tend to be installed where collisions have recently spiked, and collisions at such sites would fall on average even without a camera, so careful evaluations estimate how much of the fall is regression. Patients enter clinical trials when their symptoms are worst, and improve on average even on placebo. Pupils given extra tutoring because they did worst in a mock exam improve, partly because of the tutoring and partly by regression; only a comparison with similar pupils who were not tutored, ideally chosen at random, can separate the two. That is the subject of <LessonLink slug="experimental-design-and-anova">experimental design</LessonLink>.

<Section id="extrapolation" kind="human" title="Beyond the data: extrapolation">

Return to the last lines of the first program. At 300 kg/ha of nitrogen, about twice the largest rate in the trial, the line predicts 7.50 t/ha, and the prediction interval from the second program is 6.47 to 8.53 t/ha. In the synthetic world that generated the data, the true mean yield at 300 kg/ha is 4.94 t/ha, far outside the interval. Maize cannot use unlimited nitrogen: the response levels off, and the line, which fitted well enough between 0 and 140, sails on upwards. The data contained one hint of this, the bowed residuals, and nothing more.

The interval was not miscalculated. Every interval in this lesson is *conditional on the model*: it measures the uncertainty from noise, assuming the straight line holds. Its width grows only slowly with distance from $\bar x$, through the $(x_0 - \bar x)^2/S_{xx}$ term, and says nothing about the real danger outside the data, that the model's shape is wrong there. No statistical method can reveal the shape of a relationship where there are no data; only subject knowledge or new data can.

Extrapolation takes many forms:

- **Beyond the range of a predictor**, as here. A related warning sign is a prediction that is physically impossible, such as a negative weight or a probability above 1.
- **Forward in time.** A straight trend fitted to ten years of data and projected forty years ahead is a statement of "if the trend continues", not a forecast. Time series need their own methods, in <LessonLink slug="time-series">time series</LessonLink>.
- **To other populations.** A trial on the sandy soils of one district says less about clay soils elsewhere; a drug trial in adults says less about children. Whether a result carries over is called **external validity** or transportability, and it is taken up in <LessonLink slug="causal-inference">causal inference</LessonLink>.

A grave example combines extrapolation with a second mistake. Before the launch of the space shuttle *Challenger* in January 1986, engineers and managers discussed whether cold damaged the rubber O-ring seals in the boosters. The forecast, around freezing, was far colder than any previous launch (the coldest had been about 12 °C), and part of the discussion looked only at flights on which seals had been damaged, among which the link with temperature looked weak. Including the undamaged flights shows damage concentrated at the lowest temperatures. The shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after launch, and seven crew members died. Statisticians still teach both lessons from it: never discard the cases where nothing happened, and treat predictions outside the data with the deepest suspicion.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view">

In practice no one codes the formulas by hand. In R the tool is `lm()`; in Python, [statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/regression.html) gives the same table of estimates, standard errors, $t$ statistics and intervals. Both are shown for reference and are not run here:

```r
# R (not run here)
trial <- data.frame(N = N, yield = yield_t)
fit <- lm(yield ~ N, data = trial)
summary(fit)                      # estimates, standard errors, t, p, R^2
confint(fit)                      # confidence intervals for the coefficients
predict(fit, data.frame(N = 100), interval = "prediction")
```

```python
# Python with statsmodels (not run here)
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
fit = smf.ols("yield_t ~ N", data=df).fit()
print(fit.summary())
```

[scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/linear_model.html)'s `LinearRegression` computes the same coefficients but deliberately reports no standard errors: it is built for prediction, where the question is how well the model predicts new data, not how precisely each coefficient is known. Neither view is wrong; they answer different questions.

A few things professionals know that textbooks sometimes leave out:

- **Numerics.** Libraries never compute $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ explicitly, because doing so squares the condition number of the problem and can lose half the available digits. R's `lm` uses a QR decomposition of $X$; NumPy's `lstsq` uses the singular value decomposition. See Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.comp.numerical-methods">numerical methods</TopicLink>.
- **Unequal variance.** When residuals fan out, the coefficients are still unbiased, but the usual standard errors are wrong. Econometricians routinely report **heteroscedasticity-consistent** ("sandwich" or "robust") standard errors, which remain valid without the equal-variance assumption; transformations, in the next lesson, are another remedy.
- **Errors in the predictor.** Least squares assumes $x$ is measured exactly. If $x$ carries measurement error (blood pressure measured once, self-reported diet), the slope is biased towards zero, an effect epidemiologists call **regression dilution**. Errors-in-variables methods correct it when the size of the measurement error is known.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Regressing $y$ on $x$ and $x$ on $y$ give the same line."** They do not. The slope of $y$ on $x$ is $r\,s_y/s_x$; the slope of $x$ on $y$ is $r\,s_x/s_y$. Drawn on the same axes, the two lines coincide only when $|r| = 1$. They answer different questions: predicting $y$ from $x$ minimises vertical misses, predicting $x$ from $y$ horizontal ones. Each line shows regression to the mean in its own direction.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Regression to the mean means that everything becomes average over time."** It does not. In the simulation the spread of test 2 was the same as the spread of test 1. Individuals move in both directions; it is only the *groups selected for being extreme* that move, on average, towards the middle, because their selection favoured luck that does not repeat.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A tiny $p$-value for the slope means a strong or important relationship."** A $p$-value measures how surprising the data would be if the slope were exactly zero. With enough data, a slope too small to matter is "highly significant". Report the slope with its confidence interval, in units someone can act on.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Linear regression needs $y$ to be normally distributed."** The model assumes the *errors around the line* are normal, and even that matters mainly for small samples. The overall distribution of $y$ can be anything, since it reflects the spread of the $x$ values too. A histogram of $y$ is not a check of the model; a plot of residuals is.

</Misconception>

</Section>
