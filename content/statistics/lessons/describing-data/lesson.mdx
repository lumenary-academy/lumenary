<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Too many numbers to see">

Imagine your teacher hands you a sheet with the heights of 30 pupils in your class, measured in centimetres:

151, 146, 156, 139, 150, 157, 149, 153, 165, 150, 154, 142, 156, 150, 148, 151, 161, 143, 156, 152, 147, 150, 154, 157, 149, 162, 151, 153, 156, 150

What can you say about these heights? Staring at the list does not help much. Your eyes jump around, and it is hard to say even what a "typical" height is. Now imagine 30,000 heights, or the incomes of 30 million people.

This lesson gives you the tools statisticians use first, every time, to turn a pile of numbers into something a person can understand: a few well-chosen **numbers** that summarise the data, and a few well-chosen **pictures** that show its shape. These are the same 30 heights you met as a simulation in <LessonLink slug="what-is-statistics">What is statistics?</LessonLink>, so you can compare.

There are two questions to ask about almost any set of numbers:

1. **Where is the middle?** What is a typical value?
2. **How spread out are they?** Are the values all close together, or scattered far apart?

But before either question, there is an even earlier one: what *kind* of data do we have?

</Section>

<Section id="kinds" kind="definitions" title="Kinds of data">

Here is a small part of a class survey:

| Pupil | Country of birth | Favourite fruit | Siblings | Height (cm) | How much do you like maths? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ines | Portugal | mango | 2 | 151 | a lot |
| Tomasz | Poland | apple | 0 | 146 | a little |
| Aroha | New Zealand | banana | 3 | 156 | a lot |
| Hamid | Afghanistan | mango | 1 | 139 | somewhat |

<Definition term="categorical and numerical data">

A **categorical** variable puts each observation into a group or category: country of birth, favourite fruit, eye colour. A **numerical** variable is a number that you could sensibly do arithmetic with: number of siblings, height, time.

</Definition>

Numerical variables come in two kinds. **Counts** (sometimes called *discrete* data) can only be whole numbers: you can have 2 siblings or 3 siblings, but not 2.4. **Measurements** (sometimes called *continuous* data) can take any value in between: a height could be 151 cm, or 151.3 cm, or 151.27 cm, depending only on how precise your tape measure is.

Some categorical variables have a natural **order**: "a little", "somewhat", "a lot", or clothing sizes S, M, L. They are still categories, not numbers, even if someone writes them as 1, 2 and 3.

Why does the kind of data matter? Because it decides which summaries and which pictures make sense. You can find the average height, but there is no such thing as the "average country of birth"!

<Narrator who="chloe">

Phone numbers and postcodes are made of digits, but they are categorical: adding two phone numbers gives nonsense. The test is not "does it look like a number?" but "does arithmetic on it answer a real question?"

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="centre" kind="intuition" title="The middle: mean, median and mode">

There are three common ways to describe a typical value, and each answers a slightly different question.

<Definition term="mean">

The **mean** is what you get if you add up all the values and then share the total out equally: add the values, then divide by how many there are. It is what most people call "the average".

</Definition>

<Definition term="median">

The **median** is the middle value when the data are put in order from smallest to largest. If there is an even number of values, there are two middle values, and the median is halfway between them. Half the values are at or below the median, and half are at or above it.

</Definition>

<Definition term="mode">

The **mode** is the value, or category, that appears most often. A data set can have two modes, or more, or none worth mentioning.

</Definition>

<Example title="Points in six netball matches">

Nomvula scored 8, 13, 4, 10, 8 and 5 points in six netball matches.

- **Mean.** The total is 8 + 13 + 4 + 10 + 8 + 5 = 48 points. Shared equally over 6 matches: 48 ÷ 6 = **8 points**.
- **Median.** In order: 4, 5, 8, 8, 10, 13. With six values the two middle ones are the third and fourth, 8 and 8, so the median is **8 points**.
- **Mode.** 8 appears twice and everything else once, so the mode is **8 points**.

Here all three agree. That happens when the data are fairly balanced around the middle. Often they don't agree, and then the differences tell you something.

</Example>

### When the mean misleads: income

Nine people work at a small bakery. Their monthly pay, in an imaginary currency, is:

300, 310, 320, 320, 340, 350, 380, 400 and 2,500.

The first eight are the bakers; the 2,500 is the owner. The total is 5,220, so the **mean** pay is 5,220 ÷ 9 = **580**. The **median** is the fifth value in order, **340**. The **mode** is **320**.

An advertisement that says "average pay at our bakery: 580!" would be telling the truth about the mean. But **eight of the nine people earn less than 580**, and none of the bakers earns anything close to it. One very large value has dragged the mean far above what is typical. The median, 340, describes a typical worker much better.

This is not a made-up problem. In nearly every country, a few people earn very much more than most, so the mean income is well above the median income. That is why statistics offices usually report the **median** income when they want to describe a typical household.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Let me try it. If the owner's pay rose from 2,500 to 25,000, the mean would jump from 580 to 3,080, more than nine times what any baker earns, but the median would not move at all: it is still 340. The median only cares about which value is in the middle, not about how far away the biggest value is.

</Narrator>

Lumenary Math proves this, and more, in <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistics-intro">Statistics: centre, spread and distributions</TopicLink>.

### When the median and mode mislead

The median is not always the right choice either. If you are planning how much flour a bakery needs for a month, you need the **total**, and the mean (total ÷ days) is what helps you; the median throws away information about the very busy days.

The mode can mislead too. Fifteen pupils said how many books they read over the holidays:

0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, 10.

The mode is **0**, because four pupils read no books at all. But it would be wrong to say "the typical pupil read nothing": eleven of the fifteen read at least one, the median is **3** books, and the mean is 49 ÷ 15, about **3.3** books. The mode is most useful for **categorical** data, where it is the only one of the three that makes sense: the modal favourite fruit in a class is a perfectly good summary, but the "mean fruit" is nonsense.

| Summary | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean | totals and fair shares; balanced data | a few extreme values pull it a long way |
| Median | a "typical" value in lopsided data, like incomes or house prices | ignores how big the extreme values are |
| Mode | categories (favourite fruit, most common shoe size) | can be at an edge, far from typical |

</Section>

<Section id="spread" kind="definitions" title="How spread out? Range, IQR and standard deviation">

In <LessonLink slug="what-is-statistics">the first lesson</LessonLink>, two bus routes had the same mean journey time, 23 minutes, but one was dependable and the other wildly variable. A summary of the middle is never enough: we also need to describe the **spread**.

<Definition term="range">

The **range** is the largest value minus the smallest value.

</Definition>

The range is easy, but it depends on just two values, the most extreme ones, so a single unusual value can make it huge. The next measure ignores the extremes.

<Definition term="quartiles and interquartile range">

Put the data in order and split them at the median into a lower half and an upper half (if there is an odd number of values, leave the median itself out of both halves). The **lower quartile** (Q1) is the median of the lower half, and the **upper quartile** (Q3) is the median of the upper half. The **interquartile range** (IQR) is Q3 − Q1: the width of the middle half of the data.

</Definition>

<Example title="Quartiles of the class heights">

Put the 30 heights in order:

139, 142, 143, 146, 147, 148, 149, 149, 150, 150, 150, 150, 150, 151, 151, | 151, 152, 153, 153, 154, 154, 156, 156, 156, 156, 157, 157, 161, 162, 165

There are 30 values, so the median is halfway between the 15th and 16th, which are both 151: the **median is 151 cm**. The lower half is the first 15 values, and its middle (8th) value is **Q1 = 149 cm**. The upper half is the last 15, and its middle value is **Q3 = 156 cm**. So the **IQR is 156 − 149 = 7 cm**: the middle half of the class lies within a band just 7 cm wide, while the **range** is 165 − 139 = **26 cm**.

The five numbers minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum (here 139, 149, 151, 156, 165) are called the **five-number summary**. They are what a box plot draws, as you will see below.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

There are several slightly different recipes for quartiles, and computers do not all use the one above. NumPy's usual method gives 149.25 cm for Q1 of these heights, not 149. For big data sets the recipes agree closely; for small ones, say which recipe you used.

</Narrator>

The third measure of spread is the one scientists use most.

<Definition term="standard deviation">

The **standard deviation** measures how far values typically are from the mean. To work it out: find the mean; find how far each value is from the mean; square each of those distances; find the mean of the squares; take the square root.

</Definition>

That recipe sounds long, but it is only arithmetic. Here it is for Nomvula's netball points.

| Points | 4 | 5 | 8 | 8 | 10 | 13 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distance from the mean (8) | −4 | −3 | 0 | 0 | 2 | 5 |
| Distance squared | 16 | 9 | 0 | 0 | 4 | 25 |

The squared distances add up to 16 + 9 + 0 + 0 + 4 + 25 = 54, and their mean is 54 ÷ 6 = 9. The square root of 9 is 3, so the **standard deviation is 3 points**. In words: Nomvula's scores are typically about 3 points away from her mean of 8.

Why square the distances and then take a square root at the end? Because the plain distances, −4, −3, 0, 0, 2 and 5, always add up to zero (the mean is exactly the balancing point), so their average tells you nothing. Squaring makes every distance positive, and the square root at the end brings the answer back to the original units, points rather than "points squared".

The standard deviation of the 30 class heights works out at about **5.6 cm**. For data with a single hump in the middle, like these heights, about two thirds of the values usually lie within one standard deviation of the mean. Here the mean is about 151.9 cm, and 23 of the 30 pupils, a little more than three quarters, are between 146.3 and 157.5 cm.

<Note>

Statisticians often use a slightly different version of the standard deviation, which divides by one less than the number of values (54 ÷ 5 instead of 54 ÷ 6). It gives a better estimate of the spread of a whole population when you only have a sample. You will see why in <LessonLink slug="estimation-and-confidence-intervals">Estimation and confidence intervals</LessonLink>. For large data sets the two versions are almost the same.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Summaries and charts in Python">

Python's built-in `statistics` module can find the mean, median and mode for you. Here are the bakery's pay and the holiday books. Press **Run**.

```python run
import statistics

# Monthly pay at a small bakery, in an imaginary currency: eight bakers and the owner.
pay = [300, 310, 320, 320, 340, 350, 380, 400, 2500]
print("Pay:   ", pay)
print("Mean:  ", statistics.mean(pay))
print("Median:", statistics.median(pay))
print("Mode:  ", statistics.mode(pay))
below = sum(1 for p in pay if p < statistics.mean(pay))
print(below, "of", len(pay), "people earn less than the mean")
print()

# Books read over the holidays by 15 pupils.
books = [0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, 10]
print("Books: ", books)
print("Mean:  ", round(statistics.mean(books), 2))
print("Median:", statistics.median(books))
print("Mode:  ", statistics.mode(books))
```

Try changing the owner's 2500 to 25000, and watch which summaries move.

The next program works through the standard deviation step by step, for Nomvula's netball points, and then finds the five-number summary of the class heights. It uses **NumPy**, a Python library for calculating with whole lists of numbers at once: `points - mean` subtracts the mean from every value in one go.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Points scored by a netball player in six matches.
points = np.array([4, 5, 8, 8, 10, 13])
mean = points.mean()
distances = points - mean
print("Points:               ", points.tolist())
print("Mean:                 ", mean)
print("Distance from mean:   ", distances.tolist())
print("Squared distances:    ", (distances ** 2).tolist())
print("Average squared dist.:", (distances ** 2).mean())
print("Standard deviation:   ", np.sqrt((distances ** 2).mean()))
print("NumPy agrees:         ", points.std())
print()

# The 30 heights from the lesson "What is statistics?"
heights = np.array([139, 142, 143, 146, 147, 148, 149, 149, 150, 150, 150, 150, 150, 151, 151,
                    151, 152, 153, 153, 154, 154, 156, 156, 156, 156, 157, 157, 161, 162, 165])
lower, upper = heights[:15], heights[15:]        # the two halves of the sorted data
q1, median, q3 = np.median(lower), np.median(heights), np.median(upper)
print("Five-number summary:", heights.min(), q1, median, q3, heights.max())
print("Range:", heights.max() - heights.min(), " IQR:", q3 - q1)
print("Mean:", round(heights.mean(), 1), " Standard deviation:", round(heights.std(), 1))
print("NumPy's own quartiles:", np.percentile(heights, [25, 75]))
```

Last, the four pictures, drawn with nothing but characters. In the box plot, `|` marks the smallest and largest values, `[` and `]` the quartiles, `M` the median, and each character is one centimetre.

```python run
import numpy as np

# 1. A bar chart: favourite fruit in a class of 30 (categories).
fruit = {"mango": 9, "banana": 7, "apple": 5, "lychee": 4, "orange": 3, "papaya": 2}
for name, count in fruit.items():
    print(f"{name:>7} | {'#' * count} {count}")
print()

# 2. A histogram: the 30 heights, grouped in 5 cm bins (numbers).
heights = np.array([139, 142, 143, 146, 147, 148, 149, 149, 150, 150, 150, 150, 150, 151, 151,
                    151, 152, 153, 153, 154, 154, 156, 156, 156, 156, 157, 157, 161, 162, 165])
counts, edges = np.histogram(heights, bins=range(135, 175, 5))
for count, left in zip(counts, edges):
    print(f"{int(left)}-{int(left) + 4} | {'#' * int(count)}")
print()

# 3. A box plot of the same heights, on a scale from 135 to 170 cm (one character per cm).
lo, q1, med, q3, hi = 139, 149, 151, 156, 165
line = [" "] * 36
for cm in range(lo, hi + 1):
    line[cm - 135] = "=" if q1 <= cm <= q3 else "-"
line[lo - 135], line[hi - 135] = "|", "|"
line[q1 - 135], line[q3 - 135] = "[", "]"
line[med - 135] = "M"
print("".join(line))
print("135       145       155       165")
print()

# 4. A scatter plot: height and arm span of 15 pupils (two numbers for each pupil).
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
height = rng.integers(140, 166, size=15)
span = height + rng.integers(-4, 5, size=15)
for top in range(168, 136, -4):                       # arm span, from the top down
    cells = [" "] * 28
    for h, s in zip(height, span):
        if top - 4 < s <= top:
            cells[int(h) - 140] = "o"
    print(f"{top:4} | " + "".join(cells))
print("     +" + "-" * 29)
print("       140       150       160      height (cm)")
```

The ASCII scatter plot is rougher than the drawn one, because each row lumps together arm spans 4 cm apart, but the rising pattern is clear. Professional statisticians draw charts with plotting libraries such as Matplotlib in Python or ggplot2 in R, but the questions are the same: what kind of data is this, and which picture shows its shape honestly? <Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The average is always the mean."** In everyday speech "average" usually means the mean, but the median and the mode are averages too. When a news report says "the average house price", ask which one. For house prices and incomes, the median is usually more informative.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A histogram is just a bar chart."** They look alike, but a bar chart shows categories (with gaps), while a histogram shows a number line cut into bins (with no gaps). In a histogram, the order of the bars matters and cannot be shuffled; in a bar chart of favourite fruit you could put the bars in any order.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If two data sets have the same mean, they are basically the same."** Wanjiru's two bus routes had the same mean and completely different spreads. Always describe the spread, and always draw a picture.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"An outlier is a mistake, so delete it."** Sometimes an extreme value is a typing error (a height of 1,510 cm instead of 151 cm), and then it should be corrected. But often it is real: the owner really does earn 2,500. Deleting real values to make the data look tidy hides the truth. Check it, and if it is real, keep it and choose summaries, like the median, that are not thrown off by it.

</Misconception>
