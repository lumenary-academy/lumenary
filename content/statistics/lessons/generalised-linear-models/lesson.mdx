<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="When the response is yes, no, or how many">

Wanjiru is an ecologist surveying 200 ponds in the highlands of Kenya for a species of frog. For each pond she records one thing about the frogs, *present or absent*, and two things about the pond: its area, and whether predatory fish live in it. A few hundred kilometres away, a road-safety engineer in Nairobi is studying 80 junctions: for each, *how many* collisions happened last year, how much traffic passed through, and whether the junction is a roundabout. (Both datasets in this lesson are synthetic, generated by programs that mimic real surveys.)

Both want what regression offers: how does the response change with the predictors, and how sure are we? But neither response is a continuous measurement with constant scatter around a line.

- A yes-or-no outcome has a **probability**, which must stay between 0 and 1. A straight line will eventually leave that range; fitted to Wanjiru's data it predicts a "probability" of −0.25 for small ponds with fish, as we shall see.
- A **count** cannot be negative, and its variance grows with its mean: a junction that averages 2 collisions a year varies by a collision or two, one that averages 20 by four or five. Effects are naturally multiplicative too: a roundabout might cut collisions by a third, whatever the traffic.

**Generalised linear models** (GLMs) keep everything good about linear regression (a linear combination of predictors, coefficients with standard errors, tests of nested models) and change two things: the distribution of the response, and the scale on which the predictors act. John Nelder and Robert Wedderburn, working at Rothamsted Experimental Station in England, unified these models in 1972, showing that one fitting algorithm serves them all. Logistic regression is now the standard tool for binary outcomes in medicine, ecology and the social sciences, and Poisson regression for counts in epidemiology, insurance and traffic safety.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The question has not changed from linear regression: how does the typical response depend on the predictors? What changes is the word "typical". For a pond it is a probability, for a junction an average count, and each has a natural scale, log-odds and log-rate, on which effects add up in straight lines.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Three ingredients">

<Definition term="generalised linear model">

A GLM has three parts:

1. a **random component**: given the predictors, $Y_i$ has a distribution from the exponential family (normal, binomial, Poisson, gamma, …) with mean $\mu_i$;
2. a **linear predictor**: $\eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_{p-1} x_{i,p-1}$, or $\boldsymbol\eta = X\boldsymbol\beta$;
3. a **link function** $g$, smooth and monotone, connecting them: $g(\mu_i) = \eta_i$, so $\mu_i = g^{-1}(\eta_i)$.

</Definition>

The link stretches the restricted range of the mean onto the whole real line, where a linear predictor can roam freely. The common choices:

| Response | Distribution | Variance | Usual link $g(\mu)$ | Model name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Measurement | Normal | $\sigma^2$ | identity, $\mu$ | linear regression |
| Yes/no, or $k$ of $m$ | Binomial | $\mu(1-\mu)$ per trial | logit, $\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}$ | logistic regression |
| Count | Poisson | $\mu$ | log, $\log \mu$ | Poisson regression |
| Positive, skewed (costs, durations) | Gamma | $\phi\mu^2$ | log | gamma regression |

The distributions themselves are described in <LessonLink slug="distributions">distributions</LessonLink> and, with their mathematics, in Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-theory">probability theory</TopicLink>. Each distribution has a **canonical link** that makes the mathematics especially tidy (logit for the binomial, log for the Poisson, identity for the normal), but other links are legitimate. For binary data the **probit** link, the inverse of the standard normal distribution function, gives almost the same fits as the logit and dates back to bioassay in the 1930s; the **complementary log-log** link suits probabilities that approach 1 asymmetrically. Linear regression is the GLM with a normal distribution and the identity link, so everything here generalises the previous two lessons.

For a probability $p$, the **odds** are $p/(1-p)$: a probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 (to 1). The logit is the log of the odds. In logistic regression

$$\log\frac{p_i}{1 - p_i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots, \qquad p_i = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\eta_i}},$$

so increasing $x_j$ by one unit adds $\beta_j$ to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta_j}$, the **odds ratio**. In Poisson regression with the log link, a unit increase in $x_j$ multiplies the expected count by $e^{\beta_j}$, the **rate ratio**.

</Section>

<Section id="fitting" kind="human" title="Fitting by maximum likelihood and Newton's method">

Least squares no longer fits the bill: the variance changes from observation to observation, and the mean is a curve. GLMs are fitted by **maximum likelihood**, choosing the coefficients that make the observed data most probable (the general theory is in <LessonLink slug="likelihood-and-model-selection">likelihood and model selection</LessonLink>). For logistic regression with $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$, the probability of the data is $\prod p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}$, and its logarithm, after a line of algebra, is

$$\ell(\boldsymbol\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big[\, y_i \eta_i - \log(1 + e^{\eta_i}) \,\big], \qquad \eta_i = \mathbf x_i^\top \boldsymbol\beta.$$

<Theorem name="Score, information and Newton's method for logistic regression">

The gradient (score) and Hessian of the logistic log-likelihood are

$$\nabla \ell(\boldsymbol\beta) = X^\top(\mathbf y - \mathbf p), \qquad \nabla^2 \ell(\boldsymbol\beta) = -X^\top W X, \quad W = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1 - p_i)\big).$$

The Hessian is negative definite when $X$ has full column rank, so $\ell$ is strictly concave and has at most one maximum. Newton's method updates

$$\boldsymbol\beta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\beta + (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top (\mathbf y - \mathbf p),$$

and at the maximum, $(X^\top W X)^{-1}$ estimates the covariance matrix of $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since $\frac{d}{d\eta}\log(1 + e^{\eta}) = \frac{e^\eta}{1 + e^\eta} = p$, the derivative of the $i$-th term with respect to $\boldsymbol\beta$ is $(y_i - p_i)\mathbf x_i$; summing gives $X^\top(\mathbf y - \mathbf p)$. Differentiating again, $\frac{dp}{d\eta} = p(1-p)$, so the Hessian is $-\sum p_i(1-p_i)\mathbf x_i \mathbf x_i^\top = -X^\top W X$. For any $\mathbf v \neq \mathbf 0$, $\mathbf v^\top X^\top W X \mathbf v = \sum p_i(1-p_i)(\mathbf x_i^\top \mathbf v)^2 > 0$, because every weight is positive and $X\mathbf v \neq \mathbf 0$ by full rank. Newton's method replaces $\ell$ by its second-order Taylor expansion at the current $\boldsymbol\beta$ and jumps to that quadratic's maximum, which is the update shown. The covariance statement is the general large-sample result that the inverse of the Fisher information, here $X^\top W X$, is the asymptotic covariance of a maximum-likelihood estimator.

</Proof>

The update can be rewritten as a weighted least-squares fit of a "working response" $z_i = \eta_i + (y_i - p_i)/w_i$ with weights $w_i$, so the algorithm is also called **iteratively reweighted least squares** (IRLS). It is how R's `glm` and statsmodels fit every GLM: for any canonical link, the score is $X^\top(\mathbf y - \boldsymbol\mu)$ and the information is $X^\top W X$ with $W$ the variance function evaluated at $\boldsymbol\mu$, so one short loop fits them all. Newton's method converges quadratically near the maximum, roughly doubling the number of correct digits each step; the numerical analysis is in Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.comp.numerical-methods">numerical methods</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="logistic" kind="computation" title="Logistic regression for the frog survey">

The program generates Wanjiru's synthetic survey, fits the logistic regression by Newton's method from scratch, and checks the coefficients with scikit-learn, an independent implementation that uses a different optimiser. Pond area enters as $\log_{10}$ of the area in square metres, centred at 2.5 (about 300 m²), so one unit means a pond **ten times larger**.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

# Synthetic survey of 200 ponds: is a frog species present (1) or not (0)?
rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
n = 200
log_area = rng.normal(2.5, 0.6, n)                 # log10 of pond area in m^2 (roughly 30 to 3000 m^2)
fish = (rng.random(n) < 0.45).astype(float)        # predatory fish present?
eta = -0.5 + 2.0 * (log_area - 2.5) - 1.5 * fish   # the true log-odds in this synthetic world
frog = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-eta))).astype(float)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), log_area - 2.5, fish])

def fit_logistic(X, y, verbose=False):
    """Newton's method on the logistic log-likelihood. Returns coefficients, covariance, log-likelihood."""
    beta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for step in range(50):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ beta))
        loglik = np.sum(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))
        if verbose and step < 5:
            print(f"step {step}: beta = {np.round(beta, 4)}, log-likelihood {loglik:.4f}")
        score = X.T @ (y - p)                          # gradient of the log-likelihood
        info = X.T @ (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None])      # minus the Hessian: X^T W X
        delta = np.linalg.solve(info, score)
        beta = beta + delta
        if np.max(np.abs(delta)) < 1e-10:
            break
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ beta))
    loglik = np.sum(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))
    return beta, np.linalg.inv(X.T @ (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None])), loglik

beta, cov, ll = fit_logistic(X, frog, verbose=True)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov))
sk = LogisticRegression(penalty=None, tol=1e-10, max_iter=1000).fit(X[:, 1:], frog)
print("Newton:      ", np.round(beta, 4))
print("scikit-learn:", np.round(np.r_[sk.intercept_, sk.coef_[0]], 4))
for name, b, s in zip(["log10 area", "fish"], beta[1:], se[1:]):
    print(f"{name:>10}: coefficient {b:+.3f} (SE {s:.3f}), odds ratio {np.exp(b):.2f}, "
          f"95% CI {np.exp(b - 1.96 * s):.2f} to {np.exp(b + 1.96 * s):.2f}")

# Deviances and a likelihood-ratio test for fish
ll_null = fit_logistic(X[:, :1], frog)[2]
ll_nofish = fit_logistic(X[:, :2], frog)[2]
print(f"deviance: null {-2 * ll_null:.1f} on {n - 1} df, model {-2 * ll:.1f} on {n - 3} df")
lr = 2 * (ll - ll_nofish)
print(f"likelihood-ratio test for fish: {lr:.1f} on 1 df, p = {stats.chi2.sf(lr, 1):.1e}")

lpm = np.linalg.lstsq(X, frog, rcond=None)[0]       # a straight line fitted to the 0/1 data, for comparison
print(f"linear probability model: fitted 'probabilities' from {np.min(X @ lpm):.2f} to {np.max(X @ lpm):.2f}")
```

Newton's method starts from $\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf 0$ (every pond 50:50, log-likelihood $200\log\tfrac12 = -138.63$) and has converged to four decimals by step 4. scikit-learn agrees to every digit printed.

Reading the coefficients:

- **Area.** The coefficient 1.673 means that a pond ten times larger has log-odds of frogs 1.673 higher, so its **odds** are multiplied by $e^{1.673} = 5.33$ (95% CI 2.67 to 10.65), with fish status held fixed.
- **Fish.** The odds ratio 0.18 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.39) says that, among ponds of the same size, the odds of frogs where there are predatory fish are about one-fifth of the odds where there are none.
- **Probabilities.** Odds ratios are constant, but changes in probability are not. For a 300 m² pond without fish the model gives $p = 1/(1 + e^{0.1748}) = 0.46$; adding fish gives $1/(1 + e^{0.1748 + 1.704}) = 0.13$, a drop of 32 percentage points. For a pond where frogs are almost certain, or almost absent, the same odds ratio moves the probability far less. To communicate effects, compute predicted probabilities for typical cases.

The **deviance** generalises the residual sum of squares: $D = 2(\ell_{\text{saturated}} - \ell_{\text{model}})$, where the saturated model fits every observation perfectly (for 0/1 data its log-likelihood is 0, so $D = -2\ell$). The null model, with an intercept only, has deviance 257.7; the full model 211.5. For **nested** models, the drop in deviance is the **likelihood-ratio statistic**, approximately $\chi^2$ with as many degrees of freedom as coefficients removed. Dropping fish raises the deviance by 23.1 on 1 degree of freedom, $p = 1.5 \times 10^{-6}$. Unlike the residual sum of squares, a deviance on its own says little about fit for binary data; it is the *differences* between nested models that carry information.

The last line is the reason for all this. A straight line fitted to the 0/1 responses, the "linear probability model", gives fitted values from −0.25 to 0.91: impossible as probabilities at one end. Economists sometimes use it anyway for its simple coefficients, with robust standard errors, when fitted values stay well inside (0, 1); outside that range it misleads.

<Section id="poisson" kind="computation" title="Poisson regression for collision counts">

Now the junctions. The count at junction $i$ is modelled as Poisson with mean $\mu_i$, and the log link makes effects multiplicative:

$$\log \mu_i = \log t_i + \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{roundabout}_i,$$

where $t_i$ is the traffic through the junction, in millions of vehicles. The term $\log t_i$ has no coefficient: it is an **offset**, fixed at 1, so that the model describes the **rate** $\mu_i/t_i$, collisions per million vehicles, as $e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1\text{roundabout}_i}$. Without the offset, busier junctions would look more dangerous simply because more vehicles pass through them. Offsets appear wherever counts come with different exposures: cases per population at risk, claims per policy-year, species per hectare surveyed, defects per square metre of fabric.

The fitting algorithm is the same Newton iteration with $W = \operatorname{diag}(\mu_i)$, since the Poisson variance equals its mean.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Synthetic road data: collisions in one year at 80 junctions, with traffic (millions of vehicles).
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 80
traffic = rng.uniform(0.5, 8, n)
roundabout = (rng.random(n) < 0.4).astype(float)
hidden = rng.gamma(3, 1 / 3, n)                     # unmeasured differences between junctions (mean 1)
collisions = rng.poisson(traffic * np.exp(np.log(1.5) + np.log(0.6) * roundabout) * hidden)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), roundabout])
offset = np.log(traffic)                            # model a rate: collisions per million vehicles

def fit_poisson(X, y, offset):
    """Newton's method (= iteratively reweighted least squares) for Poisson regression with a log link."""
    beta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    beta[0] = np.log(y.sum() / np.exp(offset).sum())   # start at the overall rate
    for _ in range(50):
        mu = np.exp(X @ beta + offset)
        delta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (X * mu[:, None]), X.T @ (y - mu))
        beta = beta + delta
        if np.max(np.abs(delta)) < 1e-10:
            break
    mu = np.exp(X @ beta + offset)
    return beta, np.linalg.inv(X.T @ (X * mu[:, None])), mu

beta, cov, mu = fit_poisson(X, collisions, offset)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov))
with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
    term = np.where(collisions > 0, collisions * np.log(collisions / mu), 0.0)
deviance = 2 * np.sum(term - (collisions - mu))
pearson = np.sum((collisions - mu) ** 2 / mu)
phi = pearson / (n - 2)
print(f"collisions per junction: mean {collisions.mean():.1f}, variance {collisions.var(ddof=1):.1f}")
print(f"base rate: {np.exp(beta[0]):.2f} collisions per million vehicles at ordinary junctions")
print(f"roundabout rate ratio: {np.exp(beta[1]):.2f}")
print(f"residual deviance {deviance:.1f} on {n - 2} df; Pearson dispersion {phi:.2f}")
lo, hi = beta[1] - 1.96 * se[1], beta[1] + 1.96 * se[1]
print(f"Poisson 95% CI for the rate ratio:       {np.exp(lo):.2f} to {np.exp(hi):.2f}")
lo, hi = beta[1] - 1.96 * se[1] * np.sqrt(phi), beta[1] + 1.96 * se[1] * np.sqrt(phi)
print(f"quasi-Poisson 95% CI (SE x sqrt(phi)):  {np.exp(lo):.2f} to {np.exp(hi):.2f}")
```

The fitted base rate is 1.40 collisions per million vehicles at ordinary junctions, and the roundabout rate ratio is 0.66: roundabouts have about a third fewer collisions per vehicle. But one number in the output should stop us: the residual deviance is 221.4 on 78 degrees of freedom, and the Pearson dispersion statistic

$$\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n - p}\sum_i \frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{\hat\mu_i}$$

is 2.91. If the counts really were Poisson, each term would average about 1 and $\hat\phi$ would be close to 1. (The raw variance of 24.5 against a mean of 5.2 is not itself evidence, because junctions differ in traffic; $\hat\phi$ compares each count with its own fitted mean.)

</Section>

<Section id="overdispersion" kind="computation" title="Overdispersion">

Counts that vary more than the Poisson distribution allows are **overdispersed**, and in practice they are the rule, not the exception. Junctions differ in ways the model does not record (sight lines, speed, lighting, pedestrian crossings), so even junctions with the same traffic and design have different true rates. A mixture of Poisson distributions with different rates has variance larger than its mean. In the synthetic data, each junction's rate was multiplied by a hidden factor with mean 1 and variance $1/3$, which gives variance $\mu + \mu^2/3$ instead of $\mu$.

Overdispersion does not bias the coefficients much (the log-linear mean is still right), but it makes the Poisson standard errors too small, and so the intervals too narrow and the $p$-values too small. How much too small? Simulation answers.

```python run
import numpy as np

# How often does a 95% interval for the roundabout effect catch the truth (log 0.6)?
rng = np.random.default_rng(99)
n, true_b1 = 80, np.log(0.6)
traffic = rng.uniform(0.5, 8, n)
roundabout = (rng.random(n) < 0.4).astype(float)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), roundabout])
offset = np.log(traffic)
mu_true = traffic * 1.5 * np.exp(true_b1 * roundabout)

def fit_poisson(X, y, offset):
    beta = np.array([np.log(y.sum() / np.exp(offset).sum()), 0.0])
    for _ in range(50):
        mu = np.exp(X @ beta + offset)
        delta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (X * mu[:, None]), X.T @ (y - mu))
        beta = beta + delta
        if np.max(np.abs(delta)) < 1e-10:
            break
    mu = np.exp(X @ beta + offset)
    se = np.sqrt(np.diag(np.linalg.inv(X.T @ (X * mu[:, None]))))
    phi = np.sum((y - mu) ** 2 / mu) / (len(y) - X.shape[1])
    return beta, se, phi

for label, extra in [("pure Poisson counts", None), ("overdispersed counts", 3.0)]:
    naive = quasi = 0
    phis = []
    for _ in range(2000):
        m = mu_true if extra is None else mu_true * rng.gamma(extra, 1 / extra, n)
        beta, se, phi = fit_poisson(X, rng.poisson(m), offset)
        naive += abs(beta[1] - true_b1) <= 1.96 * se[1]
        quasi += abs(beta[1] - true_b1) <= 1.96 * se[1] * np.sqrt(phi)
        phis.append(phi)
    print(f"{label:>21}: mean dispersion {np.mean(phis):.2f}; coverage Poisson {naive / 2000:.1%}, quasi-Poisson {quasi / 2000:.1%}")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

With genuinely Poisson counts, the Poisson intervals cover the true rate ratio 95.0% of the time. With the overdispersed counts, the same intervals cover it only 76.2% of the time: one "95%" interval in four misses. Multiplying the standard errors by $\sqrt{\hat\phi}$ brings coverage back to 94.2%, and costs nothing when the counts are truly Poisson (94.7%).

</Narrator>

There are three standard remedies.

- **Quasi-Poisson**: keep the Poisson estimates, multiply standard errors by $\sqrt{\hat\phi}$, and use $F$ tests rather than $\chi^2$ tests. Simple and robust; for the junctions, the roundabout interval widens from 0.54–0.81 to 0.47–0.94.
- **Negative binomial regression**: model the extra variation explicitly, with variance $\mu + \mu^2/k$, as a Poisson whose rate has a gamma distribution, which is exactly how the synthetic data were made. It has a full likelihood, so AIC and likelihood-ratio tests remain available.
- **Random effects**: give each junction (or each group of observations) its own random term, a Poisson generalised linear *mixed* model, which also handles repeated counts at the same junctions over years. That is the territory of <LessonLink slug="multilevel-models">multilevel models</LessonLink>.

Binomial data with more than one trial per observation (such as 7 of 20 seeds germinating per tray) can be overdispersed in the same way, and the same remedies apply. A related problem is an **excess of zeros**: many sites with no collisions at all because some are almost never used. Zero-inflated and hurdle models handle this, but check first whether a negative binomial already fits.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view">

In R, `glm(frog ~ log_area + fish, family = binomial, data = ponds)` fits the logistic regression, and `glm(collisions ~ roundabout + offset(log(traffic)), family = quasipoisson, data = junctions)` the quasi-Poisson model; `MASS::glm.nb` fits negative binomial regression. In Python, [statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/glm.html) provides the same families and links. Shown for reference, not run here:

```python

import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

print(fit.summary())                  # coefficients, standard errors, deviance

                 offset=np.log(junctions["traffic"])).fit(scale="X2")   # quasi-Poisson scale
```

Points that matter in practice:

- **scikit-learn penalises by default.** Its `LogisticRegression` adds an L2 penalty unless told otherwise (`C=1.0` by default), which shrinks coefficients towards zero. That is a sensible choice for prediction with many features, but its coefficients are then not the maximum-likelihood estimates, and it reports no standard errors. The program above passed `penalty=None` to make the comparison fair.
- **Odds ratios are not risk ratios.** When the outcome is rare (under about 10%), the odds ratio approximates the ratio of probabilities; when it is common, the odds ratio is further from 1. Medical journals increasingly ask for risk differences and risk ratios, which can be computed from a fitted logistic model as averages of predicted probabilities over the sample.
- **Case-control studies.** In medicine, a study may sample people *with* a disease and people *without* it, fixing the outcome proportions by design. Remarkably, logistic regression still estimates the odds ratios of the exposures correctly (only the intercept is distorted), which is a large part of why it dominates epidemiology.
- **Beyond straight lines.** Generalised additive models replace linear terms by smooth curves; GLMs with random effects handle grouped data; and the same framework, with a different loss, is the output layer of most neural-network classifiers, where the logistic function appears as the sigmoid. See Lumenary CS's [learning from data](/en/cs/learning-from-data/).
- **Calibration and fairness.** When logistic models score people, for credit, hiring or clinical risk, check that predicted probabilities match observed frequencies in each group the model affects, and that the predictors used are lawful and justifiable. A well-fitting model can still encode an unjust history.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A logistic coefficient of 1.67 means the probability rises by 1.67, or by 167%."** The coefficient is a change in **log-odds**. It multiplies the odds by $e^{1.67} \approx 5.3$, and the change in probability depends on where you start: largest near $p = 0.5$, small near 0 or 1.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"An odds ratio of 0.18 means the event is 82% less likely."** That would be a risk ratio. Odds ratios and risk ratios agree only when the event is rare. In the pond example, fish took a 300 m² pond's probability of frogs from 0.46 to 0.13, a risk ratio of about 0.29, not 0.18.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Poisson regression is fine as long as the data are counts."** The Poisson model also asserts that the variance equals the mean, given the predictors. Real counts are usually overdispersed, and ignoring it makes intervals too narrow. Always compute the dispersion statistic.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A large residual deviance means a poor model, and the deviance is like $1 - R^2$."** For binary responses the residual deviance cannot measure absolute fit, because the saturated model is degenerate. Compare nested models by differences in deviance, and judge fit with residual plots grouped by fitted probability or with calibration checks.

</Misconception>
