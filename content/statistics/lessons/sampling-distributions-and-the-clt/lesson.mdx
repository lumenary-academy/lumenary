<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Another sample, another answer">

A transport researcher in Jakarta wants to know how long the city's workers spend commuting. She cannot ask all of them, so she surveys a random sample of 50 and finds a mean one-way commute of 42.5 minutes. A colleague runs the same survey the following week with a different random 50 and gets 54.1. Neither of them made a mistake. Different samples contain different people, so they give different answers.

This is the central fact of statistical inference: **a statistic computed from a sample is itself a random quantity.** Had chance picked a different sample, the statistic would have been different. Before we can say what a sample tells us about the population (the subject of every remaining lesson in this level), we need to know how much a statistic varies from one possible sample to another. Is the answer from 50 people likely to be within 1 minute of the truth, or within 10?

In <LessonLink slug="samples-and-surveys">Samples and surveys</LessonLink> you saw that random sampling avoids bias. This lesson asks the next question: *how far from the truth can a random sample's answer wander by chance alone?* The answer has two parts. The **standard error** measures the size of the wandering, and it shrinks in proportion to $1/\sqrt{n}$. The **central limit theorem** describes its shape, which for averages of many independent pieces is close to normal, whatever the shape of the population. Both can be proved, and both can be watched happening in a simulation, which is what we will do.

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Three distributions you must not confuse">

Three different distributions are in play whenever we sample, and most confusion in statistics comes from mixing them up.

1. **The population distribution**: how the quantity varies across everyone in the population. Commute times in our synthetic Jakarta population range from a few minutes to over two hours, with mean $\mu = 48$ minutes and standard deviation $\sigma = 24$ minutes. It is skewed to the right: a long tail of very long commutes.
2. **The sample distribution**: how the quantity varies across the 50 people we actually asked. It is a rough, noisy picture of the population distribution, and it is the only one of the three we ever see.
3. **The sampling distribution** of a statistic: how the *statistic* (here, the sample mean) would vary across all the samples of 50 we *could* have drawn. It is a thought experiment: imagine repeating the survey thousands of times and making a histogram of the thousands of means.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="parameter and statistic">

A **parameter** is a number describing the population, such as the population mean $\mu$ or standard deviation $\sigma$. It is fixed but usually unknown. A **statistic** is a number computed from a sample, such as the sample mean $\bar X = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ or the sample standard deviation $s$. It is known once we have the data, and it varies from sample to sample.

</Definition>

<Definition term="sampling distribution">

The **sampling distribution** of a statistic is its probability distribution over repeated random samples of the same size, drawn in the same way from the same population.

</Definition>

<Definition term="standard error">

The **standard error** (SE) of a statistic is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution. Since $\sigma$ is usually unknown, we estimate the standard error of a mean by $s/\sqrt{n}$, the **estimated standard error**, and software usually reports this simply as "the standard error".

</Definition>

The basic facts about the sample mean follow from the rules for expectation and variance in <LessonLink slug="distributions">Distributions</LessonLink>.

<Theorem name="Mean and standard error of the sample mean">

If $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are independent, each with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, then
$$E[\bar X] = \mu \qquad \text{and} \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By linearity of expectation, $E[\bar X] = \frac1n \sum_i E[X_i] = \frac1n \cdot n\mu = \mu$. Because the $X_i$ are independent, their variances add: $\operatorname{Var}\big(\sum_i X_i\big) = n\sigma^2$. Scaling by $\frac1n$ multiplies the variance by $\frac1{n^2}$, so $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$, and the standard deviation is $\sigma/\sqrt n$.

</Proof>

Two consequences shape all of applied statistics. First, the sample mean is right **on average**: it has no systematic tendency to over- or underestimate. Second, its precision improves only with the **square root** of the sample size. To halve the standard error you need four times as many observations; to gain one more decimal place of precision, a hundred times as many. Precision is expensive.

Notice also what the formula does *not* contain: the size of the population. When the population is much larger than the sample, sampling without replacement is practically the same as sampling independently, and a sample of 1,000 is as precise for a country of 400,000 as for a country of 1.4 billion. (The exact correction factor, $\sqrt{(N - n)/(N - 1)}$, is about 0.9987 for $n = 1000$ and $N = 390\,000$.)

</Section>

<Section id="simulation" kind="computation" title="A survey, ten thousand times">

We cannot run the Jakarta survey ten thousand times, but we can build a synthetic population and survey *it* as often as we like. Here the population is a million commute times drawn from a right-skewed gamma distribution, and each survey samples 50, 200 or 800 of them without replacement.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# A synthetic population: one-way commute times (minutes) of 1,000,000 workers. Right-skewed.
population = rng.gamma(shape=4.0, scale=12.0, size=1_000_000)
mu, sigma = population.mean(), population.std()
print(f"population: mean {mu:.2f} min, sd {sigma:.2f} min")

# Five surveys of 50 workers each give five different sample means.
for i in range(5):
    sample = rng.choice(population, size=50, replace=False)
    print(f"survey {i + 1}: sample mean {sample.mean():.2f}")

# Ten thousand surveys at each sample size: how much does the sample mean vary?
print("\n   n   sd of 10,000 sample means   sigma/sqrt(n)")
for n in [10, 50, 200, 800]:
    means = np.array([rng.choice(population, size=n, replace=False).mean() for _ in range(10_000)])
    print(f"{n:4} {means.std():18.2f} {sigma / np.sqrt(n):18.2f}")

def text_histogram(values, lo, hi, bins=12, width=40):
    counts, edges = np.histogram(values, bins=bins, range=(lo, hi))
    for c, left in zip(counts, edges):
        print(f"{left:6.0f} " + "#" * round(width * c / counts.max()))

print("\nthe population (one bar per 10 minutes):")
text_histogram(population, 0, 120)
means50 = np.array([rng.choice(population, size=50, replace=False).mean() for _ in range(10_000)])
print("\n10,000 sample means for n = 50, on the same scale:")
text_histogram(means50, 0, 120)
print("\nthe same 10,000 sample means, zoomed in (one bar per 2 minutes):")
text_histogram(means50, 36, 60)
print(f"mean of the sample means: {means50.mean():.2f}")
```

Five surveys of 50 give five different means, scattered across about 12 minutes. Across ten thousand surveys, the standard deviation of the sample means matches $\sigma/\sqrt n$ at every sample size: 7.56 against 7.59 for $n = 10$, and 0.86 against 0.85 for $n = 800$. The text histograms reproduce the figure. On the population's scale, the 10,000 means are crammed into two bars; zoomed in, they form a nearly symmetric bell around 48, even though the population itself is skewed.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Read the table along the rows: each fourfold increase in $n$ halves the spread, from 7.6 to 3.4 to 1.7 to 0.85 minutes. So the survey of 50 is typically off by about 3.4 minutes, and to be typically off by less than one minute the researcher needs about $(24/1)^2 = 576$ workers.

<Section id="clt" kind="human" title="The central limit theorem">

The standard error tells us the size of the sampling distribution. Its shape is the business of one of the most remarkable results in mathematics.

<Theorem name="Central limit theorem">

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be independent and identically distributed with mean $\mu$ and finite standard deviation $\sigma > 0$. Then for every real $z$,
$$P\!\left(\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \le z\right) \longrightarrow \Phi(z) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,$$
where $\Phi$ is the cdf of the standard normal distribution.

</Theorem>

In words: the standardised sample mean has approximately a standard normal distribution when $n$ is large, *whatever the shape of the population*, provided its variance is finite. The proof uses characteristic functions and is given in Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-theory">Probability theory</TopicLink>; here we watch it work.

Because a sum is just $n$ times a mean, the theorem also says that sums of many independent pieces are approximately normal. That is the story of the normal distribution in the previous lesson. And because a proportion is the mean of 0-or-1 variables, sample proportions are approximately normal too, with standard error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$.

### Where the theorem is slow

The theorem is a statement about a limit. It does not say how large $n$ must be, and the answer depends on the population. A useful measure is the population's **skewness** $\gamma$, its average cubed standardised distance from the mean. The skewness of the sample mean is $\gamma/\sqrt n$, so a population with skewness 2 (the exponential) gives a mean with skewness 0.37 at $n = 30$, while a population with skewness 33 needs $n \approx 8400$ before its mean is as symmetric as that. The Berry–Esseen theorem makes this precise: the largest gap between the cdf of the standardised mean and $\Phi$ is at most a constant (known to be below one half) times $E|X - \mu|^3 / (\sigma^3 \sqrt n)$.

Simulation shows what this means in practice. For each population below we draw 10,000 samples, and count how often the sample mean lands more than 1.96 standard errors below or above $\mu$. If the sampling distribution were exactly normal, each tail would hold 2.5%.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

# Three populations with known mean and standard deviation, from mildly to wildly skewed.
populations = {
    "exponential": (lambda size: rng.exponential(1.0, size), 1.0, 1.0),
    "lognormal(sigma=1.5)": (lambda size: rng.lognormal(0.0, 1.5, size),
                             np.exp(1.125), np.sqrt((np.exp(2.25) - 1) * np.exp(2.25))),
    "Pareto(alpha=2.5)": (lambda size: rng.pareto(2.5, size) + 1.0,
                          2.5 / 1.5, np.sqrt(2.5 / (1.5**2 * 0.5))),
}

def sample_means(draw, n, reps=10_000, chunk=1_000):
    """reps sample means of samples of size n, a chunk at a time to save memory."""
    return np.concatenate([draw((chunk, n)).mean(axis=1) for _ in range(reps // chunk)])

print("If the sample mean were normal, the columns would read 0.025, 0.025 and 0.050.")
print(f"{'population':21} {'n':>5} {'below mu-1.96SE':>16} {'above mu+1.96SE':>16} {'total':>6}")
for name, (draw, mu, sigma) in populations.items():
    for n in [5, 30, 200, 1000]:
        m = sample_means(draw, n)
        se = sigma / np.sqrt(n)
        below, above = np.mean(m < mu - 1.96 * se), np.mean(m > mu + 1.96 * se)
        print(f"{name:21} {n:5} {below:16.3f} {above:16.3f} {below + above:6.3f}")
```

The exponential population behaves well: by $n = 200$ the tails are 2.0% and 3.0%, and by $n = 1000$ they are essentially exact. The lognormal with $\sigma = 1.5$ (skewness about 33, typical of incomes or insurance claims) is another story. Even at $n = 1000$ the lower tail holds 0.9% and the upper tail 4.0%: the sample mean is still clearly skewed. The **total** outside $\pm 1.96$ SE is close to 5% at $n = 1000$, which can hide the problem, but anyone making a one-sided claim would be wrong by a factor of about two. The Pareto population, whose third moment is infinite, converges more slowly still. Its sample mean falls a little *below* $\mu$ more often than not, because the rare enormous values that hold up the average are usually missing from a sample, and occasionally it overshoots by a lot.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The theorem's hypothesis "finite variance" is not a technicality. For a population with infinite variance, such as a Pareto with $\alpha < 2$, the standardised mean does not approach a normal distribution at all; it approaches a different, heavy-tailed **stable** law. And the rule "$n \ge 30$ is enough" has no theorem behind it: it is a rough guide for populations that are not very skewed, and the lognormal above shows it failing at $n = 1000$.

</Narrator>

The practical advice is simple. Look at the data, and if they are strongly skewed or have a few extreme values, check the normal approximation by simulation (as above) or use methods that do not depend on it, such as the bootstrap of <LessonLink slug="estimation-and-confidence-intervals">the next lesson</LessonLink> or a transformation such as taking logarithms.

<Section id="lln" kind="human" title="The law of large numbers">

The central limit theorem describes the *fluctuations* of the sample mean around $\mu$. An older and simpler result says that those fluctuations die away.

<Theorem name="Weak law of large numbers">

If $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ are independent with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, then for every $\varepsilon > 0$,
$$P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \longrightarrow 0.$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Chebyshev's inequality says that any random variable $Y$ with mean $m$ and variance $v$ satisfies $P(|Y - m| \ge \varepsilon) \le v/\varepsilon^2$. Apply it to $Y = \bar X_n$, which has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2/n$.

</Proof>

The law of large numbers is why casinos and insurers can plan: individual outcomes are unpredictable, but averages over many independent outcomes are not. It is also why simulation works: the frequency of an event in many simulated repetitions converges to its probability. Jacob Bernoulli proved the first version for coin-like trials in his *Ars Conjectandi*, published in 1713, eight years after his death.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
N = 1_000_000
checkpoints = [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]

# Running averages of die rolls (mean 3.5) and of Cauchy draws (no mean at all).
die = rng.integers(1, 7, N)
cauchy = rng.standard_cauchy(N)
steps = np.arange(1, N + 1)
die_avg = np.cumsum(die) / steps
cauchy_avg = np.cumsum(cauchy) / steps

print(f"{'rolls':>9} {'average of die':>15} {'distance from 3.5':>18} {'average of Cauchy':>18}")
for n in checkpoints:
    print(f"{n:9} {die_avg[n - 1]:15.4f} {abs(die_avg[n - 1] - 3.5):18.4f} {cauchy_avg[n - 1]:18.4f}")

# The law of large numbers works by swamping, not by compensating.
sixes = np.cumsum(die == 6)
for n in [600, 6_000, 60_000, 600_000]:
    excess = sixes[n - 1] - n / 6
    print(f"after {n:7} rolls: {sixes[n - 1]:6} sixes, {excess:+7.0f} from n/6, share {sixes[n - 1] / n:.4f}")
```

The average of the die rolls closes in on 3.5: off by 0.26 after 100 rolls, by 0.0011 after a million. The Cauchy distribution, whose tails are so heavy that it has no mean at all, never settles: after a million draws its running average is still jumping between values like $-3$ and $+3$, because every so often a single draw is large enough to drag the whole average.

The second table answers a common misunderstanding. After 600,000 rolls the number of sixes is 336 *below* $n/6$, a larger shortfall than after 600 rolls, and yet the proportion of sixes, 0.1661, is closer to $1/6$. The law of large numbers does not work by the dice "making up" for a shortage of sixes. Dice have no memory. It works by **swamping**: the early deviation stays roughly where it was while the number of rolls grows, so its effect on the proportion shrinks.

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="Standard errors in professional practice">

**Every serious estimate comes with a standard error.** National statistics offices publish them beside their estimates. The United States Census Bureau's [American Community Survey](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs), for example, prints a margin of error next to each figure, and the UK's Office for National Statistics and Statistics Canada publish confidence intervals for their survey estimates. An opinion poll's "margin of error of 3 percentage points" is roughly two standard errors of a proportion near 50% from about 1,000 respondents: $1.96\sqrt{0.25/1000} \approx 0.031$.

**Real surveys are not simple random samples.** Household surveys usually sample villages or city blocks first and then households within them (cluster sampling), because visiting a thousand scattered addresses is expensive. People in the same cluster resemble each other, so the sample carries less independent information than its size suggests. Survey statisticians measure this with the **design effect**, the ratio of the actual variance to the variance a simple random sample of the same size would give; in household surveys, values of 1.5 or more are common. Using $s/\sqrt n$ for a clustered sample understates the uncertainty.

**Dependence is the assumption that most often fails.** Daily measurements from one sensor, repeated tests on the same patients, and pupils in the same school are not independent. Positive correlation between observations makes the true standard error larger than $s/\sqrt n$, sometimes by a lot. Later lessons on <LessonLink slug="time-series">time series</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="multilevel-models">multilevel models</LessonLink> deal with this properly.

**Other statistics have sampling distributions too.** The median, a correlation, a regression slope and a machine-learning model's accuracy on a test set all vary from sample to sample. Some have formulas for their standard errors (for normal data, the median's is about $1.25\,\sigma/\sqrt n$); for others the standard error is found by resampling the data, the bootstrap idea introduced in the next lesson and developed in <LessonLink slug="resampling-and-simulation">Resampling and simulation</LessonLink>.

**History.** Abraham de Moivre found the normal approximation to the binomial in 1733; Pierre-Simon Laplace extended it to sums of general independent variables around 1810; Aleksandr Lyapunov gave the first rigorous general proof in 1901; and George Pólya named it the *central* limit theorem in 1920, because of its central place in probability. Francis Galton's "quincunx", a board on which balls bounce left or right off rows of pins and pile up into a bell shape, made the idea visible in the 1870s, and versions of it are still found in science museums.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"With a large enough sample, the data become normal."** The data have whatever distribution the population has; a larger sample just shows that shape more clearly. The central limit theorem is about the distribution of the *mean* (and other averages) across repeated samples.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"n ≥ 30 is always enough for the normal approximation."** It depends on the population. For mildly skewed data 30 is often fine; for heavily skewed data such as incomes or claim sizes, the lognormal simulation above shows clear skewness in the mean at $n = 1000$. For infinite-variance populations, no $n$ is enough.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The standard deviation and the standard error are the same thing."** The standard deviation describes how individuals vary ($\sigma = 24$ minutes of commute); it does not shrink as you collect more data. The standard error describes how the *estimate* varies ($24/\sqrt{50} \approx 3.4$ minutes for a sample of 50), and it shrinks as $n$ grows. Reporting one in place of the other makes results look either far more or far less precise than they are.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"After a run of reds at roulette, black is due."** This is the gambler's fallacy, a misreading of the law of large numbers. The wheel has no memory; the proportion of reds settles down because later spins swamp the early run, not because later spins compensate for it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bigger population needs a bigger sample."** For a population much larger than the sample, precision depends on the sample size $n$, not on the population size $N$. A well-drawn random sample of 1,000 is about as precise in Iceland as in India. What does need a bigger sample is wanting precise results for *subgroups*: each region or age group needs its own adequate $n$.

</Misconception>
