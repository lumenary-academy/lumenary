<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Three puzzles from the kitchen drawer">

**A paper clip bends.** Straighten a steel paper clip and it stays straight. It does not spring back, and it does not snap. Yet a ceramic plate, which is much harder than the steel of a paper clip, cracks into pieces instead of bending even a little.

**A rubber band stretches.** You can pull a rubber band to several times its length, and when you let go it snaps straight back. Try that with a strip of steel, or of plastic food wrap, and it either barely stretches or stretches and stays long.

**A wire conducts.** Electricity flows easily through the copper inside a cable, but not through the plastic coating around it, though the two are touching all along their length.

In <LessonLink slug="materials-around-us">Materials around us</LessonLink> you met these behaviours from the outside, as properties. In this lesson you will look inside, at the atoms, and see that each puzzle has the same kind of answer.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here's the big idea of the whole lesson, and in a way of all of materials science: a material's properties come from two things, **how its atoms are held together** and **how they are arranged**. Change either one and you change the material. Watch for those two things in every section.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="springs" kind="intuition" title="Atoms held together by springs">

Everything is made of atoms, so small that a line of four million of them would only just stretch across a millimetre. (You can meet them properly in Lumenary Chemistry's [atoms and elements](/en/chemistry/atoms-and-elements/).) In a solid, the atoms sit close together, held by **bonds**: electric attractions between the atoms' positive centres and their negative electrons.

A bond behaves remarkably like a spring. Push two atoms too close and they push each other apart. Pull them too far and they pull back together. In between there is one comfortable distance where the push and the pull balance, and that is where the atoms sit.

This simple picture already explains several things.

- **Stiffness.** When you bend a steel ruler, you are stretching some of its bonds and squashing others, very slightly. The bonds pull back, and the ruler springs straight when you let go. Materials with stiff bonds, such as diamond and most ceramics, are stiff; materials with soft bonds are floppy.
- **Expansion.** The atoms are never still: they jiggle about their comfortable positions, and the hotter the material, the harder they jiggle. Jiggling harder pushes them, on average, slightly further apart, which is why things expand when heated and why bridges have expansion gaps.
- **Melting.** Heat a solid enough and the jiggling becomes so violent that the atoms break free of their fixed places: the solid melts. Strong bonds mean a high melting point.

Not all bonds are alike. Lumenary Chemistry's [molecules and compounds](/en/chemistry/molecules-and-compounds/) introduces the three strong kinds; materials scientists add a fourth, much weaker kind, which turns out to matter a great deal for plastics.

| Kind of bond | What the electrons do | Strength | Found in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **metallic** | the outer electrons leave their atoms and are shared by the whole piece of metal, a "sea" of electrons | strong | metals and alloys |
| **ionic** | electrons move from one kind of atom to another, leaving positive and negative ions that attract | strong | many ceramics, such as alumina and magnesia, and table salt |
| **covalent** | neighbouring atoms share pairs of electrons between them | very strong, and pointing in fixed directions | diamond, silicon, quartz, and the chains inside polymers |
| **weak attractions between molecules** | no electrons are shared; whole molecules attract each other slightly | weak: ten to a hundred times weaker | between the chains of a polymer, between the molecules of wax and ice |

</Section>

<Section id="metals" kind="examples" title="Metals: layers that slide">

In a metal, every atom has given up one or more of its outer electrons to the shared sea. What is left are positive **ions**, packed in neat layers, all alike, held together by the sea of electrons around them.

Now imagine pushing hard enough to slide one layer of ions over the next. In a metal, after the layer has moved one step, the pattern looks exactly as it did before: every ion still has neighbours in the same places, and the electron sea still holds everything together. Nothing has broken. The metal has simply changed shape, and it stays in its new shape. That is why a paper clip stays bent, and why metals can be hammered, rolled into foil and drawn into wire.

Real metals are cleverer still. A whole layer does not slide at once, which would need an enormous force. Instead, a small fault in the stacking of the atoms, called a **dislocation**, moves through the crystal, letting the layer slide one row of atoms at a time.

<Analogy>

Try moving a big rug across a floor by dragging one edge: it is hard work. Now push a small ruck into one end and roll the ruck along to the other end. The rug moves by the same amount, but you only ever had to lift a little of it at a time. A dislocation is a ruck in a layer of atoms.

</Analogy>

The electron sea explains the other metal puzzles too. Because the electrons are free to move through the whole metal, a push from a battery sets them flowing: an **electric current**. The same free electrons carry heat quickly from the hot end to the cold end, and they bounce light back, which is why polished metals are shiny. Plastic, ceramics and wood have no free electrons, so they are insulators: the copper in a cable carries the current, and the plastic around it keeps it where it belongs. (Lumenary EE's [what is electricity?](/en/ee/what-is-electricity/) follows the electrons further.)

**Try it, with an adult.** Unbend a steel paper clip: it stays bent, because layers of atoms have slid. Then bend one leg back and forth at the same spot. After a few bends it gets harder to bend, the bend feels warm, and after a while it snaps. Each bend makes more dislocations, which tangle and jam, so the metal hardens; then tiny cracks start at the bend and grow until it breaks. The broken ends are sharp, so take care. You will meet this hardening, and the slow cracking called fatigue, in <LessonLink slug="elasticity-plasticity-and-strengthening">Elasticity, plasticity and strengthening</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="fatigue-creep-and-corrosion">Fatigue, creep and corrosion</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="ceramics" kind="examples" title="Ceramics: strong bonds with no give">

Ceramics are held together by ionic and covalent bonds, which are strong and do not let the atoms move easily. That gives ceramics their good points. They are **hard**, because their atoms cannot be pushed aside to make a dent. They are **stiff**, because their bonds are stiff springs. They have **high melting points**: alumina, the ceramic in spark plugs and some hip replacements, melts at about 2072 °C, more than 500 °C hotter than iron. And because their electrons are locked in bonds, most ceramics are excellent **electrical insulators**, which is why the insulators that hold up power lines are often made of porcelain or glass.

The same strong bonds are also their weakness. Look at the right of the figure above. In an ionic ceramic, positive and negative ions alternate, each surrounded by ions of the opposite charge. Slide one layer a single step and positive ions land next to positive ions, negative next to negative. Like charges push each other apart, so instead of sliding, the layers separate: the material cracks. In covalent ceramics the bonds point in fixed directions, and bending them out of line simply snaps them. Either way, a ceramic cannot get out of trouble by changing shape the way a metal does. It is **brittle**.

Same atoms, different bonds, different material: carbon shows this beautifully. In **diamond**, every carbon atom is joined by covalent bonds to four neighbours in a three-dimensional network, making diamond the hardest natural material. In **graphite**, the carbon atoms are joined strongly in flat sheets, but the sheets are held to each other only by weak attractions, so they slide off one another. That is why graphite is soft and slippery, and why a pencil leaves a trail of sheets on the paper. In 2004, scientists in Manchester peeled graphite down to a single sheet one atom thick, called graphene, a discovery that won the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Here's how a brittle material actually fails. A tiny scratch or crack in a ceramic is like a gap in a chain: the bonds at the very tip of the crack must carry the load that the missing bonds would have shared. They break, the crack grows, and the next bonds are overloaded in turn. In a metal, layers slide at the crack tip and blunt it; in a ceramic nothing can slide, so one small flaw can run right through.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="polymers" kind="examples" title="Polymers and rubber: long, tangled chains">

A polymer is made of enormously long molecules: chains of carbon atoms, often many thousands of atoms long, joined by strong covalent bonds along the chain. A heap of polymer chains is like a bowl of cooked spaghetti, tangled together. Along each strand the bonds are strong, but between strands there are only the weak attractions between molecules.

That explains a lot about plastics. They are **light**, because they are made of light atoms, mostly carbon and hydrogen. They are **less stiff** than metals, because bending a plastic mostly pulls chains past each other against weak attractions instead of stretching strong bonds. And many plastics **soften when heated**: warm the chains and they can slide past one another, which is why a plastic bottle can be moulded and why polyethylene melts at only about 110 to 135 °C, depending on the type.

Rubber is a polymer with one extra trick. Its chains are naturally coiled and crumpled, and here and there neighbouring chains are tied together by a few strong bonds called **cross-links**. When you stretch a rubber band, you are not stretching the bonds; you are pulling the crumpled chains straight. The cross-links stop the chains from sliding past each other, so they cannot flow away.

Why does it spring back? Because the chains never stop jiggling, and there are vastly more crumpled shapes for a chain than straight ones, just as there are vastly more ways for a pile of spaghetti to be tangled than lined up. Jiggled at random, a stretched chain almost always drifts back to being crumpled, and the rubber band shortens. In 1839 the American inventor Charles Goodyear found how to add cross-links to natural rubber by heating it with sulfur, a process called **vulcanisation**; without cross-links, rubber turns sticky when warm and the chains slowly slide apart.

**Try it, with an adult.** Take a wide rubber band, hold it against your forehead, well away from your eyes, and stretch it quickly. It feels slightly warm. Hold it stretched for a few seconds, then let it relax quickly: it feels slightly cool. Stretching the chains straight releases a little heat, and crumpling them up again takes a little back. Never stretch a rubber band towards anyone's face.

Quartz and window glass make a neat pair. Quartz is a crystal of silicon and oxygen. Because a glass has no pattern to break, it has no sharp melting point: heated, it slowly softens, like toffee. Glassblowers in Murano, Hebron and Firozabad have used that gentle softening for centuries to shape glass while it is hot and gooey.

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="A chain of atoms on springs">

Let's build the spring picture as a program. The model is a row of six atoms, `o`, joined by five springs, `-----`. Pulling on the end of the row stretches every spring, and each spring stretches by the pull divided by its stiffness. The model uses "model units", chosen so that the stretching is big enough to see in a line of text. Press **Run**:

```python run
# A toy model of a solid: a row of atoms (o) joined by springs (the bonds).
# Everything is in "model units", chosen so that the stretching is big
# enough to see. Real bonds stretch far less before anything gives way.
import numpy as np

SPACING = 6    # the gap between neighbouring atoms when nothing pulls

def gaps_when_pulled(pull, stiffness):
    """Every bond in the row carries the same pull, so bond number i
    stretches by pull / stiffness[i]. Return the gap after each atom."""
    return SPACING + pull / stiffness

def draw(gaps, broken=None):
    picture = "o"
    for i, gap in enumerate(gaps):
        link = " " if i == broken else "-"
        picture += link * (round(gap) - 1) + "o"
    return picture

stiff = np.full(5, 2.0)   # stiff bonds: a pull of 2 stretches each one by 1
soft = np.full(5, 0.5)    # soft bonds: a pull of 0.5 stretches each one by 1

print("1. Six atoms and five bonds, pulled at the ends with a force of 2")
for name, pull, bonds in [("no pull", 0, stiff), ("stiff bonds", 2, stiff), ("soft bonds", 2, soft)]:
    gaps = gaps_when_pulled(pull, bonds)
    print(f"   {name:13} ", draw(gaps), f"  length {gaps.sum():.0f}")

print()
print("2. Each soft bond snaps when its pull reaches 3, except bond 4,")
print("   which has a flaw and snaps at 1.5. Pull harder, step by step:")
strength = np.array([3.0, 3.0, 3.0, 1.5, 3.0])
pull = 0.0
while True:
    pull += 0.5
    snapped = np.nonzero(pull >= strength)[0]
    if len(snapped) > 0:
        bond = snapped[0]
        gaps = gaps_when_pulled(0, soft)          # the pieces spring back
        gaps[bond] = SPACING + 3                   # and fly apart a little
        print(f"   pull {pull:3.1f}      ", draw(gaps, broken=bond), f"  bond {bond + 1} snapped!")
        break
    print(f"   pull {pull:3.1f}      ", draw(gaps_when_pulled(pull, soft)))
print(f"   Without the flaw the chain would have held until the pull reached {strength.max():.1f}.")
```

The first picture shows the main idea of stiffness. With the same pull, the chain with stiff bonds stretches from length 30 to 35, and the chain with soft bonds stretches all the way to 50. Each bond in a chain carries the **same** pull, so every bond stretches by the same amount and the stretch adds up along the chain. A chain twice as long would stretch twice as far.

The second picture shows something about strength. Every bond can carry a pull of 3, except bond 4, which has a flaw and can carry only 1.5. As the pull grows, all the bonds stretch evenly, until the pull reaches 1.5 and bond 4 snaps. The other bonds could have held twice as much, but it makes no difference: **a chain is only as strong as its weakest link**. Real materials are the same. They almost never break at the strength of their perfect bonds; they break at their worst flaw, a scratch, a bubble, or a tiny crack. Materials scientists spend a great deal of effort finding and removing flaws, which is part of what you will learn in <LessonLink slug="fracture-and-toughness">Fracture and toughness</LessonLink>.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The model exaggerates to make the stretch visible: its stiff chain stretches by a sixth of its length. Real steel springs back only from much smaller stretches. S355 steel starts to bend for good at 355 MPa, as in the last lesson, and the European design standard gives steel's stiffness as 210 000 MPa, so the biggest stretch it can spring back from is 355 ÷ 210 000, about 0.0017: less than a fifth of one percent. Change `soft` to `np.full(5, 1.0)` in the program and predict the new length before you run it.

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How materials scientists see inside">

The ideas in this lesson are not guesses: materials scientists check them directly. X-ray **diffraction**, discovered in Germany in 1912 by Max von Laue and turned into a precise tool in Britain by William and Lawrence Bragg, father and son, reveals how the atoms in a crystal are arranged, and electron microscopes now show individual rows of atoms, and even dislocations moving. Computer programs much like the one above, but with millions of atoms, let researchers watch a crack run through a ceramic, and the free [Materials Project](https://materialsproject.org/) database draws the crystal structure of almost any inorganic material. You will meet all of these in <LessonLink slug="crystal-structures">Crystal structures</LessonLink>, <LessonLink slug="defects-and-dislocations">Defects and dislocations</LessonLink>, <LessonLink slug="microscopy">Microscopy</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="atomistic-simulation">Atomistic simulation</LessonLink>.

Knowing *why* gives us control. Adding a few carbon atoms to iron jams its dislocations and turns soft iron into strong steel. Adding cross-links to rubber turns it from sticky goo into tyres. Making tiny glass fibres with almost no flaws turns brittle glass into a material strong enough to reinforce boats. Every one of these began with someone asking what the atoms were doing.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Glass is really a very slow liquid, so old church windows are thicker at the bottom."** At room temperature, the atoms in window glass are locked in place just as firmly as in a crystal. A careful calculation by the Brazilian glass scientist Edgar Zanotto showed that ordinary window glass would need far longer than the age of the universe to sag visibly. Old panes are uneven because of how glass was made then, and glaziers often fitted the thick edge at the bottom.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A piece of metal is made of metal molecules."** A metal has no separate molecules. The whole piece is one giant arrangement of ions held together by a shared sea of electrons, which is exactly what lets its layers slide without coming apart.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Stronger bonds always make a tougher material."** Ceramics and diamond have some of the strongest bonds there are, yet they are brittle. Toughness depends on whether the material can change shape at a crack tip, as metals can by sliding layers. Strong bonds give hardness, stiffness and high melting points, not toughness.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A rubber band stretches because its bonds stretch."** Its bonds barely stretch at all. Stretching rubber pulls crumpled chains straight, and they spring back because their jiggling makes crumpled shapes far more likely than straight ones.

</Misconception>
