<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="The right material for the job">

Open a kitchen cupboard and you will find the same job, holding a drink, done by four different materials: a glass bottle, an aluminium can, a plastic bottle and a paper carton. A frying pan is made of steel or cast iron, but its handle is plastic or wood. A bicycle might be steel, aluminium, titanium, carbon fibre or even bamboo. None of these choices is an accident. Each is the answer to a question engineers ask every day: *which material does this job best?*

The answer is never just "the strongest" or "the lightest". It depends on what the object must do, how many will be made, what people will pay, how long it should last, and what happens to it afterwards. This lesson shows how engineers make that choice, and what happens to materials at the end of their lives.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here's the key idea: every material choice is a **trade-off**. Strong, light, cheap, long-lasting, easy to recycle: no material is best at all of them, so choosing a material means deciding which ones matter most for *this* job, and accepting the costs that come with them.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="method" kind="definitions" title="How engineers choose">

In the 1980s and 1990s Michael Ashby and his colleagues at the University of Cambridge turned material choice into a clear method, now taught to engineering students around the world. In simple words it has four steps.

<Definition term="choosing a material in four steps">

1. **List what the job needs.** What must the part do? What must it never do (break, rust, melt, poison anyone)? And what do we most want: the lightest, the cheapest, or the one with the smallest impact on the planet?
2. **Screen.** Rule out every material that cannot meet a must-have. A frying pan must survive 250 °C, so most plastics are out.
3. **Rank.** Put the materials that remain in order of how well they do the thing we most want.
4. **Look closely.** Check the top few for everything else: price, how easy they are to shape and join, how long they last, and what happens at the end of their life.

</Definition>

Lumenary ME's [how things are made](/en/me/how-things-are-made/) shows the same thinking from a designer's side, together with the ways of shaping each material.

</Section>

<Section id="drinks" kind="examples" title="Four ways to hold a drink">

Here is a first, rough comparison for drinks containers, done by eye rather than with numbers. Each column is a requirement; each row a material.

| Container | Light? | Survives a fall? | Reseals? | Keeps the fizz in for months? | Easy to recycle into the same thing? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| glass bottle | no: by far the heaviest | no: it can shatter | with a cap | yes | yes, and it can also be washed and refilled |
| aluminium can | yes | dents, but does not break | no | yes, and keeps light out | yes, very well |
| plastic (PET) bottle | yes | yes | yes | for a while: some gas slowly escapes through the plastic | yes, if collected and sorted |
| drinks carton | yes | yes | sometimes | not used for fizzy drinks | harder: paper, plastic and aluminium foil are pressed together in thin layers |

No container wins every column. Glass is heavy, which costs fuel to carry, but a glass bottle that is washed and refilled many times, as in Germany's deposit system or with the returnable beer and soft-drink bottles common across Latin America and Africa, does its job again and again. A can is light and recycles superbly, but cannot be resealed. A PET bottle is light, clear and resealable, and is cheap to make. The best choice depends on how far the drink travels, whether the container will be refilled, and whether the local recycling system can take it back.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice what this comparison quietly assumes: that the bottle is actually refilled, that the can actually reaches a recycling plant, that the PET bottle is not thrown in a river. A material's "green" score depends as much on what people and cities do with it as on the material itself. Always ask what a comparison assumes before you trust its winner.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Choosing a bicycle frame in Python">

Now a harder job, with numbers: the frame of a bicycle. It must be **stiff**, so that it does not flex like a noodle when you pedal; **strong**, so that it never bends or breaks; and **tough**, because bicycles fall over. And riders want it as **light** as possible.

The program follows the method. It screens out the brittle materials, then works out how heavy a frame of each remaining material must be to be just as stiff and just as strong as a steel frame of 2.0 kg, with tubes of the same outside width. A tube made of a less stiff material needs a thicker wall to be as stiff, so its mass goes up in proportion to density divided by stiffness; to be as strong, its mass goes up as density divided by strength. The frame must manage both, so its mass is the larger of the two. Press **Run**:

```python run
# Choosing a material for a bicycle frame, the way materials engineers do it:
# 1. screen out materials that cannot do the job at all,
# 2. rank the rest by how light a frame they give,
# 3. look closely at the winners' other trade-offs.
#
# Typical values (rounded), as in "Materials around us":
#   density (g/cm3), stiffness (GPa), strength (MPa), and whether it is brittle
materials = [
    # name                       density  stiffness  strength  brittle
    ("steel",                     7.85,     210,       355,     False),
    ("aluminium alloy",           2.70,      69,       276,     False),
    ("titanium alloy",            4.43,     114,       880,     False),
    ("carbon-fibre composite",    1.55,      70,       550,     False),
    ("softwood timber",           0.42,      11,      14.5,     False),
    ("polyethylene (HDPE)",       0.95,       1,        25,     False),
    ("window glass",              2.50,      70,        45,     True),
    ("concrete",                  2.40,      33,       2.9,     True),
]

# Step 1: screening. A frame takes knocks; anything brittle could shatter.
candidates = [m for m in materials if not m[4]]
print("Ruled out as too brittle:", ", ".join(m[0] for m in materials if m[4]))

# Step 2: ranking. Imagine every frame has tubes of the same outside width,
# and a steel frame of 2.0 kg. To be as stiff as the steel frame, a tube's wall
# must be thicker if the material is less stiff, so its mass grows as
# density / stiffness. To be as strong, its mass grows as density / strength.
steel = materials[0]
STEEL_FRAME = 2.0   # kg

print()
print("material                stiffness   kg to be   kg to be    frame")
print("                        for weight   as stiff   as strong   mass")
rows = []
for name, density, stiffness, strength, brittle in candidates:
    for_stiffness = STEEL_FRAME * (density / stiffness) / (steel[1] / steel[2])
    for_strength = STEEL_FRAME * (density / strength) / (steel[1] / steel[3])
    frame = max(for_stiffness, for_strength)   # it must be stiff enough AND strong enough
    rows.append((frame, name, stiffness / density, for_stiffness, for_strength))
for frame, name, specific, for_stiffness, for_strength in sorted(rows):
    print(f"{name:24}{specific:8.1f}   {for_stiffness:8.2f}   {for_strength:8.2f}   {frame:6.2f} kg")
```

The ranking holds a surprise. The carbon-fibre composite gives the lightest frame, **1.18 kg**, as you might expect. But steel, titanium alloy and aluminium alloy finish almost level, at **2.00**, **2.08** and **2.09 kg**, and even softwood gets within about two-thirds of a kilogram. The second column explains why: their **stiffness for weight** (stiffness divided by density) is nearly the same, between 25.6 and 26.8, so with tubes of the same width they all need about the same mass to be equally stiff. For the metals the strength column barely matters, because they are strong enough long before they are stiff enough; softwood is the exception, held back by its strength. Polyethylene would need a 51 kg frame: plastic alone is far too floppy for a bicycle.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The phrase "tubes of the same outside width" is doing a lot of work. Real aluminium frames are lighter than steel ones because their tubes are much *fatter*: a wider tube is far stiffer for the same mass. Steel cannot copy that trick for long, because to keep the mass down a fat steel tube would need walls so thin that they would dent or crumple. Change an assumption and the ranking changes, so engineers always state theirs.

</Narrator>

Step four, looking closely, brings in everything the program ignores:

| Frame | Price | Repairs | Feel and life | End of life |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| steel | cheapest | can be welded in almost any workshop in the world | comfortable, long-lived if kept from rusting | recycled easily |
| aluminium alloy | moderate | hard to repair; usually replaced | stiff and light | recycled easily |
| titanium alloy | expensive | specialist welding only | comfortable; does not corrode | recyclable, but titanium is costly to make in the first place |
| carbon-fibre composite | expensive | specialist repair | lightest; can be shaped freely | hard to recycle |
| bamboo or wood | varies | local craftspeople | comfortable, absorbs bumps | grows back; the bamboo itself rots away harmlessly, though glued joints may not |

That is why racing bicycles are mostly carbon fibre, everyday and touring bicycles are mostly steel or aluminium, and bamboo bicycles are built by workshops in Ghana, the Philippines and elsewhere from a material that grows nearby.

<Section id="trade-offs" kind="intuition" title="Cost, weight, strength and the planet">

Cyclists have a well-known saying that a frame can be strong, light or cheap, but you can only pick two. It is a joke, but it holds a truth: the properties we want pull against each other, and so does their price.

The planet is part of the price. Every material takes energy to make, and that energy usually releases carbon dioxide. Making a kilogram of aluminium from its ore takes many times more energy than making a kilogram of steel, and titanium and carbon fibre take more still. But a kilogram is not the whole story. A lighter aircraft or car burns less fuel every day for decades, which can more than repay the extra energy used to make it. Concrete takes little energy per kilogram, but the world uses so much of it that making cement releases more carbon dioxide each year than all the world's aeroplanes. How long a product lasts matters too: a frame that lasts thirty years has to be made only once, while a cheaper one replaced every five years has to be made six times.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Here's a quick estimate. If recycled aluminium needs only about 5% of the energy of new aluminium, as the Aluminum Association reports, then making 20 cans from recycled metal uses about the same energy as making **one** can from ore, since 20 × 5% = 100%. That is why a can thrown away is such a waste, and why scrap aluminium is worth collecting.

</Narrator>

</Section>

The order matters: first **reduce** (use less), then **reuse** and **repair**, and only then **recycle**. A repaired bicycle needs no new frame at all; a recycled one still needs melting, shaping and painting. But when a material does reach the end of its life, some come back far better than others, and the reasons go right down to the atoms of <LessonLink slug="atoms-bonds-and-properties">Why materials behave as they do</LessonLink>.

**Metals recycle very well.** Melting a metal does not change its atoms: melt an aluminium can and you get aluminium again, ready to become a new can. Metals are also easy to sort. Giant magnets pull steel out of mixed rubbish, and a spinning magnet under a conveyor belt makes electric currents flow in aluminium cans that flick them off the belt into their own bin. And metals are valuable, so scrap is worth collecting. According to the Aluminum Association, about 75% of all the aluminium ever produced is still in use.

**Glass recycles well too.** Crushed bottle glass melts down into new bottles, over and over. It must be sorted by colour, and drinking glasses, ovenware and ceramic mugs must be kept out, because they melt differently and spoil the batch.

**Paper recycles a few times.** Recycled paper is made by soaking it back into fibres, but each time the fibres get shorter and weaker, so after a handful of cycles they are too short to hold together.

**Many plastics are harder.** There are dozens of different plastics, which look alike and must be sorted before they can be melted, and mixing two kinds spoils both. Each melting also breaks some of the long chains, so the plastic often comes back weaker and is made into something less demanding, such as fleece or park benches instead of new bottles. Plastics that are **cross-linked**, like the rubber in tyres and the hard resins in many composites, cannot be melted at all, because their chains are tied together. In 2022 the OECD estimated that only about 9% of the world's plastic waste was recycled.

**Composites are hardest of all.** Carbon or glass fibres locked in a cross-linked resin cannot easily be separated, which is why old wind-turbine blades and carbon-fibre frames are a growing problem, and why researchers are developing resins that can be taken apart again.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Even metals have a catch. When scrapped cars are shredded, bits of copper wire end up mixed with the steel, and copper cannot easily be removed from molten steel. A little too much copper makes steel crack when it is rolled hot, so steelmakers must dilute their scrap with cleaner metal. Recycling works best when products are designed to come apart into pure materials.

</Narrator>

### A recycling detective at home

With an adult, collect some rinsed, empty containers from the recycling bin, leaving out anything broken or with sharp edges.

- **Magnet test.** Hold a fridge magnet to each can. Steel food cans stick; aluminium drinks cans do not. You have just done what a recycling plant's magnet does.
- **Plastic codes.** Look for a small triangle with a number inside, often drawn with arrows, on the bottom of plastic containers. It is a **resin identification code**, which says which plastic it is, not whether your area recycles it. The common codes are 1 for PET (clear drinks bottles), 2 for HDPE (milk bottles), 3 for PVC, 4 for LDPE (squeezy bottles and bags), 5 for polypropylene (yoghurt pots and bottle tops), 6 for polystyrene, and 7 for everything else.
- **Weigh them.** Use a kitchen scale to weigh an empty can, an empty plastic bottle and an empty glass bottle, and read how many millilitres of drink each holds. Work out the grams of container for every 100 ml of drink. Which container carries the most drink for the least material?

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How it is done in industry and research">

Engineers choose materials with software built on Ashby's charts and large property databases, and judge their impact with **life-cycle assessment**: an accounting of the energy, water, emissions and waste of a product from the mine to the end of its life, done by methods set out in international standards (the ISO 14040 series). Designers increasingly plan for the end from the start: products that unscrew instead of being glued, a single plastic instead of three, labels that wash off in the recycling plant.

Materials scientists are working on the hardest problems. In 2016 researchers in Japan discovered a bacterium that can digest PET, and enzymes inspired by it are being developed to break PET back into the small molecules it was made from, so that it can be remade as good as new. Steelmakers in Sweden and elsewhere are testing ways to make iron from ore with hydrogen instead of coal, and cement makers are testing new recipes and ways to capture their carbon dioxide (as of 2026, most of these are at the pilot or early commercial stage). You will meet this work in <LessonLink slug="polymers">Polymers</LessonLink>, <LessonLink slug="composites">Composites</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="sustainable-materials">Sustainable materials</LessonLink>, and from the engineer's side in Lumenary ME's [sustainable engineering](/en/me/sustainable-engineering/) and Lumenary Chemistry's [sustainable chemistry](/en/chemistry/sustainable-chemistry/).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A triangle with a number means it will be recycled."** The number inside the triangle only says which plastic it is. Whether it is recycled depends on what your local collection can sort and sell.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Biodegradable plastic simply disappears."** Many biodegradable plastics break down only in the heat of an industrial composting plant. In the sea or a garden heap they can last for years.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The lightest material is always the best."** Light materials save fuel when things move, but they may cost far more energy to make, be harder to repair or recycle, or simply be unnecessary: a park bench does not need carbon fibre.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Natural materials are always better for the planet."** Wood and bamboo grow back, which is a real advantage, but only if forests are managed well, and a natural material that wears out quickly can end up costing more than a durable one.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Recycling is the best thing you can do with old things."** Recycling is good, but using less, reusing and repairing are usually better still, because they keep the product itself, and all the energy that went into making it, in use.

</Misconception>

<Reflection title="caretakers">

The book of Genesis describes the first people being placed in a garden "to dress it and to keep it", in the words of the King James Bible. Many Christians read that as a calling to be caretakers of the world's materials, using them well and wasting little. Whatever you believe, choosing a material that lasts, mending what can be mended and sending the rest back into use is a practical way of caring for the world and for the people who will live in it after us.

</Reflection>
