<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Properties from architecture">

Everything earlier in this course traced properties to chemistry and microstructure: bonds set stiffness, dislocations set strength, bands set colour. A **metamaterial** turns that around. Its properties come mainly from the geometry of a designed unit cell, repeated on a scale much smaller than the wavelength or the specimen, so that the same polymer can be stiff or soft, can fatten when stretched, or can block sound at one frequency and pass it at another, depending on how it is arranged. Natural materials discovered this first: bone, wood, honeycomb in a bird's skull and the porous core of a plant stem are all architected for stiffness at low weight.

The word arrived with optics. In 1968 Victor Veselago asked what a material with both negative permittivity and negative permeability would do; around 2000 John Pendry at Imperial College London and David Smith's group, then at the University of California, San Diego, showed how to build one for microwaves from wires and split rings, and the field took off. In parallel, mechanics had its own story: Roderic Lakes made foams that expand sideways when stretched in 1987, and additive manufacturing, covered in Lumenary ME's [additive manufacturing](/en/me/additive-manufacturing/), made it possible from the 2010s to print almost any lattice. This lesson takes the mechanical side in depth and the acoustic and optical sides in outline, then asks what has left the laboratory.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The organising idea is separation of scales. If the cell is much smaller than the specimen (for mechanics) or the wavelength (for waves), the structure can be replaced by an effective homogeneous medium with its own properties, which may be ones no ordinary solid has. When that separation fails, the "material" is really a structure, and has to be analysed as one.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Metamaterials, architected materials and effective media">

<Definition term="Architected material and metamaterial">

An **architected material** has a designed internal geometry — a lattice, honeycomb, foam or fibre arrangement — at a scale between the microstructure and the component. A **metamaterial** is an architected material whose effective properties are unusual or absent in its constituents, such as a negative Poisson's ratio, negative effective mass density or modulus near a resonance, or a negative refractive index. The **effective properties** are those of the homogeneous medium that reproduces the architected material's behaviour at scales much larger than its cell, obtained by homogenisation: averaging stress and strain (or fields) over a representative cell.

</Definition>

For an isotropic linear-elastic solid, positive-definite strain energy requires $-1 \lt \nu \lt \tfrac12$. Ordinary materials sit between about 0.2 and 0.5 (rubber near 0.5, steel about 0.3, cork near 0). Materials with $\nu \lt 0$ are called **auxetic**, a word coined by Ken Evans in 1991. Anisotropic architected materials are not bound by the isotropic limits: a honeycomb can have in-plane Poisson's ratios well above 1, as the next section shows. <LessonLink slug="multiscale-modelling">Multiscale modelling</LessonLink> covers homogenisation in general; here the cell is simple enough to analyse by hand.

</Section>

<Section id="honeycomb" kind="computation" title="The re-entrant honeycomb">

<Theorem name="Poisson's ratio of a honeycomb">

When the inclined struts deform by bending or by rotating about their joints, a honeycomb loaded along direction 1 (the horizontal) has in-plane Poisson's ratio

$$
\nu_{12} = \frac{\cos^2\theta}{\left(h/l + \sin\theta\right)\sin\theta},
$$

which is negative for re-entrant cells ($\theta \lt 0$, with $h/l + \sin\theta \gt 0$), and $\nu_{12}\,\nu_{21} = 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take the hinging picture, which gives the same ratio as bending because in both the vertical struts stay straight and the inclined struts change their angle. The cell's repeat lengths are $X = 2l\cos\theta$ horizontally and $Y = 2(h + l\sin\theta)$ vertically. A small rotation $d\theta$ gives strains $\varepsilon_1 = dX/X = -\tan\theta\,d\theta$ and $\varepsilon_2 = dY/Y = l\cos\theta\,d\theta/(h + l\sin\theta)$. Hence $\nu_{12} = -\varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_1 = \dfrac{l\cos\theta}{(h + l\sin\theta)\tan\theta} = \dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{(h/l + \sin\theta)\sin\theta}$. Loading along 2 runs the same mechanism in reverse, so $\nu_{21} = -\varepsilon_1/\varepsilon_2 = 1/\nu_{12}$. If $\theta \lt 0$, $\sin\theta \lt 0$ while the other factors are positive, so $\nu_{12} \lt 0$.

</Proof>

The program evaluates the Gibson–Ashby beam-bending formulas for $\nu_{12}$, the two in-plane moduli and the relative density, and checks the Poisson's ratio against a finite-difference calculation of the hinge kinematics.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A honeycomb cell: inclined struts of length l at angle theta to the horizontal (theta < 0: re-entrant),
# vertical struts of length h, all of thickness t, out-of-plane depth b. Gibson and Ashby's bending model.
def honeycomb(theta_deg, h_over_l, t_over_l):
    th = np.radians(theta_deg)
    s, c = np.sin(th), np.cos(th)
    nu12 = c**2 / ((h_over_l + s) * s)                         # pull along x (1), measure contraction along y (2)
    E1 = t_over_l**3 * c / ((h_over_l + s) * s**2)             # E1 / E_solid
    E2 = t_over_l**3 * (h_over_l + s) / c**3                   # E2 / E_solid
    rho = t_over_l * (h_over_l + 2) / (2 * c * (h_over_l + s)) # relative density
    return nu12, E1, E2, rho

# An independent check: the cell's repeat lengths are X = 2 l cos(theta) and Y = 2 (h + l sin(theta)).
# Rotating the struts slightly (as a hinged mechanism would) changes both; nu12 = -(dY/Y) / (dX/X).
def nu_from_kinematics(theta_deg, h_over_l, d=1e-6):
    th = np.radians(theta_deg)
    X = lambda a: 2 * np.cos(a)
    Y = lambda a: 2 * (h_over_l + np.sin(a))
    eps1 = (X(th + d) - X(th - d)) / X(th)
    eps2 = (Y(th + d) - Y(th - d)) / Y(th)
    return -eps2 / eps1

print("h/l = 2, t/l = 0.1")
print(" theta   nu12 (beam model)  nu12 (kinematics)   E1/Es      E2/Es    rel. density")
for theta in (-45, -30, -20, -10, 10, 20, 30, 45):
    nu, E1, E2, rho = honeycomb(theta, 2.0, 0.1)
    print(f" {theta:4d}    {nu:+8.3f}          {nu_from_kinematics(theta, 2.0):+8.3f}       {E1:.2e}   {E2:.2e}    {rho:.3f}")
nu, E1, E2, rho = honeycomb(30, 1.0, 0.1)
print(f"\nregular hexagons (theta = 30, h/l = 1): nu12 = {nu:.3f}, E1/Es = {E1:.2e}, relative density {rho:.3f}")
```

The regular honeycomb has $\nu_{12} = 1.000$ exactly, already outside the isotropic range. With $h/l = 2$, the re-entrant cell at $\theta = -30°$ has $\nu_{12} = -1.000$, and at $-10°$ the ratio reaches $-3.06$; the conventional cell at $+10°$ reaches $+2.57$. The two independent calculations agree to the printed digits. The moduli are tiny — about $10^{-3}$ of the solid's at a relative density of 0.1 to 0.2 — because the struts bend, and bending stiffness scales as $(t/l)^3$.

What auxetic behaviour buys: an auxetic sheet bent out of plane curves into a dome rather than a saddle, so it conforms to doubly curved surfaces; under a local impact, material flows *towards* the indentation, raising indentation resistance; and an auxetic tube expands when stretched, useful for stents and for fasteners that grip harder when pulled.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The model assumes small strains, rigid joints of negligible size, slender struts that only bend, and an infinite array of cells. Real re-entrant samples have a handful of cells across, nodes that are a large fraction of the strut length and contacts that close up at a few per cent of strain, after which the Poisson's ratio heads back towards positive values. Measured "−1" is usually a tangent value at small strain, specific to the specimen's size and boundary conditions.

</Narrator>

<Section id="lattices" kind="computation" title="Lattice materials and additive manufacturing">

Lattices — periodic networks of struts or shells — are the most practical architected materials, because additive manufacturing can build them in metals (laser powder-bed fusion), polymers (vat photopolymerisation, including continuous "digital light synthesis") and ceramics. Their behaviour depends first on whether the struts bend or stretch.

Gibson and Ashby's analysis of foams gives, for **bending-dominated** structures, a relative modulus $E^*/E_s \approx (\rho^*/\rho_s)^2$ and relative strength $\sigma^*/\sigma_{ys} \approx 0.3\,(\rho^*/\rho_s)^{3/2}$, because a strut's bending stiffness grows as its thickness to the fourth power while its mass grows as the square. In a **stretch-dominated** lattice, where loads are carried axially by triangulated members, both scale linearly, $E^*/E_s \approx \tfrac13(\rho^*/\rho_s)$ and $\sigma^*/\sigma_{ys} \approx \tfrac13(\rho^*/\rho_s)$ at best, which is far stiffer at low density. Which one a lattice is follows from **Maxwell's counting rule** for pin-jointed frames: with $b$ struts and $j$ joints, $M = b - 2j + 3$ in two dimensions and $M = b - 3j + 6$ in three. If $M \lt 0$ the frame is a mechanism, and only the joints' stiffness and the struts' bending resist load; Deshpande, Ashby and Fleck showed in 2001 that a periodic lattice needs a nodal connectivity of 6 in two dimensions and 12 in three to be stretch-dominated, which the octet truss achieves.

The block applies both laws to additively manufactured Ti-6Al-4V (rounded typical solid properties), asks what relative density gives a stiffness of 3 GPa — within the range of trabecular bone, the target for orthopaedic implants that should not shield bone from load — and applies Maxwell's rule to four cells.

```python run
# Gibson-Ashby scaling for cellular solids and lattices, with the usual prefactors:
#   bending-dominated (open-cell foams, low-connectivity lattices):  E/Es = (rho/rhos)^2,  sigma/sigma_ys = 0.3 (rho/rhos)^1.5
#   stretch-dominated (fully triangulated lattices, e.g. the octet truss):  E/Es = (rho/rhos)/3,  sigma/sigma_ys = (rho/rhos)/3
# Solid: additively manufactured Ti-6Al-4V, rounded typical values.
Es, sys, rhos = 110e3, 900.0, 4430.0          # MPa, MPa, kg/m^3

print("rel. density  density    bending: E (GPa)  strength (MPa)   stretching: E (GPa)  strength (MPa)")
for r in (0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30):
    Eb, sb = Es * r**2, sys * 0.3 * r**1.5
    Est, sst = Es * r / 3, sys * r / 3
    print(f"   {r:4.2f}      {rhos * r:5.0f} kg/m3    {Eb / 1000:8.3f}       {sb:7.1f}            {Est / 1000:7.2f}        {sst:7.1f}")

# Design question: which relative density gives a stiffness of 3 GPa (in the range of trabecular bone)?
target = 3e3
r_bend = (target / Es) ** 0.5
r_stretch = 3 * target / Es
print(f"\nfor E = 3 GPa: bending-dominated needs rel. density {r_bend:.3f}, stretch-dominated {r_stretch:.3f}")
print(f"  -> the stretch-dominated lattice is {r_bend / r_stretch:.1f} times lighter for the same stiffness")

# Maxwell's counting rule for a pin-jointed frame of b struts and j joints: M = b - 2j + 3 in 2D,
# M = b - 3j + 6 in 3D. M < 0: a mechanism, so loads bend the struts; M >= 0: struts can carry load by stretching.
frames = {"square (2D)": (4, 4, 2), "triangle (2D)": (3, 3, 2), "cube (3D)": (12, 8, 3), "octahedron (3D)": (12, 6, 3)}
print()
for name, (b, j, dim) in frames.items():
    M = b - 2 * j + 3 if dim == 2 else b - 3 * j + 6
    print(f"{name:16s} b = {b:2d}, j = {j}: M = {M:d}  -> {'stretch-dominated possible' if M >= 0 else 'mechanism: bending-dominated'}")
```

At 10 per cent relative density (443 kg/m³, lighter than many woods), the ideal stretch-dominated titanium lattice is 3.67 GPa stiff and 30 MPa strong, against 1.10 GPa and 8.5 MPa for a bending-dominated one. For a 3 GPa implant, the stretch-dominated design needs a relative density of 0.082 and the bending-dominated one 0.165, twice the mass — though for bone ingrowth, pore size and permeability matter as much as stiffness, and implant makers often accept bending-dominated cells for that reason. Maxwell's rule confirms the intuition: a square or cube is a mechanism ($M = -1$ and $-6$), a triangle or octahedron is rigid ($M = 0$).

<Narrator who="margaret">

Printed lattices rarely reach the ideal lines. Struts made by powder-bed fusion are rough, slightly wavy and thicker than designed where powder sinters onto them, nodes collect extra material, and down-facing surfaces are worst. Published tests of metal octet lattices commonly find stiffnesses well below the one-third prefactor and fatigue lives limited by surface defects, not by the alloy. Measure the as-built geometry by X-ray computed tomography before believing a model, as <LessonLink slug="microscopy">Microscopy</LessonLink> and Lumenary ME's additive manufacturing lesson both stress.

</Narrator>

The record-breaking results of the 2010s were at small scales: hollow nickel microlattices of 2011 lighter than 1 mg/cm³; stretch-dominated ceramic and polymer microlattices in 2014 that kept nearly linear stiffness scaling down to very low density; and hollow alumina nanolattices made by two-photon lithography, also in 2014, whose walls were thin enough (tens of nanometres) to bend and recover rather than fracture — a size effect, since flaws shrink with the walls, as <LessonLink slug="fracture-and-toughness">Fracture and toughness</LessonLink> predicts. Since about 2020, **inverse design** with machine learning (for example, deep-learning models that output a truss topology for a requested anisotropic stiffness tensor) and topology optimisation have turned lattice design into a computational problem, linked to Lumenary ME's [simulation-driven design](/en/me/simulation-driven-design/).

Beyond stiffness and strength, mechanical metamaterials include **pentamode** materials, easy to shear but hard to compress, which behave like solid "metafluids"; **multistable** lattices of snapping elements that absorb energy and can be reused; **chiral** lattices that twist when compressed (2017); **kirigami** sheets whose cuts make them stretch and morph; and **topological** mechanical lattices with floppy modes localised on one edge. Most of these are research topics.

</Section>

<Section id="acoustic" kind="computation" title="Acoustic and elastic metamaterials, in outline">

Waves in a periodic medium have band gaps, frequency ranges in which they cannot propagate. In a **phononic crystal** the gap comes from Bragg scattering and opens near wavelengths comparable to the period, so blocking audible sound with a phononic crystal needs cells tens of centimetres across. In 2000 Ping Sheng's group at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology showed another mechanism: lead spheres coated in soft silicone rubber, embedded in epoxy, act as **local resonators**, and near their resonance the composite behaves as if it had a negative effective mass, opening a gap at wavelengths far larger than the cell. The simplest model is a chain of masses $m_1$ joined by springs $k_1$, each carrying an inner mass $m_2$ on a soft spring $k_2$. The block computes its band structure and the transmission through 20 cells.

```python run
import numpy as np

# A one-dimensional "mass-in-mass" chain: outer masses m1 joined by springs k1 (spacing a), each carrying
# an inner mass m2 on a spring k2 -- the simplest locally resonant acoustic metamaterial.
m1, m2, k1, k2, a = 1.0, 0.5, 1.0, 0.05, 1.0     # arbitrary consistent units
w0 = np.sqrt(k2 / m2)                            # the inner resonator's own frequency

# Dispersion: for each wavenumber q, solve the 2x2 generalised eigenproblem K(q) u = w^2 M u.
qs = np.linspace(1e-4, np.pi / a, 400)
branches = []
for q in qs:
    K = np.array([[2 * k1 * (1 - np.cos(q * a)) + k2, -k2], [-k2, k2]])
    Minv_sqrt = np.diag([m1 ** -0.5, m2 ** -0.5])
    w2 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Minv_sqrt @ K @ Minv_sqrt)
    branches.append(np.sqrt(w2))
branches = np.array(branches)
gap_lo, gap_hi = branches[:, 0].max(), branches[:, 1].min()
print(f"resonator frequency w0 = {w0:.4f}")
print(f"band gap from {gap_lo:.4f} to {gap_hi:.4f} (expected near w0 to w0*sqrt(1 + m2/m1) = {w0 * np.sqrt(1 + m2 / m1):.4f})")
c_long = a * np.sqrt(k1 / (m1 + m2))                       # long-wavelength sound speed of the chain
print(f"wavelength of sound in the chain at w0: {2 * np.pi * c_long / w0:.1f} cells")

# Transmission through a finite chain of N cells driven at one end (light damping), for several frequencies.
def transmission(w, N=20, eta=0.01):
    n = 2 * N
    Kmat = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=complex)
    Mvec = np.zeros(n)
    for i in range(N):
        o, r = 2 * i, 2 * i + 1                              # outer and inner mass of cell i
        Mvec[o], Mvec[r] = m1, m2
        kk2 = k2 * (1 + 1j * eta)
        Kmat[o, o] += kk2; Kmat[r, r] += kk2; Kmat[o, r] -= kk2; Kmat[r, o] -= kk2
        if i < N - 1:
            kk1 = k1 * (1 + 1j * eta)
            p = 2 * (i + 1)
            Kmat[o, o] += kk1; Kmat[p, p] += kk1; Kmat[o, p] -= kk1; Kmat[p, o] -= kk1
    A = Kmat - w**2 * np.diag(Mvec)
    F = np.zeros(n, dtype=complex); F[0] = 1.0              # unit force on the first outer mass
    u = np.linalg.solve(A, F)
    return abs(u[2 * (N - 1)]) / abs(u[0])

print("\nfrequency   |u_out / u_in| through 20 cells")
for w in (0.10, 0.20, 0.28, 0.32, 0.36, 0.40, 0.60):
    T = transmission(w)
    print(f"  {w:5.2f}      {T:9.2e}  " + ("in the gap" if gap_lo < w < gap_hi else ""))
```

The gap runs from 0.3142 to 0.3873, just below the resonator frequency 0.3162 up to $\omega_0\sqrt{1 + m_2/m_1} = 0.3873$, and at the resonance a wave in the chain is 16.2 cells long: the gap is **sub-wavelength**, which a Bragg gap cannot be. Through 20 cells, the transmitted amplitude falls to $4.8 \times 10^{-11}$ at 0.32 and $6.1 \times 10^{-3}$ at 0.36, while outside the gap it is of order 1. The price is bandwidth: a local-resonance gap is narrow, set by the mass ratio, and damping smears it.

In practice, acoustic metamaterials appear in noise-control panels and ducts tuned to particular tones, in vibration isolation for machinery and vehicles, and as acoustic Bragg-reflector stacks in the solidly mounted bulk-acoustic-wave filters used in many phones, phononic structures engineered at the micrometre scale. Proposals to shield buildings from earthquakes with metre-scale resonator arrays have been explored in field tests, but remain far from practice.

<Section id="optical" kind="computation" title="Electromagnetic and optical metamaterials, in outline">

Electromagnetic metamaterials use sub-wavelength metal or dielectric elements to engineer the effective permittivity $\varepsilon$ and permeability $\mu$. A lattice of thin wires behaves like a very dilute metal with a low plasma frequency, below which $\varepsilon$ is negative; split-ring resonators, small broken loops, give a magnetic resonance with negative $\mu$ just above it. Combine both in the same band and the refractive index is negative, as Smith's group showed at microwave frequencies in 2000–2001. The block computes the wire medium's plasma frequency from Pendry's 1996 formula, then the phase profile a flat **metalens** must impose, a modern application of the same idea at optical wavelengths.

```python run
import numpy as np

c0 = 2.998e8
# 1. A lattice of thin metal wires behaves like a dilute metal with a low plasma frequency
#    (Pendry and colleagues, 1996): omega_p^2 = 2 pi c0^2 / (a^2 ln(a / r)), neglecting losses.
for a, r in ((5e-3, 1e-6), (5e-3, 10e-6), (1e-3, 1e-6)):
    wp = np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * c0**2 / (a**2 * np.log(a / r)))
    print(f"wires of radius {r * 1e6:3.0f} um, {a * 1e3:2.0f} mm apart: plasma frequency {wp / (2 * np.pi) / 1e9:5.1f} GHz")
a, r = 5e-3, 1e-6
wp = np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * c0**2 / (a**2 * np.log(a / r)))
for f in (4e9, 8e9, 12e9):
    eps = 1 - wp**2 / (2 * np.pi * f) ** 2
    print(f"  at {f / 1e9:4.1f} GHz the effective permittivity is {eps:+6.2f}")

# 2. A metalens: to focus a plane wave of wavelength lam at distance f, the surface must add the phase
#    phi(r) = (2 pi / lam) (f - sqrt(r^2 + f^2)), wrapped into [0, 2 pi) by nanopillars of different sizes.
lam, focal, radius = 940e-9, 1e-3, 0.5e-3            # near-infrared, 1 mm focal length, 1 mm aperture
r = np.linspace(0, radius, 6)
phi = 2 * np.pi / lam * (focal - np.sqrt(r**2 + focal**2))
print("\nmetalens, 940 nm light, f = 1 mm:")
for ri, p in zip(r, phi):
    print(f"  r = {ri * 1e6:5.0f} um: required phase {p:9.1f} rad -> wrapped {p % (2 * np.pi):4.2f} rad")
zones = (np.sqrt(radius**2 + focal**2) - focal) / lam
pillar = 400e-9
print(f"  {zones:.0f} full 2-pi zones across the radius; about {np.pi * radius**2 / pillar**2:,.0f} pillars on a 400 nm grid")
print(f"  numerical aperture {radius / np.sqrt(radius**2 + focal**2):.2f}")
```

Wires of 1 µm radius spaced 5 mm apart give a plasma frequency of 8.2 GHz, so the medium's permittivity is −3.20 at 4 GHz and positive above 8.2 GHz; a natural metal's plasma frequency is in the ultraviolet. The metalens needs a phase that grows to −789 radians at its edge, which a metasurface wraps into $[0, 2\pi)$ with 126 concentric zones of pillars, about 4.9 million of them on a 400 nm grid — a pattern made by the same lithography as a chip.

That last point explains what has reached products. **Metasurfaces** — single layers of sub-wavelength pillars that set the local phase, as in the "generalised Snell's law" of Federico Capasso's group (2011) — can be made in semiconductor fabs, and metalenses for near-infrared sensing and time-of-flight depth cameras have been shipped in consumer devices since 2022. Electronically steered metasurface antennas are used in satellite terminals and radar. By contrast, negative-index materials and invisibility "cloaks" remain demonstrations: the 2006 microwave cloak at Duke University worked in two dimensions, in a narrow band, for one polarisation; at optical frequencies, metal losses absorb most of the light, and Pendry's "perfect lens" is limited by the same losses. Lumenary EE's [antennas and radio](/en/ee/antennas-and-radio/) and [photonics](/en/ee/photonics/) cover the devices.

<Section id="real-or-curiosity" kind="human" title="What is real, and what remains a laboratory curiosity">

A fair accounting, as of late 2026:

| Status | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **In products** | Additively manufactured porous titanium in orthopaedic implants; printed polymer lattices in shoe midsoles, bicycle saddles and helmet liners (sold since about 2017–2020); honeycomb and foam cores in aircraft and packaging (architected materials, decades old); acoustic Bragg reflectors in phone filters; metalenses in sensors; metasurface antennas |
| **Being qualified** | Lattice heat exchangers and lightweight brackets in aerospace; crash and impact absorbers; vibration-isolation mounts; auxetic textiles and medical devices; machine-learning-designed lattices |
| **Laboratory curiosities, so far** | Optical invisibility cloaks at human scale; perfect lenses; seismic cloaks for cities; most programmable and topological mechanical metamaterials |

The pattern is that a metamaterial reaches a product when its manufacturing route already exists (lithography, additive manufacturing, honeycomb expansion), when its narrow band or limited strain is acceptable, and when it can be qualified like any other material — tested for fatigue, environmental durability, variability and inspection. The obstacles are rarely the physics and usually manufacturing at scale, defects, cost and certification.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A Poisson's ratio below −1 or above 0.5 is impossible."** Those bounds hold for isotropic materials. Anisotropic honeycombs and lattices can exceed them in particular directions, as the program's $\nu_{12} = +2.57$ and $-3.06$ show, without violating thermodynamics.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Metamaterials beat the laws of physics."** They exploit them: negative effective properties exist only in a frequency band near a resonance, are accompanied by dispersion and loss, and respect causality. A "negative mass" is the effective response of a resonating cell, not antimatter.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Lattices are always lighter for the same performance."** For stiffness per weight in one load case, a solid plate or a well-designed sandwich panel often beats a lattice; lattices win when multiple functions matter (energy absorption, heat exchange, bone ingrowth, tunable properties) or when the load cases are complex.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Invisibility cloaks are around the corner."** Working cloaks are narrowband, polarisation-specific, usually two-dimensional and much larger than what they hide, and broadband cloaking of visible light for a large object faces fundamental limits from causality and loss.

</Misconception>
