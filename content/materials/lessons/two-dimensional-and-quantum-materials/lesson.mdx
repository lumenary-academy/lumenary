<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="One atom thick">

In 2004 Andre Geim, Konstantin Novoselov and colleagues at the University of Manchester peeled graphite apart with adhesive tape until they had flakes one atom thick, found them on a silicon wafer by the faint colour they gave under an optical microscope, and showed that their conductivity could be changed by a gate voltage, like a transistor channel. Graphene had been a theorist's model since Philip Wallace's 1947 band calculation; few expected it to survive as a free-standing crystal. The 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics followed, and so did a new field: hundreds of layered crystals can be thinned to a single layer, each a material that is all surface, whose electrons can be tuned by a gate and whose layers can be stacked like paper in any order and at any angle.

**Quantum materials** is a broader and vaguer label for solids whose useful properties come from distinctly quantum effects acting collectively: topology, strong correlations between electrons, long-lived quantum coherence. Two-dimensional crystals are one of its main playgrounds, and the qubits of quantum computers are among its main customers. This lesson computes the band structure that started it all, then follows the ideas to moiré superlattices, topological invariants and the materials problems of quantum hardware. It builds on the tight-binding chain of <LessonLink slug="electronic-structure-and-bands">Electronic structure and bands</LessonLink>, on <LessonLink slug="semiconductor-materials">Semiconductor materials</LessonLink> and on <LessonLink slug="superconductors">Superconductors</LessonLink>.

<Section id="graphene" kind="definitions" title="Graphene's bands from a tight-binding model">

Graphene's carbon atoms sit on a **honeycomb**: a triangular Bravais lattice with a basis of two atoms, A and B, 1.42 Å apart. Each atom uses three of its four valence electrons in σ bonds to its neighbours; the fourth sits in a $p_z$ orbital perpendicular to the sheet, and those orbitals make the bands near the Fermi level.

<Definition term="Nearest-neighbour tight-binding model of graphene">

With one $p_z$ orbital per atom and a hopping energy $-t$ between nearest neighbours ($t \approx 2.8$ eV), a Bloch state $\psi_\mathbf{k} = c_A\,\phi_{A,\mathbf{k}} + c_B\,\phi_{B,\mathbf{k}}$ obeys the $2 \times 2$ eigenproblem

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \Delta/2 & -t f(\mathbf k) \\ -t f(\mathbf k)^* & -\Delta/2 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} c_A \\ c_B \end{pmatrix} = E \begin{pmatrix} c_A \\ c_B \end{pmatrix},
\qquad f(\mathbf k) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} e^{i\mathbf k \cdot \boldsymbol\delta_j},
$$

where the $\boldsymbol\delta_j$ are the three vectors from an A atom to its B neighbours, and $\Delta$ is an energy difference between the two sublattices (zero in pristine graphene). The bands are $E_\pm(\mathbf k) = \pm\sqrt{\Delta^2/4 + t^2 |f(\mathbf k)|^2}$.

</Definition>

<Theorem name="Dirac cones">

With $\Delta = 0$, the two bands touch at the corners K and K′ of the Brillouin zone, and near a corner, at $\mathbf k = \mathbf K + \mathbf q$, $E_\pm \approx \pm \hbar v_F |\mathbf q|$ with $\hbar v_F = \tfrac{3}{2} t\, a_\text{CC}$, where $a_\text{CC}$ is the carbon–carbon distance.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take $\boldsymbol\delta_1 = a(0, 1)$, $\boldsymbol\delta_{2,3} = a(\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\tfrac12)$ with $a = a_\text{CC}$, and the zone corner $\mathbf K = \bigl(\tfrac{4\pi}{3\sqrt3\,a}, 0\bigr)$. Then $\mathbf K \cdot \boldsymbol\delta_j = 0, \tfrac{2\pi}{3}, -\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$, so the three phases $\omega_j = e^{i\mathbf K\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}$ are the three cube roots of unity and $f(\mathbf K) = 0$: both bands are at $E = 0$. To first order in $\mathbf q$, $f(\mathbf K + \mathbf q) \approx i\,\mathbf q \cdot \sum_j \omega_j \boldsymbol\delta_j$, and a short calculation gives $\sum_j \omega_j \boldsymbol\delta_j = \tfrac{3a}{2}(i, 1)$, so $f \approx \tfrac{3a}{2}(-q_x + i q_y)$ and $|f| = \tfrac{3a}{2}|\mathbf q|$. Hence $E_\pm = \pm t|f| \approx \pm \tfrac{3}{2} t a |\mathbf q|$, a cone. With $\Delta \neq 0$ the same expansion gives $E_\pm \approx \pm\sqrt{\Delta^2/4 + (\hbar v_F q)^2}$, a gap of $\Delta$.

</Proof>

The program diagonalises the Hamiltonian along the path Γ–M–K–Γ and draws the two bands as a text plot, then checks the cone's slope against the theorem.

```python run
import numpy as np

t = 2.8            # nearest-neighbour hopping, eV (a typical fitted value for graphene)
acc = 1.42         # carbon-carbon distance, angstrom
# Vectors from an A atom to its three B neighbours; the lattice vectors are differences of them.
delta = acc * np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [np.sqrt(3) / 2, -0.5], [-np.sqrt(3) / 2, -0.5]])
a1, a2 = delta[0] - delta[1], delta[0] - delta[2]

def bands(k, gap=0.0):
    """Eigenvalues of the 2x2 tight-binding Hamiltonian at wavevector k (1/angstrom).
    gap: energy +gap/2 on A sites and -gap/2 on B sites (as when the two sublattices differ)."""
    f = np.exp(1j * delta @ k).sum()
    H = np.array([[gap / 2, -t * f], [-t * np.conj(f), -gap / 2]])
    e = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
    return np.where(np.abs(e) < 1e-9, 0.0, e)

# Reciprocal lattice and the high-symmetry points of the hexagonal Brillouin zone.
A = np.array([a1, a2])
B = 2 * np.pi * np.linalg.inv(A).T          # rows b1, b2 with a_i . b_j = 2 pi delta_ij
G, M, K = np.zeros(2), B[0] / 2, (2 * B[0] + B[1]) / 3
path = [("G", G), ("M", M), ("K", K), ("G", G)]

print("bands along G-M-K-G: each * is a band, one character per 0.25 eV, | marks E = 0")
for i, ((n1, p1), (n2, p2)) in enumerate(zip(path, path[1:])):
    steps = np.linspace(0, 1, 7) if i == len(path) - 2 else np.linspace(0, 1, 7)[:-1]
    for s in steps:
        lo, hi = bands(p1 + s * (p2 - p1))
        row = [" "] * 73
        row[36] = "|"
        for e in (lo, hi):
            row[int(round(36 + e / 0.25))] = "*"
        label = n1 if s == 0 else (n2 if s == 1 else " ")
        print(f"{label} {lo:6.2f} {hi:6.2f} " + "".join(row))

print(f"\nat G: {bands(G)}   at M: {bands(M)}   at K: {bands(K)}")
q = 1e-3                                       # step away from K, 1/angstrom
slope = bands(K + np.array([q, 0.0]))[1] / q   # eV * angstrom: E = hbar v_F |q|
hbar = 6.582e-16                               # eV s
print(f"cone slope {slope:.3f} eV*A (3 t a_cc / 2 = {1.5 * t * acc:.3f}); v_F = {slope * 1e-10 / hbar:.2e} m/s")
print(f"with a 0.06 eV difference between the sublattices, the gap at K is {np.diff(bands(K, gap=0.06))[0]:.3f} eV")
```

The bands span ±8.40 eV ($\pm 3t$) at Γ, pass ±2.80 eV ($\pm t$) at M, and meet at exactly zero at K. The numerical slope, 5.962 eV Å, matches $\tfrac32 t a_\text{CC} = 5.964$ eV Å, giving a Fermi velocity of $9.06 \times 10^5$ m/s, about 1/330 of the speed of light, close to measured values near $1 \times 10^6$ m/s. With one $p_z$ electron per atom the lower band is exactly full, so the Fermi level sits at the cone's tip: graphene is a semimetal whose electrons near $E_\text{F}$ behave like massless particles described by a two-dimensional Dirac equation, with the A/B sublattice playing the role of spin ("pseudospin").

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why do the bands touch at all, when a chain with two different atoms per cell opens a gap? Because in graphene A and B are identical, and the combination of inversion and time-reversal symmetry forbids a $\Delta$ term. Break the sublattice symmetry — boron nitride, the same honeycomb with boron on A and nitrogen on B, or graphene aligned on boron nitride — and a gap opens, exactly as the last line of the program shows.

</Narrator>

<Section id="gating" kind="computation" title="Density of states and the electric-field effect">

The cone has a striking consequence for the density of states, $g(E)$. In two dimensions, a band with $E \propto |\mathbf q|$ gives $g(E) \propto |E|$, vanishing at the Dirac point; with four-fold degeneracy (two spins, two valleys K and K′), the carrier density for a Fermi energy $E_\text{F}$ above the Dirac point is $n = E_\text{F}^2/\bigl(\pi(\hbar v_F)^2\bigr)$. So few states lie near $E = 0$ that a gate can move the Fermi level by a large fraction of an electronvolt, switching the carriers from holes to electrons. The program samples the whole Brillouin zone to get $g(E)$, then converts carrier densities into Fermi energies and gate voltages.

```python run
import numpy as np

t, acc = 2.8, 1.42                                       # eV, angstrom
delta = acc * np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [np.sqrt(3) / 2, -0.5], [-np.sqrt(3) / 2, -0.5]])
a1, a2 = delta[0] - delta[1], delta[0] - delta[2]
B = 2 * np.pi * np.linalg.inv(np.array([a1, a2])).T     # reciprocal lattice vectors (rows)

# Sample the whole Brillouin zone on an n x n grid and histogram the upper band |t f(k)|.
n = 600
s = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n
k = (s[:, None, None] * B[0] + s[None, :, None] * B[1]).reshape(-1, 2)
E = t * np.abs(np.exp(1j * k @ delta.T).sum(axis=1))
width = 0.5
hist, edges = np.histogram(E, bins=np.arange(0, 8.5 + width, width))
dos = hist / len(E) / width                             # states per eV per cell, per spin, upper band
print("density of states of the upper band (per eV per cell per spin)")
for lo, d in zip(edges[:-1], dos):
    print(f"{lo:4.2f}-{lo + width:4.2f} eV  {d:5.3f} " + "#" * int(round(d * 100)))

# Near the Dirac point the DOS is linear, so the carrier density goes as E_F^2.
hbar_vf = 1.5 * t * acc                                 # eV * angstrom
print("\ncarrier density n (per cm^2) -> Fermi energy above the Dirac point")
for dens in (1e11, 1e12, 1e13):
    n_per_A2 = dens * 1e-16                             # 1 cm^2 = 1e16 angstrom^2
    EF = hbar_vf * np.sqrt(np.pi * n_per_A2)            # n = E_F^2 / (pi (hbar v_F)^2), 4-fold degeneracy
    print(f"  n = {dens:.0e}: E_F = {1000 * EF:5.0f} meV")
eps0, e = 8.854e-12, 1.602e-19
for d_nm, eps_r in ((300, 3.9), (10, 3.9)):
    C = eps_r * eps0 / (d_nm * 1e-9)                    # F/m^2, a gate through SiO2
    print(f"gate on {d_nm} nm of SiO2: 1e12 cm^-2 needs {1e16 * e / C:5.1f} V")
```

The histogram rises linearly from zero — 0.012, 0.036, 0.063 per eV in the first three bins — and peaks between 2.5 and 3.0 eV, at the **van Hove singularity** from the saddle point at M ($E = t$). Near the Dirac point, $10^{12}$ carriers per cm² put the Fermi level 106 meV from the cone's tip, and $10^{13}$ put it 334 meV away, which a 300 nm silicon dioxide gate (the Manchester group's original substrate) reaches at about 14 V per $10^{12}$ cm⁻². This tunability, a carrier mobility that exceeds 10⁵ cm²/V s in clean devices encapsulated in hexagonal boron nitride, and graphene's stiffness, strength and thermal conductivity are what made it famous.

The missing gap is also graphene's limit as a transistor. A logic transistor must turn off, with an on/off current ratio of about $10^5$ or more; a graphene channel at the Dirac point still conducts, and on/off ratios at room temperature stay around ten. Opening a useful gap by cutting graphene into nanoribbons only a few nanometres wide wrecks the mobility through edge disorder. That is why the transistor hopes of two-dimensional materials moved to the dichalcogenides.

<Section id="tmds" kind="examples" title="Transition-metal dichalcogenides and van der Waals stacks">

The **transition-metal dichalcogenides** (TMDs), MX₂ with M = Mo or W and X = S, Se or Te, are layered crystals in which a sheet of metal atoms is sandwiched between two sheets of chalcogen atoms, the layers held together by van der Waals forces. Bulk MoS₂ is an indirect-gap semiconductor (about 1.2–1.3 eV); in 2010, groups at Columbia University and at Berkeley showed independently that a **monolayer** becomes a *direct*-gap semiconductor, at about 1.9 eV, and lights up in photoluminescence. In a monolayer, inversion symmetry is broken and strong spin–orbit coupling splits the bands differently in the K and K′ valleys, so circularly polarised light can address one valley or the other ("valleytronics"); and because there is so little material to screen the Coulomb attraction, electrons and holes bind into **excitons** with binding energies of hundreds of meV, compared with about 4 meV in bulk GaAs.

Two-dimensional crystals can be stacked without matching their lattices, because van der Waals bonding does not require it. Geim and Grigorieva called the result **van der Waals heterostructures**: graphene encapsulated in hexagonal boron nitride (an atomically flat insulator with a 6 eV gap) for clean transport, TMD heterobilayers whose electrons and holes sit in different layers, and tunnelling and light-emitting devices built layer by layer. The research workhorse is still mechanical exfoliation and transfer with polymer stamps, done one device at a time.

For electronics, monolayer TMDs are candidates for the transistor channel after silicon's scaling limits, because a channel 0.65 nm thick keeps good gate control at very short gate lengths where silicon's mobility collapses. Semiconductor manufacturers and research institutes in Belgium, Taiwan, the United States, Korea, China and elsewhere grow MoS₂ and WSe₂ on 200–300 mm wafers by metal–organic chemical vapour deposition and report research transistors, and in 2025 a team at Fudan University reported a simple 32-bit RISC-V processor built from about 5,900 MoS₂ transistors. As of 2026 the open problems are materials problems: low-resistance contacts to an atomically thin semiconductor, p-type devices to complement n-type MoS₂, gate dielectrics that do not damage or dope the channel, and defect densities in wafer-scale films. Industry roadmaps place 2D channels, if at all, in the 2030s. Lumenary EE's [transistor scaling](/en/ee/transistor-scaling/) covers the device side.

</Section>

<Section id="moire" kind="computation" title="Twisted bilayers and moiré physics">

Lay one graphene sheet on another with a small twist $\theta$ and a **moiré** pattern appears, a superlattice of period $L = a/(2\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2})$ with $a = 2.46$ Å, in which the stacking varies smoothly from regions where atoms sit on top of each other to regions where they interleave. Electrons tunnelling between the layers feel this landscape. In 2011 Rafi Bistritzer and Allan MacDonald predicted that at particular **magic angles**, the first near 1.1°, the bands near the Fermi level become almost perfectly flat. Their criterion compares the interlayer tunnelling energy $w$ with the kinetic energy $\hbar v_F k_\theta$ across the moiré Brillouin zone, where $k_\theta = 2|\mathbf K|\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}$ is the separation of the two layers' Dirac points; the first flat band appears when $\alpha = w/(\hbar v_F k_\theta)$ is close to $1/\sqrt3$.

```python run
import numpy as np

a = 2.46                     # graphene lattice constant, angstrom
hbar_vf = 1.5 * 2.8 * 1.42   # eV * angstrom, from the tight-binding cone
w = 0.110                    # interlayer tunnelling energy, eV (a typical fitted value)
K = 4 * np.pi / (3 * a)      # distance from G to K, 1/angstrom

print("twist    moire period   atoms per moire cell   alpha = w / (hbar v_F k_theta)")
for deg in (5.0, 3.0, 2.0, 1.5, 1.1, 1.05, 0.8, 0.5):
    th = np.radians(deg)
    L = a / (2 * np.sin(th / 2))                 # moire period
    atoms = 4 * (L / a) ** 2                     # two layers x two atoms x (L/a)^2 cells
    k_theta = 2 * K * np.sin(th / 2)             # separation of the two layers' Dirac points
    alpha = w / (hbar_vf * k_theta)
    print(f"{deg:5.2f} deg  {L / 10:7.1f} nm     {atoms:12,.0f}            {alpha:5.3f}")

# The first magic angle of the Bistritzer-MacDonald model is where alpha is about 1/sqrt(3).
th_magic = 2 * np.arcsin(w * np.sqrt(3) / (hbar_vf * 2 * K))
print(f"\nalpha = 1/sqrt(3) at a twist of {np.degrees(th_magic):.2f} degrees")

# Kinetic versus interaction energy at 1.1 degrees: the flat band's width is a few meV,
# while the Coulomb energy between electrons one moire period apart (screened by hBN, eps ~ 4) is
L = a / (2 * np.sin(np.radians(1.1) / 2)) * 1e-10
coulomb = 1.602e-19 / (4 * np.pi * 8.854e-12 * 4 * L)
print(f"Coulomb energy of two electrons one moire period apart: {1000 * coulomb:.0f} meV")
```

At 1.1° the moiré period is 12.8 nm and each moiré cell holds about 10,850 carbon atoms, far beyond what DFT can treat directly, which is why continuum models like Bistritzer and MacDonald's are the standard tool. The criterion gives a first magic angle of 1.07° for these parameters. The physics that follows is in the last line: a flat band has a bandwidth of only a few meV, while two electrons one moiré period apart repel each other with about 28 meV. When interactions beat kinetic energy, electrons stop behaving as independent particles.

In 2018 Pablo Jarillo-Herrero's group at MIT, with hBN crystals from Kenji Watanabe and Takashi Taniguchi at Japan's National Institute for Materials Science (the source of the clean hBN used in a very large share of the field's devices), reported that magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene becomes a **correlated insulator** at half filling of its flat bands and a **superconductor** nearby, all tunable by a gate. Since then the moiré family has grown: moiré TMD bilayers that act as tunable simulators of the Hubbard model; superconductivity in twisted bilayer WSe₂; and in 2023 the **fractional quantum anomalous Hall effect** — fractionally quantised Hall conductance with no applied magnetic field — in twisted bilayer MoTe₂, reported by groups at the University of Washington and Shanghai Jiao Tong University, followed in 2024 by an MIT group in rhombohedral five-layer graphene aligned with hBN.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Every one of those devices is assembled by hand, and the twist angle is never uniform: across a few micrometres it typically wanders by a few hundredths of a degree, and near 1.1° that changes the moiré period by several per cent and the flat-band physics considerably. Scanning-probe maps of real devices show patchworks of slightly different angles. That is why results are reported device by device, and why reproducibility, not discovery, is often the hard part of moiré physics.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="topology" kind="computation" title="Topological insulators, in outline">

Band theory classifies insulators by their gap. Topology adds a finer classification: two insulators with the same gap can differ in a property of their occupied Bloch states over the whole Brillouin zone that cannot change without closing the gap. The prototype is the **Chern number**.

<Definition term="Berry curvature and Chern number">

For a band with Bloch eigenvectors $|u_\mathbf k\rangle$ over a two-dimensional Brillouin zone, the Berry connection is $\mathbf A(\mathbf k) = i\langle u_\mathbf k|\nabla_\mathbf k u_\mathbf k\rangle$ and the Berry curvature $\Omega(\mathbf k) = \partial_{k_x}A_y - \partial_{k_y}A_x$. The **Chern number** of an isolated band is $C = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_\text{BZ}\Omega(\mathbf k)\,d^2k$, which is always an integer. A two-dimensional insulator whose filled bands have total Chern number $C$ has Hall conductance $\sigma_{xy} = C\,e^2/h$ and $|C|$ chiral conducting channels along each edge.

</Definition>

The integer is the same kind of object as the Euler characteristic of a surface — a global count that a smooth deformation cannot change. In 1988 Duncan Haldane showed that graphene's honeycomb could have $C \neq 0$ with no net magnetic field: add a complex hopping $t_2 e^{\pm i\phi}$ between second neighbours, which breaks time-reversal symmetry. The block computes the Chern number with the lattice method of Fukui, Hatsugai and Suzuki, which multiplies the overlaps of neighbouring eigenvectors around each small plaquette of a discretised zone; the phases sum exactly to $2\pi C$, even on a coarse grid, as long as the gap stays open.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Haldane's model: graphene's honeycomb (hopping t1) plus a sublattice energy difference M and a complex
# second-neighbour hopping t2*exp(i*phi), which breaks time-reversal symmetry without a net magnetic field.
delta = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [np.sqrt(3) / 2, -0.5], [-np.sqrt(3) / 2, -0.5]])   # C-C distance = 1
nnn = np.array([delta[1] - delta[2], delta[2] - delta[0], delta[0] - delta[1]])  # second neighbours
B = 2 * np.pi * np.linalg.inv(np.array([delta[0] - delta[1], delta[0] - delta[2]])).T

def hamiltonian(k, t1, t2, phi, M):
    """2x2 Bloch Hamiltonians for an array of wavevectors k with shape (..., 2)."""
    f = t1 * np.exp(1j * k @ delta.T).sum(-1)
    dz = M - 2 * t2 * np.sin(phi) * np.sin(k @ nnn.T).sum(-1)
    d0 = 2 * t2 * np.cos(phi) * np.cos(k @ nnn.T).sum(-1)
    H = np.empty(k.shape[:-1] + (2, 2), dtype=complex)
    H[..., 0, 0], H[..., 1, 1] = d0 + dz, d0 - dz
    H[..., 0, 1], H[..., 1, 0] = f, np.conj(f)
    return H

def chern_and_gap(t1, t2, phi, M, n=36):
    """Chern number of the lower band by the Fukui-Hatsugai-Suzuki lattice method, and the minimum gap."""
    s = np.arange(n) / n
    k = s[:, None, None] * B[0] + s[None, :, None] * B[1]          # (n, n, 2) grid over the zone
    E, V = np.linalg.eigh(hamiltonian(k, t1, t2, phi, M))
    u = V[..., :, 0]                                               # lower-band eigenvectors
    link = lambda a, b: np.sum(np.conj(a) * b, axis=-1)
    u1, u2 = np.roll(u, -1, axis=0), np.roll(u, -1, axis=1)        # neighbours (periodic zone)
    u12 = np.roll(u1, -1, axis=1)
    F = np.angle(link(u, u1) * link(u1, u12) * link(u12, u2) * link(u2, u))   # Berry flux per plaquette
    return F.sum() / (2 * np.pi), (E[..., 1] - E[..., 0]).min()

t1, t2 = 1.0, 0.1
print(f"critical mass 3*sqrt(3)*t2 = {3 * np.sqrt(3) * t2:.3f} (for phi = 90 degrees)\n")
print(" phi (deg)    M      Chern number   smallest gap")
for phi_deg, M in ((0, 0.2), (0, 0.6), (90, 0.0), (90, 0.3), (90, 0.5), (90, 0.7), (-90, 0.3), (45, 0.2)):
    C, gap = chern_and_gap(t1, t2, np.radians(phi_deg), M)
    C = 0.0 if abs(C) < 5e-4 else C                                # print -0.000 as 0.000
    print(f"  {phi_deg:5d}     {M:4.2f}      {C:+6.3f}        {gap:6.3f}")
```

With $\phi = 0$ (no time-reversal breaking) a sublattice mass opens an ordinary gap and $C = 0$. With $\phi = 90°$ the lower band has $C = +1$ for every mass below the critical value $3\sqrt3\,t_2 = 0.520$, and $C = 0$ above it; reversing $\phi$ gives $C = -1$. Near the transition, at $M = 0.50$, the gap has shrunk to 0.039: the only way to change the integer is to close the gap, which happens at $M = 0.520$. In a real sample, the boundary between a $C = 1$ region and the vacuum ($C = 0$) must therefore host states inside the gap — the edge channels — and they are robust because no local imperfection can change a global integer.

Kane and Mele showed in 2005 that spin–orbit coupling makes graphene two copies of Haldane's model with opposite Chern numbers for the two spins, a **quantum spin Hall insulator** protected by time-reversal symmetry; in graphene the gap is far too small to observe, but in 2007 Laurens Molenkamp's group in Würzburg found the effect in HgTe quantum wells, as Bernevig, Hughes and Zhang had predicted. The three-dimensional versions followed: Bi₂Se₃ and Bi₂Te₃, whose surfaces host a single Dirac cone, seen by photoemission from 2009. In 2013 a team led by Qi-Kun Xue at Tsinghua University observed the **quantum anomalous Hall effect** — Haldane's idea made real — in thin films of magnetically doped (Bi,Sb)₂Te₃, though only below about 30 mK at first; intrinsic magnetic topological insulators such as MnBi₂Te₄ raised the temperatures. As of 2026 the clearest practical prospect is in metrology, where quantum anomalous Hall devices are being explored as resistance standards that need no magnet.

</Section>

<Section id="qubits" kind="human" title="Materials for quantum computing">

Every qubit platform is limited by materials, usually by tiny populations of defects that absorb energy or scramble phase. Lumenary EE's [quantum hardware](/en/ee/quantum-hardware/) covers the devices; Lumenary Chemistry's [quantum computing for chemistry](/en/chemistry/quantum-computing-for-chemistry/) covers what they might compute.

**Superconducting circuits.** Transmon qubits are aluminium or niobium circuits on silicon or sapphire with Al/AlOₓ/Al Josephson junctions, operated near 10 mK. Their energy relaxation is dominated by **two-level systems** — atomic-scale defects, mostly in the amorphous oxides on metal surfaces and at the metal–substrate and substrate–air interfaces — that resonantly absorb microwave photons. Materials work has moved the field: in 2021 a Princeton group showed that **tantalum** films, whose native oxide is thinner and better behaved, gave transmons with coherence times above 0.3 ms; in 2025 the same groups reported tantalum qubits on high-resistivity silicon with lifetimes up to about 1.7 ms. Surface treatments, cleaner substrates, encapsulation layers that prevent oxide growth, and junction fabrication with fewer defects are where coherence is now gained. A second materials problem is **quasiparticles** and ionising radiation: cosmic rays and natural radioactivity deposit energy in the substrate that briefly breaks Cooper pairs across many qubits at once, a correlated error that error correction handles badly; phonon traps and shielding are being engineered against it.

**Defects as qubits.** The **nitrogen–vacancy centre** in diamond (a nitrogen atom next to a missing carbon) has an electron spin that can be initialised and read out with green light at room temperature, with a ground-state splitting of 2.87 GHz; its coherence improves in isotopically purified ¹²C diamond, since ¹³C nuclei carry spins that cause noise. NV centres are excellent sensors of magnetic fields at the nanoscale and were used in 2015 for a loophole-free Bell test at Delft. **Silicon carbide** hosts divacancies and silicon vacancies with similar properties in a material with wafer-scale industrial production. **Silicon** itself, isotopically enriched to more than 99.9 per cent ²⁸Si, which has no nuclear spin, gives electron and nuclear spin qubits in devices close to those of standard chip manufacture, and optically active centres such as the T centre are being developed for networking. For all of these, the materials questions are how to place a single defect where you want it, keep its environment quiet and keep many of them identical.

**Topological qubits, carefully.** Majorana zero modes in superconductor–semiconductor nanowires would store quantum information non-locally and be protected from local noise. The experimental record has been difficult: a 2018 *Nature* paper reporting quantised Majorana conductance was retracted in 2021 after data problems were found, and Microsoft's February 2025 *Nature* paper on interferometric parity measurement in InAs–Al devices was published with an editorial note stating that its results did not represent evidence for Majorana zero modes; Microsoft's accompanying announcement of a topological processor went further than the peer-reviewed paper. Follow-up data in 2025 continued the debate. As of late 2026, a topological qubit has not been demonstrated to the satisfaction of most of the field. The materials challenge — atomically clean semiconductor–superconductor interfaces with very little disorder — is real and is being worked on.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="What is real, what is promising, and what is hype">

Graphene's story is a useful calibration. It was announced in the late 2000s as a material that would transform electronics, batteries, water filtration and aircraft. Twenty years on, graphene and few-layer graphene are in real products — heat-spreading films in phones, conductive additives in battery electrodes and coatings, strengthening additives in polymers and concrete, sensors — sold by companies in China, Europe, North America and elsewhere, and the European Union's ten-year [Graphene Flagship](https://graphene-flagship.eu/) has moved many research results towards industry. It is not in your processor, and much of the "graphene" sold in bulk is in fact multilayer flakes of varying quality, which is why standards bodies developed measurement standards for graphene powders in the early 2020s. The other 2D materials are earlier on the same road.

Two habits protect against hype. First, ask for the number that matters in the application, measured in a device under realistic conditions: a mobility in a transistor with a gate dielectric and contacts, not in a suspended flake at 2 K; a coherence time for a qubit in a processor, not a champion device. Second, wait for independent replication of extraordinary claims. In July 2023 a claim of room-temperature, ambient-pressure superconductivity in a copper-doped lead apatite ("LK-99") was tested by laboratories on several continents within weeks; it was not reproduced, and the observed effects were traced to impurity phases such as copper sulfide. That was science working quickly and in public, and <LessonLink slug="superconductors">Superconductors</LessonLink> tells related stories.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Graphene's electrons travel at the speed of light."** They behave like *massless* particles, with a linear dispersion like light's, but their velocity is about $10^6$ m/s, roughly 1/300 of the speed of light, set by the hopping energy and the bond length.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A topological insulator is an insulator coated with a metal."** Its metallic surface is not a separate layer; it is required by the bulk's topological invariant, and appears at any boundary with an ordinary insulator or vacuum. It cannot be removed without closing the bulk gap or breaking the protecting symmetry.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Any monolayer is a better semiconductor than bulk."** Thinning changes the band structure (MoS₂ goes from indirect to direct gap), but monolayers are also more sensitive to their substrate, contacts, adsorbates and defects, and their room-temperature mobilities are lower than silicon's. Their advantage is electrostatic control at extreme thinness, not mobility.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Quantum materials are a new class of substance."** The term is a shorthand for solids whose useful properties need quantum mechanics beyond the independent-electron band picture: topology, strong correlations, coherence. Many are old materials, such as bismuth telluride, a thermoelectric since the 1950s, looked at with new questions.

</Misconception>
