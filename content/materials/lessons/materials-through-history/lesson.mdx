<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Ages named after materials">

Historians call long stretches of the human past the Stone Age, the Bronze Age and the Iron Age. We name these ages after materials because materials decided what people could do: what tools they could make, what they could build, how they farmed, travelled and fought. Some people say that we now live in a Silicon Age, named after the material inside every computer chip.

This lesson is a journey through that history, across many civilisations. It is not one story but many, because people on different continents discovered different materials, at different times, often quite independently.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here's a question to carry through the whole lesson: why did each new material appear *when* it did? Usually the answer is that people had learned something new about heat, about rocks, or about what atoms do when you mix and heat them. Every age began with someone mastering a hotter fire or a cleverer recipe.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="stone-and-clay" kind="history" title="Stone, clay and fire">

The oldest stone tools known were found in 2015 at Lomekwi, near Lake Turkana in Kenya: sharp flakes struck from stones about **3.3 million years ago**, before our own species existed. For almost all of human history since, stone was the main material for tools. Toolmakers knew their materials well. Flint and obsidian, a natural volcanic glass, are brittle, and when struck correctly they break along smooth, curved surfaces with edges sharper than most steel blades. That is the brittle cracking of <LessonLink slug="atoms-bonds-and-properties">Why materials behave as they do</LessonLink> put to good use. Obsidian was traded over hundreds of kilometres: in Anatolia, in the Ethiopian highlands, and in Mesoamerica, where the great city of Teotihuacan controlled a large obsidian trade.

A great early step was learning to *change* a material, not just shape it, and clay is the clearest example. Wet clay is soft; heated in a fire, it changes for good into hard, waterproof **ceramic**, because heat joins its tiny mineral grains together. The oldest pottery yet found, from Xianrendong Cave in southern China, is about **20,000 years old**, and the Jōmon people of Japan were making pots more than 15,000 years ago. Bricks followed: sun-dried mud bricks, and later fired bricks, laid in the carefully planned streets of the Indus Valley cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro about 4,500 years ago. Mud brick is still a superb building material in hot, dry places; the Great Mosque of Djenné in Mali is the largest mud-brick building in the world.

</Section>

<Section id="metals" kind="history" title="Copper, bronze and iron">

**Copper** is one of the few metals that can be found as lumps of pure metal. People around the Great Lakes of North America hammered native copper into tools and ornaments thousands of years ago. But most copper is locked in rocks called **ores**, combined with other elements. About 7,000 years ago, at Belovode in what is now Serbia, people were already **smelting**: heating copper ore with charcoal in a very hot fire so that the copper was set free from the rock. Smelting was probably discovered in more than one place.

**Bronze** is copper with a little tin added, usually about one part tin to nine parts copper. It is much harder than copper, holds a sharper edge, and flows easily into moulds when molten. From about 5,000 years ago bronze spread across Southwest Asia, Egypt and the Indus Valley, and the bronze workers of Shang-dynasty China cast enormous, intricate ritual vessels over 3,000 years ago. Much later, about 1,100 years ago, artists at Igbo-Ukwu in Nigeria cast bronzes so delicate that experts still marvel at how they were made, and in the Andes, Inca metalworkers used tin bronze for tools and ornaments. Tin, though, is rare, found in only a few places, so bronze depended on long trade routes.

**Iron** ore, by contrast, is found almost everywhere. But iron is harder to win from its ore: it needs a hotter, better-controlled furnace, and the result was at first a spongy lump that had to be hammered while hot to drive out the slag. Early iron was not even harder than good bronze. Its great advantage was that it was *common*, so tools could be made for everyone, not just the rich. Smiths then learned that iron with a little carbon absorbed from the charcoal, which we call **steel**, becomes much harder, especially when it is plunged red-hot into water. Ironworking spread around Anatolia and the eastern Mediterranean from about 3,200 years ago. In West Africa, people of the Nok culture in Nigeria were smelting iron by about 2,500 years ago; around the same time, Chinese ironworkers built furnaces hot enough to melt iron and pour it into moulds, more than a thousand years before Europe could. Smiths in South India and Sri Lanka made a famous high-carbon steel, **wootz**, traded as far as the Middle East, and the iron pillar at Delhi, about 1,600 years old and seven metres tall, has hardly rusted in all that time.

Smelting, casting and forging all use furnaces and molten metal, which is why they belong in foundries, workshops and laboratories with trained people and protective equipment. Archaeologists who rebuild ancient furnaces to learn how they worked do so with exactly that care.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Careful: the neat sequence "Stone Age, Bronze Age, Iron Age" was invented in the 1830s by the Danish museum curator Christian Jürgensen Thomsen to sort the objects in his collection, and it fits Europe and Southwest Asia far better than anywhere else. In much of Africa south of the Sahara, people went from stone tools straight to iron, with no bronze age. In the Americas people worked gold, silver, copper and bronze, but did not smelt iron before the 1500s. And the ages always overlapped: stone tools stayed in use long after metal arrived.

</Narrator>

<Section id="industrial" kind="history" title="Steel, concrete and the industrial revolution">

For thousands of years steel was precious, made in small batches by skilled smiths. The industrial revolution changed that. In 1709, at Coalbrookdale in England, Abraham Darby found how to smelt iron using coke, made from coal, instead of charcoal, which needed whole forests. Then in 1856 Henry Bessemer showed that blowing air through molten iron burns out excess carbon and makes steel in minutes rather than days; in the United States, William Kelly had experimented with the same idea. Steel became cheap. Railways, steamships, bridges and the steel frames of the first skyscrapers followed. Steelworks spread around the world: the Yawata works in Japan began making steel in 1901, and the Tata Iron and Steel Company was founded at Jamshedpur in India in 1907. Today steel is still the backbone of construction, and it is the most recycled material in the world.

Concrete has a longer story. Roman builders mixed lime with volcanic ash, water and stones to make a concrete that set hard, even under the sea. The dome of the Pantheon in Rome, about 1,900 years old, is still the largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world. Modern **Portland cement** was patented in England by Joseph Aspdin in 1824, and in 1867 the French gardener Joseph Monier patented flowerpots of concrete with iron mesh inside: the start of **reinforced concrete**, in which steel takes the pulling and concrete the squashing. Concrete is now the most used manufactured material on Earth, and making its cement releases a great deal of carbon dioxide, a problem you will meet in <LessonLink slug="choosing-and-reusing-materials">Choosing and reusing materials</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="plastics" kind="history" title="Plastics: polymers made to order">

People used natural polymers long before anyone knew what a polymer was. In Mesoamerica, about 3,600 years ago, people mixed the milky sap of the rubber tree with the juice of a morning-glory vine to make bouncing rubber balls for their ball games. In India, lac, a resin made by tiny insects, was used for varnish and sealing wax for many centuries. Horn, amber, silk and natural rubber were all polymers.

The first plastics were made by changing natural polymers: Alexander Parkes showed Parkesine, made from plant cellulose, in London in 1862. In 1907 the Belgian-born chemist Leo Baekeland, working in New York, made **Bakelite**, the first fully synthetic plastic, hard, heat-resistant and electrically insulating, perfect for telephones and plugs. Polyethylene was discovered by accident at the ICI company in England in 1933, and Wallace Carothers's team at DuPont in the United States invented nylon in 1935. In the 1950s Karl Ziegler in Germany and Giulio Natta in Italy found catalysts that made polyethylene and polypropylene cheap to produce, and shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Plastics are now made in hundreds of millions of tonnes a year: they keep food fresh, make medicine safe and cars lighter, and they also leave behind the waste problem you will meet in the next lesson.

</Section>

<Section id="silicon" kind="history" title="Silicon and the information age">

The materials of the information age are chosen not for strength but for what they do with electricity and light. In 1947 John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley at Bell Labs in the United States made the first **transistor**, a tiny electrical switch, from a crystal of germanium. Silicon soon took over: the first silicon transistors came from Texas Instruments in 1954, and in 1959 Mohamed Atalla, born in Egypt, and Dawon Kahng, born in Korea, made the kind of silicon transistor now found by the billion in every chip. In 1958 and 1959 Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce built the first **integrated circuits**, many parts on one piece of semiconductor.

None of this works without astonishingly perfect materials. Chips are made on wafers sliced from single crystals of silicon, grown by slowly pulling a seed crystal out of molten silicon, a method invented by the Polish chemist Jan Czochralski in 1916. The silicon must be better than 99.9999999% pure: one stray atom in a billion is already a lot. Other materials joined the revolution: glass fibres so clear that light travels kilometres through them, proposed by Charles Kao in 1966 and now carrying the internet (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2009); the gallium nitride crystals of blue light-emitting diodes, perfected in Japan by Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2014); and the electrode materials of the lithium-ion battery (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2019). Lumenary EE's [semiconductors and diodes](/en/ee/semiconductors-and-diodes/) explains how these devices work, and you will meet the materials in <LessonLink slug="semiconductor-materials">Semiconductor materials</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="battery-materials">Battery materials</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="world" kind="examples" title="Materials that changed the world">

Many more materials changed history, from every part of the world. Here are a few.

| Material | Where and when | What it changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glass | Mesopotamia, about 4,500 years ago; glassblowing in the eastern Mediterranean, about 2,000 years ago | beads, then cheap bottles, windows, lenses and, much later, telescopes and microscopes |
| Egyptian blue | Egypt, more than 4,500 years ago | the first synthetic pigment, made by heating sand, copper and lime together |
| Paper | China, about 2,000 years ago, improved by the official Cai Lun around 105 CE | cheap writing material; it spread through Samarkand and Baghdad to the rest of the world |
| Porcelain | China, more than 1,000 years ago | hard, white, translucent ceramic, so prized that in English "china" became its name |
| Maya blue | Mesoamerica, more than 1,000 years ago | a blue paint of indigo dye locked inside clay that has survived centuries in the rain |
| Wootz steel | South India and Sri Lanka, about 2,000 years ago | famous high-carbon steel for blades, traded across Asia |
| Bamboo | East, South and Southeast Asia, for thousands of years | a natural composite, light and strong, used for houses, bridges and scaffolding to this day |

<Narrator who="margaret">

Here's a way to check "about 2,000 years ago" against a date: 2026 − 105 = 1921, so paper as Cai Lun improved it is about 1,900 years old. Archaeologists and historians date things with care, using written records, layers in the ground and radiocarbon dating of charcoal from ancient fires. Even so, every "first" in this lesson is only the oldest example found *so far*: a new dig can push it back.

</Narrator>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="All of history in one day">

The timeline above needed a stretched scale to fit everything in. Here is another way to feel the sizes of these times: squeeze the whole 3.3 million years, from the first stone tools to today, into a single day of 24 hours, starting at midnight. When does each material appear? Press **Run**:

```python run
# Squeeze the whole history of human materials, from the oldest known
# stone tools to today, into a single day of 24 hours.
# Dates are "about how many years ago", counted back from 2026.
events = [
    ("oldest known stone tools (Kenya)",          3_300_000),
    ("oldest known pottery (China)",                 20_000),
    ("copper smelted from ore (Serbia)",              7_000),
    ("bronze in common use (Southwest Asia)",         5_000),
    ("iron spreads (Anatolia and beyond)",            3_200),
    ("Pantheon's concrete dome (Rome)",               1_900),
    ("cheap steel: Bessemer's process (England)",       170),
    ("first fully synthetic plastic (USA)",             119),
    ("silicon integrated circuit (USA)",                 67),
]

DAY = 24 * 60 * 60                 # seconds in a day
start = events[0][1]               # the day begins with the first stone tools

def clock(years_ago):
    """The time of day at which an event happens, as hours:minutes:seconds."""
    seconds_before_midnight = DAY * years_ago / start
    t = round(DAY - seconds_before_midnight)
    return f"{t // 3600:02d}:{t % 3600 // 60:02d}:{t % 60:02d}", seconds_before_midnight

print("event                                        time of day   before midnight")
for name, years_ago in events:
    time, before = clock(years_ago)
    if before >= 60:
        gap = f"{before / 60:6.1f} minutes"
    else:
        gap = f"{before:6.1f} seconds"
    print(f"{name:44} {time}   {gap}")

print()
print(f"One second of this day stands for {start / DAY:.0f} years.")
```

On this clock, people use stone tools all day and all night. Pottery turns up at 23:51, less than nine minutes before midnight. Copper smelting arrives about three minutes before midnight, bronze and iron in the last two and a half minutes, and the Pantheon's concrete dome less than a minute before the end. Cheap steel, plastics and silicon chips all squeeze into the last **five seconds**. Each second of this day stands for about 38 years, a little more than a human generation.

The program shows something true about materials: change has sped up enormously. That is not because people became cleverer. It is because each discovery made the next ones easier, and because far more people now work on materials, sharing what they learn, than ever before.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How we know: materials science meets archaeology">

How do we know that copper was smelted at Belovode 7,000 years ago? Not from writing, because there was none. Archaeologists found green copper ore, lumps of **slag** (the glassy waste left after smelting) and charcoal. Materials scientists then examined the slag under microscopes and analysed the elements in it, which showed that it could only have come from smelting, while radiocarbon dating of the charcoal gave its age. This field, studying ancient objects with the tools of materials science, is called **archaeometallurgy** or, more broadly, archaeological science.

The same tools solve puzzles that matter today. In 2023 a team led from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology studied small white lumps of lime in Roman concrete. They showed that the Romans mixed in quicklime hot, and that when a crack lets water in, the lime dissolves and refills the crack with fresh mineral: Roman concrete partly heals itself. Engineers are now testing whether modern concrete could do the same. Conservation scientists in museums from Cairo to Beijing use X-rays and microscopes to learn how objects were made and how to keep them safe; you will meet those instruments in <LessonLink slug="x-ray-diffraction">X-ray diffraction</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="microscopy">Microscopy</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Stone Age people were primitive."** Stone toolmakers had a deep, practical knowledge of how brittle materials crack, which they used to make blades sharper than most steel. They also found and traded the best stone over great distances, and invented pottery, a genuinely new material.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Iron replaced bronze because it was better from the start."** Early iron was often softer than good bronze. Iron won because its ore is found almost everywhere, while tin is rare, and because smiths later learned to turn iron into hard steel.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every part of the world went through the same ages at the same time."** The ages came at very different times in different places, overlapped, and in some regions were skipped altogether: much of Africa went straight from stone to iron.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Plastics are a modern invention."** Synthetic plastics are modern, but people have used natural polymers, such as rubber, lac, horn and silk, for thousands of years.

</Misconception>

<Reflection title="skill as a gift">

In the book of Exodus, a craftsman named Bezalel is described as filled with the Spirit of God, with wisdom, understanding and skill to work in gold, silver and bronze, to cut stones and to carve wood. It is one of the oldest texts to honour the knowledge of materials as a gift worth celebrating. The smiths, potters and glassmakers of every culture in this lesson shared that patient skill, and so do materials scientists today.
