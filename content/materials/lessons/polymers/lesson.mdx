<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="One idea, a thousand materials">

The world makes a little over 400 million tonnes of plastics a year ([Plastics Europe](https://plasticseurope.org/)), more by volume than steel. They are carrier bags and car bumpers, pipes and paints, contact lenses and carpet fibres, and the ropes that moor ships, which by weight are stronger than steel wire. Living things are built from polymers too: cellulose, proteins, DNA, natural rubber.

All of them rest on one idea, which took chemists until the 1920s to accept: a **polymer** is a single molecule made of thousands of small units joined by covalent bonds into a chain. Hermann Staudinger argued for it in 1920 against strong opposition ([Staudinger](https://doi.org/10.1002/cber.19200530627)) and received the Nobel Prize for it in 1953. Within a few decades came nylon, polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene and PET, and the plastics age.

What makes this family so varied is that the same backbone, arranged differently, gives different materials. Polyethylene can be a soft film, a stiff milk bottle or a fibre up to fifteen times stronger than steel per kilogram, depending on how its chains are branched, how long they are, and how they are packed. This lesson follows that thread: chains, then their lengths, then how they pack and move, and finally what happens to them at the end of their use. The chemistry of making them — addition and condensation polymerisation — is in Lumenary Chemistry's [carbonyls and aromatics](/en/chemistry/carbonyls-and-aromatics/) and [carbon and functional groups](/en/chemistry/carbon-and-functional-groups/).

</Section>

<Section id="chains" kind="definitions" title="Chains: architecture and shape">

<Definition term="polymer, repeat unit, degree of polymerisation">

A **polymer** is a molecule made of many **repeat units** joined by covalent bonds. The number of repeat units in a chain is its **degree of polymerisation**, $X$; its molar mass is $M = X M_0$, where $M_0$ is the molar mass of the repeat unit. A **copolymer** contains two or more kinds of repeat unit, arranged at random, alternately, in long blocks, or as side chains grafted onto a backbone.

</Definition>

Architecture matters as much as chemistry. Branches keep polyethylene chains from packing closely: low-density polyethylene (LDPE), with many branches, is soft and transparent, while nearly linear high-density polyethylene (HDPE) packs into stiffer, more crystalline solids. **Tacticity** — whether side groups along the chain all point the same way (isotactic), alternate (syndiotactic) or are random (atactic) — decides whether chains can crystallise at all: isotactic polypropylene crystallises and melts at about 165 °C, atactic polypropylene is a sticky, amorphous goo. The catalysts that gave chemists control over branching and tacticity, discovered by Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta in the 1950s, made HDPE and polypropylene the largest-volume plastics today.

Left alone in a melt or a solution, a long chain does not lie straight. Each bond can rotate, so the chain wanders like a random walk and folds itself into a loose **random coil**. How big is the coil? A simulation answers.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Freely jointed chains: N bonds of length b, each pointing in a random direction in 3D.
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
b, chains = 1.0, 2000
print("    N   <R^2>/(N b^2)   R_rms/b   sqrt(N)   contour/R_rms")
for N in (10, 100, 1000):
    v = rng.normal(size=(chains, N, 3))
    v /= np.linalg.norm(v, axis=2, keepdims=True)        # unit bond vectors, uniform on a sphere
    R = v.sum(axis=1) * b                                # end-to-end vectors
    R2 = (R**2).sum(axis=1).mean()
    print(f"{N:5d} {R2 / (N * b**2):12.3f} {np.sqrt(R2):10.1f} {np.sqrt(N):9.1f} {N * b / np.sqrt(R2):12.1f}")

# A real polyethylene chain: bond angles and rotations stiffen it, which multiplies <R^2>
# by the characteristic ratio C (about 7 for polyethylene).
M, l, C = 100_000, 0.154, 7.0               # g/mol, C-C bond length (nm), characteristic ratio
n = 2 * M / 28.05                           # two backbone bonds per CH2-CH2 repeat unit
print(f"\nPE, M = {M:,} g/mol: {n:,.0f} bonds, coil size R_rms = {np.sqrt(C * n) * l:.0f} nm, "
      f"fully stretched (zig-zag) length = {n * l * np.sin(np.radians(109.47 / 2)) / 1000:.2f} um")
```

The mean-square end-to-end distance is $\langle R^2\rangle = N b^2$, so the coil's size grows only as $\sqrt N$: a chain of 1000 bonds spans about 31 bond lengths, not 1000. A real polyethylene molecule of 100 kg/mol, with 7130 backbone bonds, forms a coil about 34 nm across but would be 0.90 µm long if pulled straight — some 27 times longer. Coils of neighbouring chains interpenetrate and **entangle**, like cooked spaghetti, and entanglement is what gives polymer melts their high viscosity and solid plastics their toughness.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The random coil is a state of maximum entropy: there are vastly more crumpled shapes than straight ones. Stretch a rubber band and you pull its chains towards the few straight shapes; let go and they return to the coil, not because any bond is strained but because of statistics. That is why the force in a rubber band at fixed stretch *rises* with temperature, the opposite of a metal spring, and why its modulus, about $G = \rho RT/M_c$, is tiny: with 5 kg/mol between cross-links, $G \approx 0.46$ MPa, thousands of times softer than a glassy plastic.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="molar-mass" kind="computation" title="Molar-mass distributions">

A polymer sample is not one molecule but billions, of many lengths. So we describe it by averages:

$$M_n = \frac{\sum_i N_i M_i}{\sum_i N_i}, \qquad M_w = \frac{\sum_i N_i M_i^2}{\sum_i N_i M_i}, \qquad Đ = \frac{M_w}{M_n},$$

where $N_i$ chains have molar mass $M_i$. The **number average** $M_n$ is the total mass divided by the number of chains; the **weight average** $M_w$ weights each chain by its mass and so is pulled up by the long ones. Their ratio, the **dispersity** $Đ$, is 1 only if every chain is the same length. Properties follow different averages: colligative properties and chain-end effects follow $M_n$, while melt viscosity, which grows roughly as $M_w^{3.4}$ above the entanglement threshold, is dominated by the longest chains.

In **step-growth** (condensation) polymerisation — how PET, nylons and many polyesters are made — any two molecules can react, and chains grow by joining. Wallace Carothers, who invented nylon at DuPont in 1935, showed that the average degree of polymerisation depends on the fraction $p$ of end groups that have reacted: $X_n = 1/(1-p)$. Paul Flory derived the whole distribution.

```python run
import numpy as np

def averages(N, M):
    """Number- and weight-average molar mass, and dispersity, from chain counts N_i of mass M_i."""
    N, M = np.asarray(N, dtype=float), np.asarray(M, dtype=float)
    Mn = (N * M).sum() / N.sum()
    Mw = (N * M**2).sum() / (N * M).sum()
    return Mn, Mw, Mw / Mn

# A blend: equal NUMBERS of chains of 10, 50 and 200 kg/mol.
Mn, Mw, D = averages([1, 1, 1], [10_000, 50_000, 200_000])
print(f"blend: Mn = {Mn:,.0f}, Mw = {Mw:,.0f} g/mol, dispersity {D:.2f}")

# Step-growth (condensation) polymerisation of a hydroxy-acid, repeat unit M0, to extent p.
# Flory: the number fraction of chains with n units is (1 - p) p^(n - 1).
M0 = 72.06                                   # lactic acid repeat unit, g/mol
n = np.arange(1, 200_001)
print("\n    p      Xn = 1/(1-p)   Mn (sum)   Mw (sum)   Mw (formula)   dispersity")
for p in (0.90, 0.98, 0.99, 0.995):
    frac = (1 - p) * p ** (n - 1)
    Mn, Mw, D = averages(frac, n * M0)
    print(f"{p:7.3f} {1 / (1 - p):12.0f} {Mn:10,.0f} {Mw:10,.0f} {M0 * (1 + p) / (1 - p):13,.0f} {D:10.3f}")
```

The blend shows how far apart the averages can be: $M_n$ is 86,667 g/mol but $M_w$ is 163,846, a dispersity of 1.89, because the longest chains carry most of the mass. The step-growth table shows the harsh arithmetic of condensation: even at 99 % conversion the chains average only 100 units (7,206 g/mol for poly(lactic acid)), too short to be useful, and the dispersity approaches 2, matching Flory's formula $M_w = M_0(1+p)/(1-p)$ exactly. That is why condensation polymers need very pure monomers in exact proportions, why water must be removed to drive the reaction on, and why high-molar-mass poly(lactic acid) is made instead by opening the ring of its cyclic dimer, lactide. **Chain-growth** polymerisation, in which monomers add one at a time to a few active chain ends, reaches high molar mass at once; "living" chain-growth methods give dispersities close to 1.

<Narrator who="margaret">

How are these averages measured? In **size-exclusion chromatography** a dissolved sample flows through a column packed with porous beads; large coils cannot enter the pores and come out first, small ones wander through the pores and come out later. Calibrated with standards, or fitted with a light-scattering detector that measures molar mass directly, the trace of concentration against elution time *is* the distribution, from which $M_n$, $M_w$ and $Đ$ are computed exactly as above.

</Narrator>

<Section id="families" kind="definitions" title="Thermoplastics, thermosets and elastomers">

<Definition term="thermoplastic, thermoset, elastomer">

A **thermoplastic** is made of separate chains held together only by entanglement and weak intermolecular forces; it softens on heating and can be melted and reshaped repeatedly. A **thermoset** is a densely cross-linked network formed by an irreversible curing reaction; it does not melt, and decomposes if heated too far. An **elastomer** is a lightly cross-linked network above its glass transition, able to stretch several times its length and spring back.

</Definition>

Most of the tonnage is thermoplastic: polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, PET and polystyrene alone are about two-thirds of all plastics. Thermosets — epoxies, phenolics (Bakelite, from 1907, was the first fully synthetic plastic), unsaturated polyesters and polyurethanes — are chosen where heat resistance, dimensional stability or adhesion matters, and as the matrix of most <LessonLink slug="composites">composites</LessonLink>. Elastomers became engineering materials when Charles Goodyear found in 1839 that heating natural rubber with sulfur cross-links it, turning a sticky gum into tyre material. **Thermoplastic elastomers**, such as styrene–butadiene–styrene block copolymers, get the effect of cross-links from glassy polystyrene domains that hold rubbery butadiene chains together at room temperature but soften when heated, so they can be moulded like thermoplastics.

</Section>

<Section id="glass-transition" kind="intuition" title="Crystallinity and the glass transition">

Polymer chains are too long and tangled to crystallise completely. The best a regular chain can do is fold back and forth into thin **lamellae**, about 10 nm thick, which grow outwards from a nucleus into spherical **spherulites**, with disordered, amorphous chain segments between the lamellae. Such a polymer is **semicrystalline**, and its crystallinity — from about 40 % in LDPE to over 80 % in some HDPE — sets its stiffness, its density and its barrier to gases. Irregular chains (atactic polystyrene, PMMA, polycarbonate) cannot crystallise at all and stay **amorphous**, which is why they are transparent.

Because crystals are denser, crystallinity can be measured with a balance. If $\rho_a$ and $\rho_c$ are the densities of the fully amorphous and fully crystalline polymer, the mass fraction crystallinity of a sample of density $\rho$ is

$$\chi_c = \frac{\rho_c(\rho - \rho_a)}{\rho(\rho_c - \rho_a)}.$$

For polyethylene, $\rho_a \approx 0.855$ and $\rho_c \approx 1.00$ g/cm³, so an HDPE of density 0.960 g/cm³ is about 75 % crystalline, and an LDPE of 0.920 g/cm³ about 49 %.

The amorphous parts of every polymer pass through a **glass transition** at a temperature $T_g$. Below it, chain segments are frozen in place and the polymer is a rigid glass with a modulus of a few GPa. Above it, segments of a few dozen bonds can rearrange; the amorphous polymer becomes leathery and then rubbery, its modulus falling by a factor of about a thousand, and an uncross-linked one eventually flows. The glass transition is not a phase transition with a fixed temperature like melting; it is the temperature at which molecular rearrangement becomes too slow to follow the experiment, so it shifts by a few degrees with the cooling rate.

The figure explains which polymers are useful where. Amorphous thermoplastics are used below $T_g$ (polystyrene and polycarbonate are rigid at room temperature because their $T_g$ is well above it). Elastomers are used above $T_g$ (natural rubber's is about −70 °C). Semicrystalline polymers work between $T_g$ and $T_m$, where the crystals give stiffness and the rubbery amorphous regions give toughness: polyethylene and polypropylene at room temperature are exactly this. **Plasticisers**, small molecules mixed between chains, lower $T_g$; they are how rigid PVC becomes a flexible hose or cable sheath.

</Section>

<Section id="viscoelasticity" kind="computation" title="Viscoelasticity">

Hang a weight from a plastic strip and it keeps stretching for hours; stretch a strip to a fixed length and the force needed to hold it slowly falls. These are **creep** and **stress relaxation**, and polymers show them even at room temperature, because chain segments keep rearranging under load. A polymer is neither an elastic solid nor a viscous liquid but **viscoelastic**, and the simplest models combine a spring (modulus $E$, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$) with a dashpot (viscosity $\eta$, $\sigma = \eta\,\dot\varepsilon$).

In the **Maxwell model** the spring and dashpot carry the same stress and their strains add: $\dot\varepsilon = \dot\sigma/E + \sigma/\eta$. Under a constant strain the stress relaxes as $\sigma = \sigma_0 e^{-t/\tau}$ with **relaxation time** $\tau = \eta/E$; under a constant stress it creeps without limit. In the **Kelvin–Voigt model** they share the strain and their stresses add: $\sigma = E\varepsilon + \eta\dot\varepsilon$. Under constant stress it creeps towards $\sigma/E$ as $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$; but it cannot relax, because a sudden strain would need an infinite force on the dashpot. Here both are stepped forward in time numerically and checked against the exact solutions.

```python run
import numpy as np

E, eta = 1.0e3, 1.0e5            # spring modulus (MPa) and dashpot viscosity (MPa s)
tau = eta / E                    # relaxation (or retardation) time: 100 s
dt, T = 0.01, 500.0
t = np.arange(0.0, T + dt / 2, dt)

# Creep: a stress of 10 MPa applied at t = 0 and held. Step each model's ODE forward in time.
s0 = 10.0
eps_M = np.empty_like(t); eps_M[0] = s0 / E          # Maxwell: d(eps)/dt = (d sigma/dt)/E + sigma/eta
eps_K = np.empty_like(t); eps_K[0] = 0.0             # Kelvin-Voigt: sigma = E eps + eta d(eps)/dt
for k in range(len(t) - 1):
    eps_M[k + 1] = eps_M[k] + dt * s0 / eta
    eps_K[k + 1] = eps_K[k] + dt * (s0 - E * eps_K[k]) / eta

# Relaxation: a strain of 1 % applied at t = 0 and held; Maxwell: d(sigma)/dt = -sigma/tau.
sig_M = np.empty_like(t); sig_M[0] = E * 0.01
for k in range(len(t) - 1):
    sig_M[k + 1] = sig_M[k] - dt * sig_M[k] / tau

print("  t (s)   creep strain (%)          relaxation stress (MPa)")
print("          Maxwell  Kelvin-Voigt     Maxwell  (exact)  Kelvin-Voigt")
for ti in (0, 10, 50, 100, 200, 500):
    k = int(round(ti / dt))
    print(f"{ti:6d} {100 * eps_M[k]:9.3f} {100 * eps_K[k]:10.3f} {sig_M[k]:14.3f} "
          f"{E * 0.01 * np.exp(-ti / tau):8.3f} {E * 0.01:10.3f}")
print(f"Kelvin-Voigt exact at 100 s: {100 * s0 / E * (1 - np.exp(-1)):.3f} %")

# Time-temperature superposition: above Tg, every relaxation time is multiplied by the same
# shift factor a_T, which the WLF equation gives with its "universal" constants.
Tg, tau_g = 100.0, 100.0         # e.g. polystyrene; a relaxation time of ~100 s at Tg
print("\n T (C)   log10 a_T   relaxation time")
for T in (100, 105, 110, 120, 140, 160):
    log_aT = -17.44 * (T - Tg) / (51.6 + T - Tg) + 0.0   # (+ 0.0 avoids printing -0.00)
    print(f"{T:5.0f} {log_aT:10.2f} {tau_g * 10**log_aT:13.2e} s")
```

After one relaxation time (100 s) the Maxwell stress has fallen to $1/e$ of its start, 3.679 MPa, and the Kelvin–Voigt strain has reached 63.2 % of its limit, both matching the exact solutions. The Maxwell model captures relaxation and flow (a melt), the Kelvin–Voigt model bounded, recoverable creep (a cross-linked solid); real polymers need both, and the **standard linear solid**, a spring in parallel with a Maxwell element, is the simplest model that shows an instant elastic response, bounded creep and relaxation to a finite stress. Engineering models use many Maxwell elements with a spread of relaxation times, a **Prony series**, fitted to measured data and built into finite-element codes.

The second table is the **Williams–Landel–Ferry** (WLF) equation, $\log_{10} a_T = -C_1(T - T_g)/(C_2 + T - T_g)$ with $C_1 \approx 17.44$ and $C_2 \approx 51.6$ K ([Williams, Landel and Ferry](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja01619a008)). Heating a polymer 10 °C above $T_g$ speeds every relaxation up nearly a thousand times; 40 °C above, about forty million times. That is **time–temperature superposition**: a one-hour test at a raised temperature can stand in for years at service temperature, which is how plastic pipes are rated for fifty years of service.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The WLF equation holds from $T_g$ to about $T_g + 100$ °C, for amorphous polymers in equilibrium. Below $T_g$ the glass is out of equilibrium and slowly densifies (**physical ageing**), so its creep rate depends on how long ago it was made; in semicrystalline polymers the crystals change with temperature, so the shift is not universal. Extrapolating creep data across those boundaries is a classic way to under-design a plastic part, which is why pressure-pipe standards require long tests at several temperatures, not one.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="additives" kind="human" title="Additives">

Almost no plastic is a pure polymer. **Stabilisers** protect it during processing and use: antioxidants (hindered phenols and phosphites) interrupt the radical chain reactions of oxidation, and ultraviolet absorbers and hindered-amine light stabilisers protect outdoor plastics from sunlight. **Plasticisers**, up to a third or more of the mass of flexible PVC, lower $T_g$; several phthalate plasticisers are now restricted in toys and childcare articles in the European Union, the United States and elsewhere because of evidence of harm, and are being replaced. **Fillers and reinforcements** — talc, calcium carbonate, glass fibres — add stiffness or reduce cost. **Impact modifiers**, small rubber particles dispersed in a glassy matrix, make high-impact polystyrene and ABS tough by setting off many tiny crazes instead of one crack. **Flame retardants**, **pigments** (titanium dioxide for white, carbon black for black and for UV protection) and **antistatic and slip agents** complete the list.

Additives are why two bottles of "the same" polymer behave differently, and they are a central problem in recycling: they cannot be removed by melting, they accumulate when different streams are mixed, and some legacy additives, such as certain brominated flame retardants, keep old plastics out of new food-contact and toy applications.

</Section>

<Section id="end-of-life" kind="human" title="Degradation and recycling">

Polymers degrade in a few main ways. **Thermo-oxidation** and **photo-oxidation** are radical chain reactions: a radical on the chain picks up oxygen, steals a hydrogen from a neighbour, and the resulting hydroperoxides break down to new radicals and cut chains, so the plastic yellows, cracks at the surface and becomes brittle. **Hydrolysis** attacks the ester and amide bonds of condensation polymers (PET, polyamides, polylactic acid), which is why PET is dried before melting. Polyolefins, with only carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen bonds, do not hydrolyse at all, which makes them durable in use and persistent afterwards: they fragment into **microplastics** long before they are mineralised.

"Biodegradable" therefore depends on conditions. Poly(lactic acid) breaks down in an industrial composter at about 58 °C, where it is first hydrolysed, but very slowly in a garden heap or the sea. Some polyhydroxyalkanoates, made by bacteria, do degrade in soil and seawater. And "bio-based" is a different property altogether: polyethylene made from sugar-cane ethanol is chemically identical to fossil polyethylene and just as persistent.

Of all plastic waste, only about 9 % was recycled worldwide, around 19 % incinerated and about half landfilled, with the rest mismanaged or leaked into the environment, according to the OECD's Global Plastics Outlook of 2022 (a similar picture to [Geyer, Jambeck and Law](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1700782)). **Mechanical recycling** — sort, wash, shred, melt, re-form — works well for clean single-polymer streams such as PET bottles and HDPE containers. Its limits are materials science: every melting cuts some chains, lowering $M_w$ and toughness; mixed polymers are immiscible and form weak blends; multilayer packaging cannot be separated; and contamination and additives restrict what recycled resin can be used for. Resin codes 1 to 7 identify the polymer, but do not mean that a local scheme accepts it. **Chemical recycling**, which takes polymers back to monomers or oils, is covered in Lumenary Chemistry's [sustainable chemistry](/en/chemistry/sustainable-chemistry/); and design for recycling — one polymer per package, washable labels, no carbon-black pigment that sorting machines cannot see — is part of <LessonLink slug="sustainable-materials">sustainable materials</LessonLink>.

<Narrator who="margaret">

You can follow recycling damage with the averages from earlier. If each pass through an extruder cuts on average one bond in every original chain at random, $M_n$ halves after the first pass and falls to a third after the second, since each scission adds one chain to the count. Melt-flow index, a quick factory measurement of how much melt passes through a standard die in ten minutes, rises as $M_w$ falls, and is the number recyclers watch.

</Narrator>

As of 2026, governments are negotiating a global treaty on plastic pollution under the UN Environment Programme ([UNEP](https://www.unep.org/inc-plastic-pollution)); sessions in Busan (2024) and Geneva (2025) ended without an agreed text, with production limits the main point of disagreement.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="reading" title="Polymers in industry and research">

**Processing.** Thermoplastics are shaped by extrusion (pipes, film, fibres), injection moulding (most solid parts), blow moulding (bottles), film blowing and thermoforming, and by material-extrusion 3D printing (see [manufacturing processes](/en/me/manufacturing-processes/) and [additive manufacturing](/en/me/additive-manufacturing/) in Lumenary ME). Each leaves the chains oriented in the flow direction, so the same polymer can be stiffer one way than another.

**Characterisation.** Size-exclusion chromatography gives the molar-mass distribution, differential scanning calorimetry gives $T_g$, $T_m$ and crystallinity (from the heat of melting), dynamic mechanical analysis gives the viscoelastic moduli against temperature and frequency, and rheometers measure melt flow. You will meet the thermal methods in <LessonLink slug="spectroscopy-and-thermal-analysis">spectroscopy and thermal analysis</LessonLink>.

**Research in 2026.** Networks with exchangeable bonds, such as **vitrimers** ([Montarnal et al.](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1212648)), behave as thermosets in use but can be reshaped and repaired when heated, an approach to recyclable thermosets and composites. Polymers designed for chemical recycling back to monomer, bacterial and enzymatic depolymerisation, sequence-controlled and bio-based polymers, polymer electrolytes for batteries, and machine-learning models of polymer properties are all active fields.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Plastic is one material."** Polyethylene and polycarbonate differ as much as lead and titanium. Even one polymer, polyethylene, ranges from soft film to fibres stronger than steel per kilogram, depending on branching, molar mass and processing.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The glass transition is a kind of melting."** Melting is a first-order phase transition of crystals at a sharp temperature. The glass transition is a kinetic freezing of the amorphous regions, with no latent heat and a temperature that depends on how fast it is measured. Amorphous polymers have a $T_g$ but no $T_m$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Biodegradable plastics disappear anywhere."** Many need the heat, moisture and microbes of an industrial composter. And bio-based is not the same as biodegradable.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A recycling symbol means it will be recycled."** The resin identification code says which polymer an item is made of. Whether it is collected and recycled depends on local schemes, markets and contamination.

</Misconception>
