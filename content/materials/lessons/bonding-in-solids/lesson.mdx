<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why is diamond stiff and candle wax soft?">

In <LessonLink slug="atoms-bonds-and-properties">Atoms, bonds and properties</LessonLink> you met the idea that what a material does depends on how its atoms hold on to each other. This lesson makes that idea quantitative. Here are some numbers worth explaining. A diamond is about five hundred times stiffer than solid argon. Tungsten melts at 3695 K, sodium at 371 K and neon at 25 K. An aluminium rail expands about five times as much as a tungsten one when both are warmed by the same amount. Steel can be bent into a paper clip; salt crystals and glass shatter.

Every one of these facts can be traced back to a single curve: the **potential energy of two atoms as a function of the distance between them**. Its depth tells you how hard it is to pull the solid apart and roughly where it melts. Its curvature at the bottom sets the stiffness. Its lopsidedness causes thermal expansion. The *kind* of bond, ionic, covalent, metallic or one of the weak secondary bonds, decides the depth and shape of the curve and whether bonds point in particular directions.

Lumenary Chemistry covers bonding from the chemist's side: electronegativity, Lewis structures and molecular shapes in [chemical bonding](/en/chemistry/chemical-bonding/), and lattice energies of ionic crystals, with the Born–Landé equation and Madelung constants, in [the solid state and main-group chemistry](/en/chemistry/solid-state-and-main-group/). We will not repeat those. This lesson takes the materials scientist's view: from the energy curve of one bond to the properties an engineer measures.

</Section>

<Section id="kinds-of-bond" kind="intuition" title="Four kinds of bond, seen from inside a solid">

A materials scientist asks three questions about the bonds in a solid. How strong are they? Do they point in particular directions? And are the electrons that form them free to move? The four main types answer differently.

| Bonding | What holds the atoms | Directional? | Cohesive energy (eV per atom) | Typical consequences | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic | attraction between cations and anions | no, but like charges must not touch | large (NaCl: 8.2 eV per ion pair, from gaseous ions) | hard, brittle, high melting, insulating | NaCl, MgO, Al₂O₃ (partly) |
| Covalent | shared electron pairs between neighbours | strongly | large (Si 4.6, diamond 7.4) | very stiff, hard, brittle, often semiconducting | diamond, Si, SiC |
| Metallic | ions in a sea of delocalised electrons | weakly | wide range (Na 1.1, Cu 3.5, W 8.9) | ductile, conducting, shiny | Cu, Fe, Al, W |
| Secondary (van der Waals, hydrogen bonds) | fluctuating or permanent dipoles | weakly (H-bonds more so) | small (Ar 0.08; one H-bond in ice about 0.26) | soft, low melting | solid argon, ice, between polymer chains |

The directionality matters more than its modest place in the table suggests. In a metal, an atom is happy with any arrangement of neighbours at about the right distance, so planes of atoms can slide past each other one step at a time without the solid falling apart. That is the root of ductility, though, as <LessonLink slug="defects-and-dislocations">Defects and dislocations</LessonLink> will show, the sliding happens through defects rather than whole planes at once. In a covalent crystal, sliding would mean breaking and re-forming bonds at fixed angles, which needs far more energy than fracture, so the crystal cracks instead. In an ionic crystal, sliding by half a step brings like charges face to face; the repulsion opens a crack.

Most real solids are mixtures. Bonding is better described as a position inside a triangle whose corners are the three pure types. The **van Arkel–Ketelaar triangle** places a compound AB using only Pauling electronegativities: the horizontal axis is their average, the vertical axis their difference.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The energy curve of a bond">

<Definition term="interatomic potential, bond force">

An **interatomic potential** $U(r)$ is the energy of a pair of atoms as a function of the distance $r$ between their nuclei, taken as zero when they are far apart. The **force** between them is $F(r) = -\,dU/dr$; with this sign convention a positive $F$ pushes the atoms apart and a negative $F$ pulls them together.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equilibrium spacing, well depth, bond stiffness">

The **equilibrium spacing** $r_0$ is where $U$ has its minimum, so $F(r_0) = 0$. The **well depth** $U_0 = -U(r_0)$ is the energy needed to separate the pair. The **bond stiffness** $S_0 = U''(r_0)$ is the spring constant for small stretches: near $r_0$, $F \approx -S_0\,(r - r_0)$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="cohesive energy">

The **cohesive energy** of a solid is the energy needed to separate it into free, neutral atoms, usually quoted per atom in electronvolts (1 eV per atom is 96.485 kJ/mol). For an ionic solid the related lattice energy, measured from free *ions*, is larger; see [the solid state and main-group chemistry](/en/chemistry/solid-state-and-main-group/).

</Definition>

Every potential has the same two ingredients: an attraction that dominates at long range and a repulsion that dominates at short range, when filled electron shells begin to overlap. The simplest useful form, introduced by John Lennard-Jones in the 1920s for noble gases, uses inverse powers for both.

<Definition term="Lennard-Jones potential">

$$U(r) = 4\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right]$$

Here $\varepsilon$ is an energy and $\sigma$ the distance at which $U = 0$. The $r^{-6}$ term is the London dispersion attraction between fluctuating dipoles, which really does fall off as the sixth power; the $r^{-12}$ repulsion is a convenient choice, not a law.

</Definition>

<Theorem name="Properties of the Lennard-Jones bond">

For the Lennard-Jones potential, the equilibrium spacing is $r_0 = 2^{1/6}\sigma \approx 1.122\,\sigma$, the well depth is $U_0 = \varepsilon$, the bond stiffness is $S_0 = 36 \cdot 2^{2/3}\,\varepsilon/\sigma^2 \approx 57.1\,\varepsilon/\sigma^2$, and the attraction is strongest at $r_s = (26/7)^{1/6}\sigma \approx 1.244\,\sigma$, where it equals $2.40\,\varepsilon/\sigma$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $u = (\sigma/r)^6$, so $U = 4\varepsilon(u^2 - u)$. Then $F = -dU/dr = \dfrac{24\varepsilon}{r}\left(2u^2 - u\right)$, which vanishes when $u = 1/2$, that is $r_0 = 2^{1/6}\sigma$, and there $U = 4\varepsilon(1/4 - 1/2) = -\varepsilon$. Differentiating once more, $U''(r) = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{r^2}\left(624u^2 - 168u\right)$, which at $u = 1/2$ gives $\dfrac{72\varepsilon}{r_0^2} = \dfrac{72\varepsilon}{2^{1/3}\sigma^2} = 36\cdot 2^{2/3}\,\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\sigma^2}$. The pull is strongest where $dF/dr = 0$, which is where $U'' = 0$: $624u = 168$, so $u = 7/26$ and $r_s = (26/7)^{1/6}\sigma$. Substituting gives $|F(r_s)| \approx 2.396\,\varepsilon/\sigma$; the program below checks every step symbolically.

</Proof>

<Section id="lj-computed" kind="computation" title="The Lennard-Jones bond, computed">

The program derives the force, the equilibrium spacing, the stiffness and the strongest pull with SymPy, and tabulates the curve. It also computes the third derivative at the minimum, which we will need for thermal expansion.

```python run
import sympy as sp

# Lennard-Jones potential in reduced units: energies in epsilon, lengths in sigma
r = sp.symbols("r", positive=True)
U = 4 * (r**-12 - r**-6)
F = -sp.diff(U, r)                                # positive = repulsive
r0 = sp.solve(sp.Eq(F, 0), r)[0]                  # equilibrium spacing
k0 = sp.simplify(sp.diff(U, r, 2).subs(r, r0))    # bond stiffness U''(r0)
c3 = sp.simplify(sp.diff(U, r, 3).subs(r, r0))    # U'''(r0): how lopsided the well is
rs = sp.solve(sp.Eq(sp.diff(F, r), 0), r)[0]      # where the attraction is strongest

print("F(r)     =", sp.factor(F))
print("r0       =", r0, "=", sp.N(r0, 6), "sigma")
print("U(r0)    =", sp.simplify(U.subs(r, r0)), "epsilon")
print("U''(r0)  =", k0, "=", sp.N(k0, 6), "epsilon/sigma^2")
print("U'''(r0) =", c3, "=", sp.N(c3, 6), "epsilon/sigma^3")
print("strongest pull at r =", sp.N(rs, 6), "sigma, F =", sp.N(F.subs(r, rs), 6), "epsilon/sigma")

f_U, f_F = sp.lambdify(r, U), sp.lambdify(r, F)
print("\n r/sigma    U/eps   F/(eps/sigma)")
for x in [0.95, 1.0, 1.05, 1.1, float(r0), 1.2, float(rs), 1.3, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5]:
    print(f" {x:6.3f}  {f_U(x):8.3f}   {f_F(x):9.3f}")
```

Two features of the output carry the rest of the lesson. The force rises very steeply on the compressed side (59 ε/σ at 0.95σ) but the attraction never exceeds 2.40 ε/σ on the stretched side, so solids resist compression far better than tension. And $U'''(r_0) = -756\sqrt 2 \approx -1069\,\varepsilon/\sigma^3$ is large and negative: the well is lopsided, and that, as we will see, is why solids expand when heated.

</Section>

<Section id="stiffness" kind="human" title="From one bond to stiffness and strength">

Picture a solid as layers of atoms joined by springs. Pull on a block whose cross-section contains one bond per area $r_0^2$, and stretch each bond by $\delta$. The force per bond is $S_0\delta$, so the stress is $\sigma_{\text{stress}} = S_0\delta/r_0^2$, and the strain is $\delta/r_0$. Their ratio is Young's modulus:

$$E \approx \frac{S_0}{r_0}.$$

This ignores the geometry of the crystal (how many bonds cross a plane, at what angles), which changes the answer by a factor of order one, but it captures the main point: **stiffness is the curvature of the energy curve divided by the bond length**. For any potential of the form $U = A/r^n - B/r^m$, the curvature at the minimum works out to $S_0 = mn\,U_0/r_0^2$, so $E \sim mn\,U_0/r_0^3$: an energy per unit volume. Deep wells and short bonds make stiff materials.

The same picture gives the **ideal strength**, the stress needed to pull a perfect crystal apart: the strongest pull per bond, divided by the area per bond, $F_{\max}/r_0^2$.

```python run
kB = 1.380649e-23          # J/K
eV = 1.602176634e-19       # J

# Lennard-Jones argon with Rahman's 1964 parameters: epsilon/kB = 120 K, sigma = 0.340 nm
eps = 120 * kB
sigma = 0.340e-9
r0 = 2 ** (1 / 6) * sigma
S0 = 36 * 2 ** (2 / 3) * eps / sigma**2          # bond stiffness U''(r0), N/m
Fmax = 2.39643 * eps / sigma                      # strongest pull, N
E_est = S0 / r0
strength = Fmax / r0**2
print(f"argon: epsilon = {eps / eV * 1000:.2f} meV, r0 = {r0 * 1e9:.3f} nm")
print(f"  bond stiffness S0 = {S0:.3f} N/m")
print(f"  Young's modulus E ~ S0/r0 = {E_est / 1e9:.2f} GPa")
print(f"  ideal strength ~ Fmax/r0^2 = {strength / 1e9:.3f} GPa = E/{E_est / strength:.0f}")

# Working backwards: the bond stiffness implied by a measured modulus, S0 ~ E r0
# (typical room-temperature moduli, rounded; r0 = nearest-neighbour distance)
materials = [("diamond (covalent)", 1050, 0.154), ("tungsten (metallic)", 411, 0.274),
             ("MgO (ionic)", 300, 0.211), ("iron (metallic)", 211, 0.248),
             ("aluminium (metallic)", 70, 0.286), ("argon, LJ model (van der Waals)", E_est / 1e9, r0 * 1e9)]
print("\nmaterial                          E/GPa   r0/nm   S0 = E r0 (N/m)")
for name, E, d in materials:
    print(f"{name:32} {E:7.1f}  {d:6.3f}  {E * d:9.1f}")
```

Lennard-Jones argon comes out at about 2 GPa, the stiffness of a soft plastic, which is the right order for solid argon near absolute zero. Its bond stiffness, 0.8 N/m, is about two hundred times smaller than diamond's 160 N/m. Metallic and ionic bonds fall in between, around 20 to 110 N/m. The ratio of ideal strength to modulus, about 1/27, depends only on the shape of the potential; other shapes give somewhat different ratios, and the usual rule of thumb is that a perfect crystal's strength lies between a thirtieth and a tenth of its modulus.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Run the same estimate for steel and the answer is uncomfortable. With $E \approx 211$ GPa, the ideal strength should be around $211/27 \approx 8$ GPa, yet a structural steel yields at 0.25 to 0.5 GPa, and pure annealed iron at far less. Real metals are weaker than their bonds by a factor of ten to a thousand. The bonds are not to blame: the culprits are defects, the subject of <LessonLink slug="defects-and-dislocations">Defects and dislocations</LessonLink>.

</Narrator>

The modulus, by contrast, is *not* sensitive to defects. It is an average over trillions of bonds, each stretched a little, and a few missing atoms or misplaced planes hardly change it. That is why the modulus of a given steel barely changes with heat treatment while its strength can change tenfold, a theme that returns in <LessonLink slug="elasticity-plasticity-and-strengthening">Elasticity, plasticity and strengthening</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="expansion" kind="human" title="Thermal expansion: the lopsided well">

When a solid is heated its atoms vibrate more. If the well were a perfect parabola, a vibrating atom would spend as much time compressed as stretched, and the average spacing would not change. But the real well is steep on the compressed side and gentle on the stretched side. A hotter atom swings further out than in, so its average position moves outwards. **Thermal expansion is a direct measure of how lopsided the bond is.**

<Theorem name="Thermal expansion from anharmonicity">

Let a bond with potential $U(r)$ be in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, and expand $U$ about its minimum to third order, $U \approx U(r_0) + \tfrac12 S_0 x^2 + \tfrac16 U'''(r_0)\,x^3$ with $x = r - r_0$. To first order in $k_B T$, the mean stretch is

$$\langle x \rangle \approx -\frac{U'''(r_0)}{2\,S_0^2}\,k_B T,$$

so the linear expansion coefficient is $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{r_0}\dfrac{d\langle x\rangle}{dT} \approx -\dfrac{k_B\, U'''(r_0)}{2\,r_0\,S_0^2}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Classically the probability of a stretch $x$ is proportional to the Boltzmann factor $e^{-U(x)/k_B T}$. With $\beta = 1/k_B T$ and the cubic term small, $e^{-\beta U} \approx e^{-\beta S_0 x^2/2}\left(1 - \tfrac16\beta U''' x^3\right)$. The Gaussian factor is even, so $\int x\, e^{-\beta S_0 x^2/2}dx = 0$ and $\int x^4 e^{-\beta S_0 x^2/2}dx \big/ \int e^{-\beta S_0 x^2/2}dx = 3/(\beta S_0)^2$. Hence $\langle x\rangle \approx -\tfrac16 \beta U'''\cdot 3/(\beta S_0)^2 = -U''' k_B T/(2 S_0^2)$, to first order in $U'''$. A harmonic bond, with $U''' = 0$, does not expand.

</Proof>

The program checks the theorem by brute force: it averages the bond length over the Boltzmann distribution of a Lennard-Jones well and of a harmonic well with the same depth and curvature.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Mean length of one bond at temperature T (reduced units: t = kB T / epsilon, lengths in sigma)
r = np.linspace(0.85, 1.75, 20001)           # the bond is confined by its neighbours
r0 = 2 ** (1 / 6)
S0 = 36 * 2 ** (2 / 3)
U_lj = 4 * (r**-12 - r**-6)
U_harm = -1 + 0.5 * S0 * (r - r0) ** 2       # same depth and curvature, no lopsidedness

def mean_r(U, t):
    w = np.exp(-(U - U.min()) / t)           # Boltzmann weights
    return np.sum(r * w) / np.sum(w)

slope = 756 * np.sqrt(2) / (2 * S0**2)       # -U'''/(2 S0^2)
print(" t = kT/eps   <r> LJ    <r> harmonic   shift LJ   theorem")
for t in [0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3]:
    m_lj, m_h = mean_r(U_lj, t), mean_r(U_harm, t)
    print(f"   {t:5.2f}    {m_lj:.5f}    {m_h:.5f}     {m_lj - r0:+.5f}   {slope * t:+.5f}")
alpha = slope / r0
print(f"\nalpha = {alpha:.4f} kB/eps; for argon (eps/kB = 120 K): {alpha / 120:.2e} per K")

# Real metals: room-temperature expansion coefficient (1e-6 per K) and melting point (K), rounded
metals = {"Pb": (28.9, 601), "Al": (23.1, 933), "Ag": (18.9, 1235), "Cu": (16.5, 1358),
          "Au": (14.2, 1337), "Ni": (13.4, 1728), "Fe": (11.8, 1811), "Ti": (8.6, 1941),
          "Mo": (4.8, 2896), "W": (4.5, 3695)}
print("\nmetal  alpha/(1e-6 per K)   Tm/K   alpha*Tm")
for m, (a, Tm) in metals.items():
    print(f"  {m:3}       {a:5.1f}          {Tm:5}    {a * 1e-6 * Tm:.4f}")
prod = np.array([a * 1e-6 * Tm for a, Tm in metals.values()])
print(f"mean alpha*Tm = {prod.mean():.3f}, range {prod.min():.3f} to {prod.max():.3f}")
```

At low temperature the Lennard-Jones bond stretches exactly as the theorem predicts (0.00167σ against 0.00164σ at $k_BT = 0.01\,\varepsilon$), and the harmonic bond does not stretch at all. At higher temperatures the real well's gentle outer wall lets the bond stretch even faster than the cubic approximation says. For argon the model gives $\alpha \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-3}$ per kelvin, about a hundred times the expansion of iron. The theorem explains why: for wells of the same shape, $\alpha$ is proportional to $k_B/\varepsilon$, so a shallow well expands much more than a deep one.

The second table shows the same idea across real metals. The expansion coefficient spans a factor of six, from tungsten to lead, but the product $\alpha T_m$ stays between 0.014 and 0.023. Metals with deep wells (high melting points) have small expansion; shallow wells expand a lot. This is why high-melting solids such as tungsten, silicon carbide and most engineering ceramics expand little, and why the mismatch in $\alpha$ between a metal and a ceramic coating is one of the classic causes of cracking and peeling.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The proof is classical: it lets the atom have any energy. Below roughly the Debye temperature (a few hundred kelvin for most metals), quantum mechanics freezes out the vibrations, and the expansion coefficient falls towards zero as $T \to 0$; the classical result applies only in the high-temperature limit. And a few solids, such as silica glass near room temperature and the alloy Invar, have almost no expansion for reasons beyond a single bond: their structures or magnetism compensate.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="melting" kind="human" title="Melting points and bond strength">

Deeper wells should hold atoms in place to higher temperatures. The program compares the cohesive energy of some elements with their melting points, then tries Frederick Lindemann's 1910 idea that a crystal melts when its atoms vibrate by about a tenth of their spacing.

```python run
import numpy as np

kB_eV = 8.617333e-5     # Boltzmann constant in eV/K

# Cohesive energy (eV per atom) and melting point (K), rounded values from standard tables
solids = [("Ne", "van der Waals", 0.020, 24.6), ("Ar", "van der Waals", 0.080, 83.8),
          ("Na", "metallic", 1.11, 371), ("Al", "metallic", 3.39, 933),
          ("Cu", "metallic", 3.49, 1358), ("Fe", "metallic", 4.28, 1811),
          ("Si", "covalent", 4.63, 1687), ("W", "metallic", 8.90, 3695)]
print("solid  bonding          Ecoh/eV   Tm/K    kB*Tm/Ecoh")
for name, kind, Ec, Tm in solids:
    print(f"  {name:3}  {kind:14} {Ec:7.3f}  {Tm:7.1f}    {kB_eV * Tm / Ec:.3f}")
Ec = np.array([s[2] for s in solids])
Tm = np.array([s[3] for s in solids])
print(f"correlation of log Ecoh with log Tm: {np.corrcoef(np.log(Ec), np.log(Tm))[0, 1]:.3f}")

# How much of the cohesive energy does melting use? Heat of fusion (kJ/mol), rounded
for name, H_fus, Ec_eV in [("Ar", 1.18, 0.080), ("Al", 10.71, 3.39), ("Cu", 13.26, 3.49)]:
    print(f"{name}: heat of fusion = {H_fus / 96.485:.3f} eV per atom = "
          f"{100 * H_fus / 96.485 / Ec_eV:.0f} % of the cohesive energy")

# Lindemann: melting when the rms vibration reaches about 0.1 r0. For one harmonic bond
# <x^2> = kB T / S0, so kB T_m ~ (0.1 r0)^2 S0.
r0 = 2 ** (1 / 6)                 # sigma
S0 = 36 * 2 ** (2 / 3)            # epsilon / sigma^2
t_L = (0.1 * r0) ** 2 * S0
print(f"\nLindemann estimate for a Lennard-Jones bond: kB T_m = {t_L:.3f} epsilon")
print(f"argon (epsilon/kB = 120 K): T_m ~ {t_L * 120:.0f} K (measured: 83.8 K)")
```

Across more than two orders of magnitude, from neon to tungsten, the melting point tracks the cohesive energy closely (the logarithms correlate at 0.99). For the metals and silicon, $k_B T_m$ is about 0.024 to 0.036 of the cohesive energy; for the noble-gas solids it is nearer 0.1. A melting solid does not need anything like its cohesive energy: melting aluminium or copper absorbs only 3 to 4 per cent of the energy needed to separate it into free atoms, and even argon only 15 per cent. The liquid keeps nearly all its bonds; it merely loses their long-range order. In a metal much of the cohesion comes from the shared electron sea, which barely notices whether the ions are lined up, so melting costs a smaller fraction than in a van der Waals solid. That is one reason the ratio differs between the families.

Lindemann's criterion gives 86 K for argon, close to the measured 83.8 K. Do not be too impressed. The 0.1 is itself an empirical number, and a single bond is not a crystal: in the solid each atom is held by twelve neighbours, which stiffens it, while it can vibrate in three directions, which loosens it. The errors partly cancel. Melting is a competition between the free energies of solid and liquid, and a criterion that looks only at the solid cannot be the whole story; the thermodynamics comes in <LessonLink slug="phase-diagrams">Phase diagrams</LessonLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="secondary" kind="human" title="Secondary bonds hold the soft world together">

Van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds are ten to a hundred times weaker than primary bonds, but they decide the properties of a large part of the material world.

- **Polymers.** A polyethylene chain is held together by strong C–C covalent bonds, but neighbouring chains are held to each other only by van der Waals forces. Pulling a plastic bag mostly stretches and slides those weak links, which is why its modulus is between about 0.2 and 1 GPa rather than the hundreds of GPa of a single chain pulled along its length. Ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene fibres, with chains drawn out straight and parallel, reach a large fraction of the chain's own stiffness and are used in ropes and protective armour. More in <LessonLink slug="polymers">Polymers</LessonLink>.
- **Layered solids.** Graphite, molybdenum disulfide and hexagonal boron nitride consist of strongly bonded sheets stacked with van der Waals gaps. The sheets slide, which makes these solids lubricants, and they can be peeled apart into the single-layer materials of <LessonLink slug="two-dimensional-and-quantum-materials">Two-dimensional and quantum materials</LessonLink>.
- **Hydrogen-bonded solids.** Ice, cellulose (the strongest component of wood and cotton) and the silk of spiders owe their structure to hydrogen bonds, each worth roughly 0.2 to 0.3 eV.
- **The glass transition** of a polymer is the temperature at which secondary bonds between chains stop locking the chains in place; it is the subject of part of <LessonLink slug="glasses-and-nanomaterials">Glasses and nanomaterials</LessonLink>.

<Analogy>

A pile of uncooked spaghetti is like a polymer: each strand is strong along its length, but the pile is held together only by friction between strands, so it is easy to pull apart sideways. Soaking it (heating a polymer above its glass transition) lets the strands slide even more easily. The analogy stops at friction: secondary bonds are attractions, not rubbing.

</Analogy>

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view: potentials in practice">

Real materials research uses interatomic potentials for **atomistic simulation**: molecular dynamics runs that follow millions of atoms for nanoseconds, enough to watch a crack advance, a dislocation move or a grain boundary migrate. The choice of potential is the single biggest decision in such a study, and the field has a ladder of options.

- **Pair potentials** (Lennard-Jones, Morse, Buckingham) are fast and adequate for noble gases, some molecular crystals and, with charges added, many ionic oxides.
- **Many-body potentials for metals.** The embedded-atom method of Murray Daw and Mike Baskes (1984) gives each atom an energy that depends on the density of electrons contributed by its neighbours, not just on a sum over pairs. It captures surface energies and vacancy energies that pair potentials get badly wrong.
- **Bond-order potentials for covalent solids** (Stillinger–Weber and Tersoff for silicon, later reactive force fields such as ReaxFF) add terms for bond angles, because covalent bonds care about direction.
- **Machine-learned potentials**, starting with Jörg Behler and Michele Parrinello in 2007, fit a flexible model to thousands of quantum-mechanical calculations. As of 2026 there are "universal" models trained on large databases of computed structures, such as [MACE](https://github.com/ACEsuit/mace), [CHGNet](https://github.com/CederGroupHub/chgnet) and the models in [MatGL](https://github.com/materialsvirtuallab/matgl), used as a starting point and then fine-tuned for a specific system. They are far slower than a pair potential but far faster than the quantum calculations they imitate; see <LessonLink slug="atomistic-simulation">Atomistic simulation</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="ai-materials-discovery">AI-assisted materials discovery</LessonLink>.

Potentials are published, compared and archived: the [NIST Interatomic Potentials Repository](https://www.ctcms.nist.gov/potentials/) and [OpenKIM](https://openkim.org/) collect them with tests of the properties they reproduce, and the [Materials Project](https://materialsproject.org/) provides elastic constants computed from quantum mechanics against which a potential can be checked. A typical input for the open-source code [LAMMPS](https://www.lammps.org/), here for the argon crystal of this lesson, looks like this (shown for illustration; it is not run here):

```text
# LAMMPS input (not run here): an FCC argon crystal held at 40 K and zero pressure
units           metal                  # energies in eV, distances in angstroms, time in ps
lattice         fcc 5.26
region          box block 0 6 0 6 0 6
create_box      1 box
create_atoms    1 box
mass            1 39.948
pair_style      lj/cut 8.5
pair_coeff      1 1 0.01034 3.40       # epsilon in eV, sigma in angstroms
velocity        all create 40.0 4928459
fix             1 all npt temp 40.0 40.0 0.1 iso 0.0 0.0 1.0
thermo          1000
run             20000
```

Measuring the box size at several temperatures in such a run gives the thermal expansion of the full three-dimensional crystal, which our single bond only approximates.

<Narrator who="gideon">

A pair potential has a hidden fingerprint. For a cubic crystal in which every force acts along the line between two atoms, and every atom sits at a centre of symmetry, and the crystal is unstressed, the elastic constants must satisfy the Cauchy relation $C_{12} = C_{44}$. Many metals break it badly (in copper $C_{12}$ is well over one and a half times $C_{44}$), which proves that no pair potential, however carefully fitted, can describe them. That is why metals need many-body models.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

"Melting breaks the bonds." A liquid metal keeps nearly all of its cohesion: the latent heat of melting copper is about 4 per cent of its cohesive energy. Melting destroys long-range order, not bonding. Boiling, which takes the atoms far apart, is what breaks the bonds, which is why heats of vaporisation are many times larger than heats of fusion.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Strong bonds make strong materials." Strong bonds make *stiff* materials with high melting points. Strength, the stress at which a material yields or breaks, is usually controlled by defects and is often a hundred times below the ideal strength computed from the bonds. Diamond and glass have strong bonds and break easily from a scratch; mild steel has weaker bonds and is tough.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Solids expand because their atoms get bigger when hot." Atoms do not swell. The average distance between them grows because the potential well is lopsided; a perfectly harmonic solid would not expand at all.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Metals bend because metallic bonds are weak." Tungsten's bonds are among the strongest of any element (cohesive energy 8.9 eV per atom), and tungsten can still be drawn into lamp filament wire when hot. Metals deform because their bonds are *non-directional*, so planes of atoms can slip without the solid losing cohesion, not because their bonds are weak.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Van der Waals forces are too weak to matter." They hold every polymer, every molecular crystal and every layered solid together, and they let a gecko walk across a ceiling. Their weakness per bond is offset by their number.

</Misconception>

</Section>
