<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Same steel, different worlds">

Take two identical bars of a steel with 0.8 wt% carbon, heat both until they are austenite, and cool them differently. Let one cool slowly in the furnace: it becomes pearlite, soft enough to machine. Plunge the other into water: it becomes martensite, so hard that a file skids off it, and so brittle that it may crack. The composition is the same and so, below 727 °C, is the equilibrium phase diagram of <LessonLink slug="phase-diagrams">phase diagrams</LessonLink>. What differs is the path, and the path is the business of **phase transformations**.

Japanese swordsmiths exploited this centuries before anyone could explain it. They coated a blade in clay, thinly along the edge and thickly along the spine, before quenching it. The edge cooled fast and became martensite, hard enough to hold an edge; the spine cooled more slowly and stayed tougher. The boundary between the two shows on the polished blade as the wavy line called the *hamon*. In 1906, Alfred Wilm in Germany found that an aluminium alloy quenched from a high temperature slowly *hardened* by itself over several days at room temperature. His alloy, Duralumin, was soon flying in airships and aircraft, but the reason, invisible particles a few nanometres across, was not seen until 1938.

A phase diagram says where a material is heading. This lesson asks how a new phase starts (nucleation), how it spreads (growth and the kinetics of the whole transformation), and how engineers use the answers in steels and aluminium alloys.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Why change needs a push">

Below its melting point, a solid has a lower Gibbs energy than the liquid, so the liquid "wants" to freeze. But the solid cannot appear everywhere at once. It must start as tiny clusters of atoms, and a cluster has a surface, an interface between solid and liquid, which costs energy. For a very small cluster, most of its atoms are at the surface, the surface cost outweighs the gain from the atoms inside, and the cluster is more likely to dissolve than to grow. Only a cluster that by chance grows past a certain **critical size** gains more than it pays, and then it grows steadily. That chance event is **nucleation**.

So every transformation needs **undercooling** (or, for transformations on heating, overheating): the further below the equilibrium temperature, the larger the driving force and the smaller the critical cluster. But most solid-state transformations also need atoms to diffuse, and diffusion slows exponentially as the temperature falls (<LessonLink slug="diffusion">diffusion</LessonLink>). Just below the equilibrium temperature there is plenty of mobility but almost no driving force; far below it there is plenty of driving force but almost no mobility. The transformation is fastest in between. This competition is the key to the C-shaped curves of the TTT diagram later in the lesson.

<Analogy>

Starting a new club in a school takes a founding group. One or two enthusiasts give up, because the effort of organising outweighs the fun; once a dozen have joined, the club is self-sustaining and grows. If the school day is very busy, nobody has time to start anything; if the idea is only slightly appealing, nobody bothers. New clubs appear fastest when the idea is attractive *and* people have time to move around.

</Analogy>

</Section>

<Section id="nucleation" kind="definitions" title="Nucleation and the critical radius">

<Definition term="homogeneous and heterogeneous nucleation">

**Homogeneous nucleation** happens at random anywhere in the parent phase, with no help. **Heterogeneous nucleation** happens on something already present, such as a mould wall, an impurity particle, a grain boundary or a dislocation, which supplies part of the interface for free.

</Definition>

Model a nucleus as a sphere of radius $r$. Let $\Delta G_v \gt 0$ be the drop in Gibbs energy per unit volume when the new phase forms (the driving force), and $\gamma$ the energy per unit area of the interface. Then forming the nucleus changes the Gibbs energy by

$$\Delta G(r) = -\tfrac43\pi r^3\,\Delta G_v + 4\pi r^2\,\gamma.$$

<Theorem name="Critical radius and nucleation barrier">

$\Delta G(r)$ has a maximum at the **critical radius** and **nucleation barrier**

$$r^* = \frac{2\gamma}{\Delta G_v}, \qquad \Delta G^* = \frac{16\pi\gamma^3}{3\,\Delta G_v^2}.$$

Clusters smaller than $r^*$ tend to shrink and larger ones tend to grow. For solidification near the melting point $T_\text{m}$, $\Delta G_v \approx \Delta H_v\,\Delta T/T_\text{m}$, where $\Delta H_v$ is the latent heat per unit volume and $\Delta T = T_\text{m} - T$ the undercooling, so $r^* \propto 1/\Delta T$ and $\Delta G^* \propto 1/\Delta T^2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Setting $d\Delta G/dr = -4\pi r^2\Delta G_v + 8\pi r\gamma = 0$ gives $r^* = 2\gamma/\Delta G_v$, and the second derivative there, $-8\pi\gamma \lt 0$, confirms a maximum. Substituting, $\Delta G^* = -\tfrac43\pi\tfrac{8\gamma^3}{\Delta G_v^2} + 4\pi\tfrac{4\gamma^2}{\Delta G_v^2}\gamma = \tfrac{16\pi\gamma^3}{3\Delta G_v^2}$. For the driving force, $\Delta G_v = \Delta H_v - T\Delta S_v$, and at $T_\text{m}$ it vanishes, so $\Delta S_v = \Delta H_v/T_\text{m}$; if $\Delta H_v$ and $\Delta S_v$ barely change over the undercooling, $\Delta G_v \approx \Delta H_v(1 - T/T_\text{m}) = \Delta H_v\,\Delta T/T_\text{m}$.

</Proof>

The rate at which nuclei form per unit volume follows a Boltzmann factor, $I = I_0\exp(-\Delta G^*/k_\text{B}T)$, with a prefactor $I_0$ that counts atoms and their attempts to join a cluster. Because $\Delta G^*$ sits in an exponent and depends on $1/\Delta T^2$, the rate switches on abruptly. The block computes this for liquid copper, with a handbook latent heat, the interface energy that David Turnbull estimated from his 1950 undercooling experiments, and an order-of-magnitude prefactor.

```python run
import math

# Homogeneous nucleation of solid copper in its undercooled liquid.
k_B = 1.380649e-23        # J/K
N_A = 6.02214076e23       # 1/mol
Tm = 1357.8               # K, melting point
dH = 13.26e3              # J/mol, enthalpy of fusion (handbook value, rounded)
Vm = 7.1e-6               # m^3/mol, molar volume of solid copper
gamma = 0.177             # J/m^2, solid-liquid interface energy (Turnbull's 1950 estimate)
I0 = 1e40                 # nuclei per m^3 per s: attempt rate, order of magnitude only

dHv = dH / Vm             # J/m^3
v_atom = Vm / N_A         # m^3 per atom
print(f"latent heat per volume {dHv:.2e} J/m^3, volume per atom {v_atom:.2e} m^3\n")
print(" dT (K)   dGv (J/m^3)   r* (nm)   atoms in nucleus   dG*/kT   log10 I (per m^3 per s)")
for dT in (20, 50, 100, 150, 200, 220, 240, 260):
    dGv = dHv * dT / Tm                      # driving force per unit volume
    r_star = 2 * gamma / dGv
    dG_star = 16 * math.pi * gamma**3 / (3 * dGv**2)
    n_star = 4 / 3 * math.pi * r_star**3 / v_atom
    T = Tm - dT
    barrier = dG_star / (k_B * T)
    log_I = math.log10(I0) - barrier / math.log(10)
    print(f"{dT:6d}   {dGv:11.2e}   {r_star * 1e9:7.2f}   {n_star:16.0f}   {barrier:6.0f}   {log_I:8.1f}")

# Undercooling at which a 1 cm^3 sample would nucleate about once per second (I = 1e6 per m^3 per s).
lo, hi = 50.0, 400.0
for _ in range(60):
    dT = (lo + hi) / 2
    dGv = dHv * dT / Tm
    barrier = 16 * math.pi * gamma**3 / (3 * dGv**2) / (k_B * (Tm - dT))
    lo, hi = (dT, hi) if barrier > math.log(I0 / 1e6) else (lo, dT)
print(f"\nhomogeneous nucleation becomes noticeable near dT = {dT:.0f} K ({dT / Tm:.2f} Tm)")
```

At 20 K of undercooling the critical nucleus would hold about 760 000 atoms and the barrier is 6647 $k_\text{B}T$: the predicted rate, $10^{-2847}$ nuclei per cubic metre per second, means never. At 200 K the critical nucleus has shrunk to about 760 atoms, with a radius of 1.3 nm, and the rate has climbed through more than 2800 orders of magnitude. Homogeneous nucleation becomes noticeable near 198 K of undercooling, 0.15 $T_\text{m}$; Turnbull's carefully cleaned droplets of many metals froze after undercooling by roughly a fifth of their absolute melting temperatures, the same order.

Real liquids almost never get that far, because they nucleate **heterogeneously**. A nucleus forming as a cap on a flat surface, meeting it at a contact angle $\theta$, has the same critical radius but needs fewer atoms, and the barrier is reduced by the factor

$$S(\theta) = \tfrac14\,(2 + \cos\theta)(1 - \cos\theta)^2,$$

which is 1 for $\theta = 180°$ (the surface does not help) and falls to 0.156 at $\theta = 60°$ and 0.013 at $\theta = 30°$. Because the factor multiplies an exponent of tens to thousands of $k_\text{B}T$, a good substrate makes nucleation easy at undercoolings of a few kelvin. Foundries exploit this on purpose: adding **grain refiners**, such as aluminium–titanium–boron master alloys that release tiny boride particles into molten aluminium, gives many nuclei and a fine, uniform grain size. Cloud seeding with silver iodide particles does the same for ice in supercooled clouds, whose pure droplets can stay liquid down to about −38 °C.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The classical theory treats a nucleus of a few hundred atoms as a little sphere of bulk solid with a sharp surface and the bulk value of $\gamma$, which is a bold extrapolation. And because $\Delta G^* \propto \gamma^3$, a 10 % error in $\gamma$ changes the barrier by a third, and the rate by many orders of magnitude. The theory predicts the *form* of nucleation behaviour well, a sharp onset that scales with undercooling; quantitative rates are usually fitted rather than predicted.

</Narrator>

<Section id="kinetics" kind="computation" title="Growth and the Avrami equation">

Once nuclei exist, they grow. When atoms only need to hop across the interface, as in solidification of a pure metal or recrystallisation, the interface moves at a steady speed $v$ and a grain's radius grows in proportion to $t$. When growth needs solute to diffuse to or from the interface, as for a carbon-poor ferrite grain growing in austenite, the radius grows only as $\sqrt{Dt}$, the diffusion length of the previous lesson.

The fraction transformed, $f(t)$, then rises along an S-shaped curve: slowly at first while nuclei are few and small, quickly in the middle, and slowly at the end as growing grains meet one another (**impingement**) and the untransformed regions shrink. The theory was worked out between 1937 and 1941 by Andrey Kolmogorov, William Johnson and Robert Mehl, and Melvin Avrami, and goes by their initials, JMAK.

<Theorem name="The Johnson–Mehl–Avrami–Kolmogorov equation">

Let nuclei appear at random positions in the untransformed material, at a constant rate $I$ per unit volume, and let each grow as a sphere at constant speed $v$ until it meets others. Then the fraction transformed is

$$f(t) = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\,I v^3 t^4\right),$$

and more generally $f = 1 - \exp(-kt^n)$, where the **Avrami exponent** $n$ depends on how nucleation and growth happen. In two dimensions (a thin film), the same assumptions give $f = 1 - \exp(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}Iv^2t^3)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Imagine that grains could grow through each other, and that nuclei could also form inside already transformed material ("phantom" nuclei). The total volume fraction they would occupy, counting overlaps more than once, is the **extended fraction** $f_\text{ext}$. A nucleus born at time $\tau$ has radius $v(t - \tau)$ at time $t$, so in three dimensions $f_\text{ext} = \int_0^t I\cdot\tfrac43\pi v^3(t - \tau)^3\,d\tau = \tfrac{\pi}{3}Iv^3t^4$. Now account for overlap. In a short interval, the extended fraction grows by $df_\text{ext}$, and because nuclei are placed at random, only the part that lands in untransformed material, a fraction $1 - f$ of it, is real: $df = (1 - f)\,df_\text{ext}$. Integrating, $-\ln(1 - f) = f_\text{ext}$, which gives the result. Phantom nuclei cause no error: a phantom born inside a grain growing at the same speed stays inside that grain. In two dimensions the area of a disc, $\pi v^2(t - \tau)^2$, replaces the sphere's volume.

</Proof>

The exponent $n$ is a fingerprint of the mechanism. With interface-controlled growth in three dimensions, $n = 4$ for a constant nucleation rate and $n = 3$ for **site saturation**, when all nuclei are present at the start; with diffusion-controlled growth the corresponding values are 2.5 and 1.5. In two dimensions each is one smaller. Plotting $\ln(-\ln(1-f))$ against $\ln t$ gives a straight line of slope $n$, the **Avrami plot**.

The block tests the theory by simulation. Nuclei appear at random places and times in a periodic unit square, and each grows as a disc at constant speed; a 256 × 256 grid records when each cell is first reached. It compares the simulated fraction with the JMAK formula, fits the Avrami exponent, and repeats the experiment with site saturation.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 256                                   # grid of N x N cells on a unit square, periodic edges
xs = (np.arange(N) + 0.5) / N
X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, xs)
v = 0.01                                  # growth speed: distance per unit time

def arrival_times(nuclei):
    """Time at which each cell is first reached by a growing disc.  nuclei: rows (x, y, t0)."""
    first = np.full((N, N), np.inf)
    for x0, y0, t0 in nuclei:
        dx = np.abs(X - x0); dx = np.minimum(dx, 1 - dx)     # periodic (minimum image)
        dy = np.abs(Y - y0); dy = np.minimum(dy, 1 - dy)
        np.minimum(first, t0 + np.hypot(dx, dy) / v, out=first)
    return first

def avrami_exponent(t, f):
    keep = (f > 0.05) & (f < 0.95)
    slope, _ = np.polyfit(np.log(t[keep]), np.log(-np.log(1 - f[keep])), 1)
    return slope

t = np.linspace(0.25, 10, 40)

# 1. Constant nucleation rate I (per unit area per unit time) for 10 time units.
I = 45.0
count = rng.poisson(I * 1.0 * 10)
nuclei = np.column_stack([rng.random(count), rng.random(count), rng.uniform(0, 10, count)])
first = arrival_times(nuclei)
f_sim = np.array([np.mean(first <= ti) for ti in t])
f_jmak = 1 - np.exp(-math.pi / 3 * I * v**2 * t**3)
print(f"constant nucleation: {count} nuclei (phantoms included)")
print("   t    simulated f   JMAK f")
for i in range(3, 40, 4):
    print(f"{t[i]:5.2f}   {f_sim[i]:11.3f}   {f_jmak[i]:6.3f}")
print(f"fitted Avrami exponent n = {avrami_exponent(t, f_sim):.2f}  (theory: 3 in two dimensions)")
print("\nthe square at t = 5 (# transformed, . not yet):")
for row in range(0, N, 12):
    print("".join("#" if first[row, col] <= 5 else "." for col in range(0, N, 6)))

# 2. Site saturation: all nuclei present at t = 0.
count = 120
nuclei = np.column_stack([rng.random(count), rng.random(count), np.zeros(count)])
first = arrival_times(nuclei)
f_sim = np.array([np.mean(first <= ti) for ti in t])
print(f"\nsite saturation: {count} nuclei at t = 0")
print(f"fitted Avrami exponent n = {avrami_exponent(t, f_sim):.2f}  (theory: 2 in two dimensions)")
print(f"half transformed at t = {np.interp(0.5, f_sim, t):.2f}; "
      f"JMAK predicts {math.sqrt(math.log(2) / (math.pi * count * v**2)):.2f}")
```

The simulated fractions follow the JMAK curve to within about 0.02 all the way from 0.4 % to 99.8 % transformed; the small excess late on comes from the random draw, which happened to produce 458 nuclei where 450 were expected. The fitted exponents are 3.08 and 2.08, against 3 and 2 in theory, and with site saturation the half-transformation time, 4.28, matches the formula's 4.29. The snapshot at $t = 5$, when the square is 45 % transformed, shows what the formula does not: discs of every size, the older ones large and already merging, the newest ones single cells.

<Narrator who="margaret">

In the laboratory, nobody watches the grains directly. The fraction transformed is inferred from a property that changes with it: a dilatometer measures the tiny change in a sample's length as austenite turns into less dense ferrite, a differential scanning calorimeter measures the heat released, and electrical resistivity or X-ray diffraction peak intensities can be used too. The Avrami plot of those data gives $n$, which tells you whether nucleation was continuous or site-saturated, and whether growth was diffusion-controlled.

</Narrator>

<Section id="ttt-cct" kind="examples" title="TTT and CCT diagrams">

Steel heat treatment is planned with two kinds of map, both introduced by Edgar Bain and his colleagues at United States Steel from 1930. An **isothermal transformation** or **time–temperature–transformation (TTT)** diagram is made by quenching small samples of austenite to a fixed temperature below 727 °C, holding them for different times, and recording when the transformation starts (often taken as 1 %) and finishes (99 %). Plotted against a logarithmic time axis, the start and finish curves are shaped like a C: slow near 727 °C, where the driving force is small, slow at low temperatures, where diffusion is slow, and fastest at the **nose** in between.

The block builds an illustrative start curve from this reasoning: an Arrhenius factor for diffusion times a driving force that grows as the cube of the undercooling, with the nose placed at 550 °C and 1 s, roughly where it sits for a plain eutectoid steel. In the figure the finish curve lies a factor of 7.7 later at every temperature, which is what the JMAK equation with $n = 3$ gives between 1 % and 99 % transformed.

A part being quenched does not jump to one temperature and wait; it cools continuously. A **continuous-cooling transformation (CCT)** diagram, measured with dilatometers that cool samples at controlled rates, is the more useful map for that, and its curves sit lower and later than the TTT curves. A rough way to get one from the other is **Scheil's additivity rule**: treat the cooling path as a series of short isothermal holds and say the transformation starts when the fractions of the incubation time used up add to one, $\sum \Delta t/\tau(T) = 1$. The block applies it to find the slowest cooling that still misses the nose, the **critical cooling rate** for a fully martensitic structure.

```python run
import math

# An illustrative model of the start of transformation in a plain eutectoid steel:
# rate ~ (diffusion, Arrhenius) x (driving force, ~ undercooling cubed), so
#   tau(T) = tau_n * exp(Q/R (1/T - 1/T_n)) * ((Te - T_n) / (Te - T))**3,
# with Q chosen so that the "nose" (the shortest tau) is at T_n.  Temperatures in kelvin.
R = 8.314
Te = 727 + 273.15             # eutectoid temperature: no transformation above it
T_n, tau_n = 550 + 273.15, 1.0   # nose at 550 C, 1 s (roughly like a plain eutectoid steel)
Q = 3 * R * T_n**2 / (Te - T_n)   # d(ln tau)/dT = 0 at T_n
Ms = 220 + 273.15             # martensite start, roughly, for 0.76 wt% C

def tau(T):
    if T >= Te:
        return math.inf
    return tau_n * math.exp(Q / R * (1 / T - 1 / T_n)) * ((Te - T_n) / (Te - T)) ** 3

print(f"Q = {Q / 1000:.0f} kJ/mol\n")
print("isothermal start times (the TTT 'start' curve):")
for T_c in (700, 650, 600, 550, 500, 450, 400, 300):
    print(f"  {T_c} C: {tau(T_c + 273.15):7.2f} s")

def cooling_start(rate, T0=800 + 273.15, dt=1e-3):
    """Cool at a constant rate (K/s) from T0.  Scheil's additivity rule: the transformation
    starts when the sum of dt / tau(T) reaches 1.  Return the start temperature (K) or None."""
    total, T = 0.0, T0
    while T > Ms:
        total += dt / tau(T)
        if total >= 1:
            return T
        T -= rate * dt
    return None

print("\ncontinuous cooling from 800 C:")
for rate in (1, 5, 20, 50, 100, 150, 200):
    T = cooling_start(rate, dt=min(0.01, 0.2 / rate))
    result = f"starts at {T - 273.15:.0f} C" if T else "no transformation before Ms: martensite"
    print(f"  {rate:4d} K/s: {result}")

lo, hi = 50.0, 400.0                 # bisection for the critical cooling rate
for _ in range(30):
    mid = (lo + hi) / 2
    lo, hi = (mid, hi) if cooling_start(mid, dt=0.2 / mid) else (lo, mid)
print(f"\ncritical cooling rate for martensite: about {mid:.0f} K/s")
```

The model's start times, 33 s at 700 °C, 1 s at the nose and 31 s at 300 °C, have the shape of a real plain-carbon steel's diagram. On continuous cooling, the transformation starts lower than any isothermal curve would suggest: at 627 °C for 20 K/s and at 536 °C for 100 K/s, and cooling faster than about 203 K/s from 800 °C avoids it altogether. Real plain carbon steels need critical rates of the same order, which only a vigorous water quench of a thin section can deliver.

That is why alloy steels exist. Adding manganese, chromium, nickel and molybdenum, or a few thousandths of a per cent of boron, slows the diffusional transformations and shifts the C-curves to the right, so slower quenches, in oil or even air, still produce martensite, and thicker sections harden right through. This property is **hardenability**, and it is measured by the **Jominy end-quench test** (standardised as ASTM A255 and ISO 642): a bar austenitised in a furnace is sprayed with water on one end only, so each position along it has cooled at a different, known rate, and hardness measured along the bar maps hardness against cooling rate.

<Section id="martensite" kind="human" title="Martensite">

If austenite is cooled so fast that carbon has no time to diffuse, it still transforms, but in a completely different way. Below the **martensite start temperature** $M_\text{s}$, whole regions of the crystal shear into a new structure in a coordinated movement of atoms, each moving less than one interatomic distance, with interfaces sweeping through the crystal at a large fraction of the speed of sound. Because there is no diffusion, the product, **martensite**, has exactly the composition of the austenite it came from: it is iron supersaturated with carbon. The carbon atoms, trapped in gaps that are too small for them in a body-centred lattice, stretch it along one axis into a **body-centred tetragonal** cell, whose axial ratio $c/a$ grows with carbon content (approximately $1 + 0.045$ per wt% carbon). The strained lattice resists the motion of dislocations very strongly, which is why martensite is so hard and, in higher-carbon steels, so brittle.

<Definition term="athermal martensite">

In steels, the amount of martensite depends on how far below $M_\text{s}$ the steel is cooled, not on how long it is held there. Each new degree of undercooling produces new plates almost instantly; holding does almost nothing. Transformation is complete only at the **martensite finish** temperature $M_\text{f}$, and austenite that has not transformed when cooling stops is **retained austenite**.

</Definition>

Two empirical formulas from the steel industry put numbers on this. K. W. Andrews' 1965 equation estimates the start temperature from composition (wt%): $M_\text{s}\,(°\text{C}) \approx 539 - 423\,\text{C} - 30.4\,\text{Mn} - 17.7\,\text{Ni} - 12.1\,\text{Cr} - 7.5\,\text{Mo}$. Carbon lowers $M_\text{s}$ dramatically, from about 500 °C in a very low-carbon steel to roughly 220 °C in a eutectoid steel and lower still at higher carbon contents, which is why high-carbon tool steels can retain austenite and are sometimes cooled below 0 °C to finish the transformation. And Koistinen and Marburger's 1959 equation gives the fraction transformed at a temperature $T$ below $M_\text{s}$: $f_\text{M} = 1 - \exp[-0.011\,(M_\text{s} - T)]$, with temperatures in kelvin or Celsius.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Fresh martensite in a medium- or high-carbon steel is too brittle to use; a hardened part straight from the quench can crack from its own internal stresses. That is why steel is almost always **tempered** after quenching, reheated to a few hundred degrees Celsius so that some carbon leaves the martensite as fine carbides. The trade between hardness and toughness this buys is the subject of <LessonLink slug="processing-and-heat-treatment">processing and heat treatment</LessonLink>.

</Narrator>

Martensitic transformations are not only for steels. In nickel–titanium alloys (developed at the US Naval Ordnance Laboratory around 1960) the martensite plates can move back and forth: deformed when martensitic, the alloy returns to its original shape when warmed (the **shape-memory effect**), and just above its transformation temperature it can be stretched by several per cent and spring back (**superelasticity**), which is used in self-expanding stents, orthodontic wires and actuators.

</Section>

<Section id="precipitation" kind="examples" title="Precipitation hardening">

Wilm's Duralumin, and the 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx aluminium alloys that build aircraft, bicycle frames and car parts today, are strengthened by **precipitation hardening** (also called age hardening). The phase diagram makes it possible: in the aluminium–copper system, aluminium dissolves up to 5.65 wt% copper at 548 °C, but almost none at room temperature. The treatment has three steps.

1. **Solution treatment.** Heat an alloy with, say, 4 wt% copper into the single-phase field, around 500 to 530 °C, until all the copper is dissolved.
2. **Quench.** Cool it quickly to room temperature, too fast for the equilibrium phase to form. The result is a **supersaturated solid solution**, with far more copper dissolved than equilibrium allows, and many quenched-in vacancies that will help it diffuse.
3. **Ageing.** Hold it at room temperature (natural ageing) or at about 150 to 190 °C (artificial ageing). The copper comes out of solution as a dense population of tiny particles.

The particles do not go straight to the equilibrium phase θ (Al₂Cu). Its interface with aluminium would cost too much energy, so the nucleation barrier is high. Instead the alloy passes through a sequence of metastable structures, each with an easier interface: first **Guinier–Preston zones**, discs of copper atoms one or two atomic layers thick and a few nanometres across, discovered independently by André Guinier and George Preston in 1938 from faint streaks in X-ray patterns; then the coherent θ″ and semi-coherent θ′; and finally θ. This route, where the first product is not the most stable phase but the easiest to nucleate, is common enough to have a name: Ostwald's step rule.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Look at what is happening down at the atoms: the nucleation barrier grows as the cube of the interface energy, so a phase whose lattice lines up with aluminium's, plane for plane, can nucleate at rates many orders of magnitude higher than the stable θ, whose lattice does not. Kinetics chooses the phase that is easiest to *start*, not the one that is best to *end* with, and engineers harvest the strength of those intermediate structures.

</Narrator>

Why do the particles strengthen the alloy, and why does too much ageing soften it again? Strength comes from obstacles to dislocations (<LessonLink slug="defects-and-dislocations">defects and dislocations</LessonLink>). While the particles are tiny and coherent with the matrix, dislocations shear through them, and the resistance grows as the particles grow. Once the particles are larger and further apart, dislocations find it easier to bow out between them and pass by, leaving loops around them (the Orowan mechanism), and the resistance now *falls* as the spacing grows. The peak strength sits at the crossover. With longer ageing, the particles **coarsen**: large ones grow at the expense of small ones, which dissolve because their high surface-to-volume ratio raises their solubility, and Lifshitz, Slyozov and Wagner showed in 1961 that the mean radius grows as $t^{1/3}$. Fewer, larger, more widely spaced particles mean an **overaged**, softer alloy. <LessonLink slug="elasticity-plasticity-and-strengthening">Elasticity, plasticity and strengthening</LessonLink> puts numbers on both mechanisms.

The same idea strengthens nickel superalloys in jet-engine turbine blades, packed with cube-shaped precipitates of the ordered phase Ni₃(Al,Ti); maraging steels; and some copper alloys for springs and connectors.

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Transformations in research and industry">

**Simulation.** The **phase-field method** describes a microstructure by smooth fields that change across interfaces a few nanometres wide and evolves them from the free energy, predicting dendrites, precipitate shapes and martensite plates without tracking each interface by hand (see Long-Qing Chen's review); open-source frameworks include PRISMS-PF and MOOSE. For precipitation, numerical models combining classical nucleation, growth and coarsening, such as the Kampmann–Wagner approach in MatCalc (Austria) and Thermo-Calc's precipitation module (Sweden), predict particle sizes and ageing curves from a heat-treatment schedule.

**Seeing transformations.** Synchrotron X-ray and neutron diffraction follow phase fractions inside bulk samples in real time during heating and cooling. **Atom-probe tomography** maps individual atoms in three dimensions and can resolve GP zones and the first clusters of solute atoms, the stages that classical theory treats most crudely. Transmission electron microscopy (<LessonLink slug="microscopy">microscopy</LessonLink>) sees precipitates and martensite plates directly.

**Engineering.** Steelmakers design controlled rolling and cooling around CCT diagrams, producing advanced high-strength steels for car bodies with tailored mixtures of ferrite, bainite, martensite and retained austenite. As of 2026, **integrated computational materials engineering** increasingly links process simulation, transformation models and property prediction into one chain, with machine learning used to speed up the individual models (see <LessonLink slug="multiscale-modelling">multiscale modelling</LessonLink>).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The more you undercool, the faster the transformation."** Only for transformations that need no long-range diffusion. For diffusional transformations, speed rises with undercooling at first and then falls, giving the nose of the C-curve. Martensite is the exception that proves the point: it needs no diffusion, so it can form at low temperatures where diffusional transformations are frozen out.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"TTT diagrams tell you what happens when you quench a part."** They describe isothermal holds. A part cooling continuously needs a CCT diagram, whose curves are lower and later, and different positions in a thick part cool at different rates.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Martensite forms because carbon diffuses into the iron fast."** Martensite forms precisely because carbon has no time to diffuse: it is the product of a diffusionless shear that traps carbon in the lattice.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Quenching hardens an aluminium alloy, as it hardens steel."** Straight after the quench, a precipitation-hardening aluminium alloy is at its softest: the supersaturated solution is soft and easily formed. The hardness comes later, during ageing. Aircraft rivets made of some of these alloys used to be kept in freezers after quenching to delay natural ageing until they were driven.

</Misconception>
