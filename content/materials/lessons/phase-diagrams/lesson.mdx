<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Maps for alloy makers">

For most of the twentieth century, electronics were held together by a solder of about 63 % tin and 37 % lead. Pure lead melts at 327 °C and pure tin at 232 °C, yet this mixture melts at only 183 °C, lower than either of its ingredients, and it goes from solid to liquid at a single temperature, like a pure metal, instead of turning mushy over a range. Both properties made it ideal for soldering components without cooking them. When the European Union restricted lead in electronics from 2006, the industry needed another alloy with the same trick, and found it in tin with a few per cent of silver and copper, which melts near 217 °C.

Where does a "lowest melting" composition come from, and how would anyone find the next one? The answer is a **phase diagram**: a map that shows, for every composition and temperature, which phases are present at equilibrium, what each is made of and how much of each there is. Phase diagrams tell a steelmaker which temperature turns a steel into austenite, a foundry why one aluminium alloy pours well, a road engineer why salt stops melting ice in very cold weather, and a chip-maker which metals will react with silicon. They are the most used reference in materials engineering.

This lesson reads phase diagrams and explains where they come from. It begins with definitions and Gibbs's phase rule, works through the three basic kinds of binary diagram, reads the iron–carbon diagram that governs all steels, and ends by computing the free-energy curves that the diagrams are drawn from.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Phases, components and equilibrium">

<Definition term="component">

The **components** of a system are the chemically independent constituents needed to describe its composition: Cu and Ni for copper–nickel alloys, Fe and C for steels, H₂O and NaCl for salty water. A system of two components is **binary**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="phase">

A **phase** is a region of material that is uniform in structure and composition, separated from other phases by boundaries. Ice, liquid water and water vapour are three phases of one component. So are ferrite (body-centred cubic iron) and austenite (face-centred cubic iron): two solids with different crystal structures are different phases. A **solid solution** with dissolved atoms on lattice sites or in the gaps between them is still one phase.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equilibrium and the phase diagram">

At constant temperature and pressure, a system is in **equilibrium** when its Gibbs energy $G$ is as low as it can be. An **equilibrium phase diagram** shows, against temperature and composition (at a fixed pressure, usually 1 atm), which phases have the lowest total $G$. It does not say how long the system takes to get there; that is the subject of <LessonLink slug="phase-transformations">phase transformations</LessonLink>.

</Definition>

Lumenary Chemistry's [gases, liquids and solids](/en/chemistry/gases-liquids-and-solids/) reads one-component diagrams such as water's, plotted against pressure and temperature. Materials scientists mostly work at atmospheric pressure, where the pressure of a solid hardly matters, and want to know what happens when they *mix*. So their diagrams put composition across and temperature up. How many variables can be changed independently while a given set of phases stays in equilibrium? Josiah Willard Gibbs answered in the 1870s.

<Theorem name="The Gibbs phase rule">

For a system of $C$ components with $P$ phases in equilibrium, the number of intensive variables (temperature, pressure, compositions) that can be varied independently without changing the number of phases is

$$F = C - P + 2.$$

At fixed pressure, as on the diagrams in this lesson, $F = C - P + 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Each phase's composition is fixed by $C - 1$ mole fractions (they sum to 1), so with temperature and pressure there are $P(C - 1) + 2$ intensive variables. Equilibrium requires each component to have the same chemical potential in every phase, as the free-energy section below explains: $P - 1$ equations for each of the $C$ components, $C(P - 1)$ in all. The number of free variables is the difference, $P(C-1) + 2 - C(P-1) = C - P + 2$. Fixing the pressure removes one more.

</Proof>

For a binary alloy at fixed pressure, $F = 3 - P$. A single phase has $F = 2$: temperature and composition can both change, so single phases occupy **areas** of the diagram. Two phases have $F = 1$: choose the temperature and the compositions of both phases are fixed, which is why two-phase regions are crossed by horizontal **tie lines**. Three phases have $F = 0$: they can coexist only at one temperature, with fixed compositions, so three-phase equilibria appear as **horizontal lines** called invariant reactions. A binary diagram can never show four phases in equilibrium.

</Section>

<Section id="isomorphous" kind="computation" title="Complete solubility and the lever rule">

The simplest binary diagram belongs to two metals that dissolve in each other completely, in the solid as well as the liquid. Copper and nickel are the classic pair: both are face-centred cubic, their atoms differ in size by about 2 %, and they have similar electronegativities and valences. (These are the conditions William Hume-Rothery identified for wide solid solubility, discussed further in <LessonLink slug="metals-and-alloys">metals and alloys</LessonLink>.) Their diagram has just two curves: the **liquidus**, above which everything is liquid, and the **solidus**, below which everything is solid. Between them, solid and liquid coexist, and because the shape is a lens it is often called a lens diagram. Such a system is **isomorphous**.

The shape of the lens can be computed. If both the liquid and the solid are **ideal solutions**, a component's chemical potential in a phase is its pure value plus $RT\ln x$, and equating chemical potentials between solid and liquid for each element gives $x^\text{liquid}/x^\text{solid} = \exp(-\Delta G_\text{fus}/RT)$, with $\Delta G_\text{fus} = \Delta H_\text{fus}(1 - T/T_\text{m})$. Two such equations, one per element, fix both compositions at each temperature. The block computes the lens from rounded handbook values of the melting points and enthalpies of fusion of copper and nickel, then applies the lever rule, stated after the block, to an alloy with nickel mole fraction 0.44 at 1250 °C.

```python run
import numpy as np

R = 8.314                                   # J/(mol K)
# Melting points (K) and enthalpies of fusion (J/mol): rounded handbook values.
Tm = {"Cu": 1357.8, "Ni": 1728.0}
Hf = {"Cu": 13.26e3, "Ni": 17.48e3}

def lens(T):
    """Mole fractions of Ni in the solid and the liquid in equilibrium at T (K),
    if both phases are ideal solutions: x_liquid / x_solid = exp(-dG_fusion / RT) for each element."""
    kCu = np.exp(-Hf["Cu"] * (1 - T / Tm["Cu"]) / (R * T))
    kNi = np.exp(-Hf["Ni"] * (1 - T / Tm["Ni"]) / (R * T))
    x_solid = (1 - kCu) / (kNi - kCu)
    return x_solid, kNi * x_solid

print(" T (C)    solidus x_Ni   liquidus x_Ni")
for T_c in range(1100, 1451, 50):
    xs, xl = lens(T_c + 273.15)
    print(f"{T_c:5d}    {xs:12.3f}   {xl:13.3f}")

# The lever rule: an alloy with x_Ni = 0.44 held at 1250 C.
x0, T = 0.44, 1250 + 273.15
xs, xl = lens(T)
f_solid = (x0 - xl) / (xs - xl)
print(f"\nat 1250 C the tie line runs from liquid x_Ni = {xl:.3f} to solid x_Ni = {xs:.3f}")
print(f"an alloy with x_Ni = {x0} is {100 * f_solid:.1f} % solid and {100 * (1 - f_solid):.1f} % liquid (by moles)")

# Where does this alloy start and finish freezing?  Bisection on the liquidus and solidus.
def crossing(which):
    lo, hi = Tm["Cu"] + 0.01, Tm["Ni"] - 0.01        # x_Ni along each curve rises with T
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        x = lens(mid)[which]
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if x < x0 else (lo, mid)
    return mid - 273.15
print(f"it starts to freeze at {crossing(1):.0f} C (liquidus) and is solid below {crossing(0):.0f} C (solidus)")
```

At every temperature the solid is richer in nickel, the higher-melting metal, than the liquid it is in equilibrium with: at 1250 °C the tie line runs from liquid with $x_\text{Ni} = 0.402$ to solid with $x_\text{Ni} = 0.474$. The ratio of the two, $k = x_\text{solid}/x_\text{liquid} = 1.18$ for nickel here, is the **partition** (or distribution) **coefficient**. It decides how a solute is redistributed when an alloy freezes, from the uneven composition of castings to zone refining, which purifies silicon and germanium for electronics by sweeping a molten zone along a bar so that impurities with $k \lt 1$ are carried to one end. The alloy with $x_\text{Ni} = 0.44$ starts to freeze at 1264 °C, is 52.5 % solid at 1250 °C, and is completely solid below 1237 °C.

<Theorem name="The lever rule">

On a tie line from phase α of composition $x_\alpha$ to phase β of composition $x_\beta$, an alloy of overall composition $x_0$ consists of the fractions

$$f_\beta = \frac{x_0 - x_\alpha}{x_\beta - x_\alpha}, \qquad f_\alpha = \frac{x_\beta - x_0}{x_\beta - x_\alpha} = 1 - f_\beta.$$

The fractions are by moles when the compositions are mole fractions, and by mass when they are mass fractions.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Conservation of matter: the total amount of the alloy is $f_\alpha + f_\beta = 1$, and the amount of component B is the same whether counted in the alloy or in the two phases, $x_0 = f_\alpha x_\alpha + f_\beta x_\beta$. Substituting $f_\alpha = 1 - f_\beta$ and solving gives the result. Geometrically, $f_\alpha (x_0 - x_\alpha) = f_\beta (x_\beta - x_0)$: the alloy point is the balance point of a lever with masses $f_\alpha$ and $f_\beta$ at its ends, which is where the name comes from.

</Proof>

The rule is easy to get backwards. The fraction of β is the length of the arm on the **α side** divided by the whole tie line: the closer the alloy sits to one end, the more of that phase it contains. For the alloy above, $f_\text{solid} = (0.44 - 0.402)/(0.474 - 0.402) = 0.525$.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Two warnings about the model. First, it is ideal: the assessed copper–nickel diagram in ASM's database has a somewhat wider lens, because real Cu–Ni solutions have a small positive heat of mixing, which at a few hundred degrees Celsius even opens a gap in the solid. Second, every number here is for equilibrium, which a casting cooling in minutes never reaches; the first solid to form stays nickel-rich in the middle of each grain, an effect called coring that <LessonLink slug="processing-and-heat-treatment">processing and heat treatment</LessonLink> calculates.

</Narrator>

<Section id="eutectics" kind="examples" title="Eutectics and other invariant reactions">

Most pairs of metals do not dissolve in each other completely in the solid. Lead dissolves at most about 18 wt% tin, and tin barely any lead, because their crystal structures differ. Their diagram has two solid phases, α (lead-rich, face-centred cubic) and β (tin-rich, tetragonal), each a single phase over a narrow range of composition, with a two-phase field α + β between them. The two liquidus lines fall from the melting points of the pure metals and meet at the **eutectic point**, from the Greek for "easily melted".

At the eutectic point three phases coexist, so $F = 0$ and the **eutectic reaction**

$$\text{L}\ (61.9\ \text{wt\% Sn}) \;\longrightarrow\; \alpha\ (18.3\ \text{wt\% Sn}) + \beta\ (97.8\ \text{wt\% Sn})$$

happens at exactly 183 °C. A liquid of the eutectic composition freezes completely at that one temperature into a fine mixture of the two solids, often as alternating plates (lamellae) a few micrometres or less apart, because atoms only have to diffuse sideways from one plate to the next. The eutectic is not a compound: it is a **microconstituent**, a recognisable arrangement of two phases.

<Example title="A 40 wt% Sn solder cooling through the eutectic">

An alloy with 40 wt% tin starts to freeze when it crosses the α liquidus, at about 240 °C, and α crystals grow as it cools. Just above 183 °C, the tie line runs from α (18.3 wt% Sn) to liquid (61.9 wt% Sn), so

$$f_\alpha = \frac{61.9 - 40}{61.9 - 18.3} = 0.502, \qquad f_\text{L} = 0.498.$$

At 183 °C that remaining liquid, which now has exactly the eutectic composition, freezes as eutectic. Just below 183 °C the phases are α and β, with $f_\alpha = (97.8 - 40)/(97.8 - 18.3) = 0.727$ and $f_\beta = 0.273$. So the solid contains 50.2 % primary α, which formed first as rounded or tree-like grains, and 49.8 % eutectic, which itself is 22.5 % (of the whole) α and 27.3 % β. A metallographer polishing and etching the solder would see large α grains in a fine striped matrix, and could check these numbers by measuring the area fractions. The lever rule gives the amounts of **phases** or of **microconstituents**, depending on which tie line is used.

</Example>

<Narrator who="margaret">

The same diagram explains road salt. Salt and water form a eutectic at about 23 wt% NaCl and −21 °C, so no salty water can stay liquid below −21 °C, and near that temperature salt melts ice only slowly. That is why road services switch to other de-icers, such as calcium chloride, whose eutectic with water is near −50 °C, or to sand and grit, in very cold weather.

</Narrator>

Three-phase reactions come in a few kinds, all horizontal lines on a binary diagram. In each, one phase on cooling becomes two, or two become one.

| Reaction | On cooling | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| eutectic | L → α + β | Pb–Sn at 183 °C; Al–Si casting alloys at 577 °C |
| eutectoid | γ → α + β (all solid) | austenite → ferrite + cementite in steel at 727 °C |
| peritectic | L + α → β | in Fe–C near 1495 °C; in many copper alloys |

Diagrams can also contain **intermediate compounds** such as Fe₃C, Mg₂Si or the intermetallics in solder joints, which appear as vertical lines or narrow single-phase fields and split a complicated diagram into simpler pieces.

</Section>

The most important feature is the **eutectoid** at 727 °C and 0.76 wt% carbon, where austenite becomes ferrite plus cementite: $\gamma \to \alpha + \mathrm{Fe_3C}$. Because the reaction happens in the solid, it produces a very fine lamellar mixture called **pearlite** (it looks like mother-of-pearl under the microscope). A steel with exactly 0.76 wt% carbon, slowly cooled, is all pearlite. A **hypoeutectoid** steel with less carbon first grows ferrite at the austenite grain boundaries as it cools from the A₃ line to 727 °C, and the remaining austenite, enriched to 0.76 wt%, then becomes pearlite. A **hypereutectoid** steel grows cementite first, below the $A_\text{cm}$ line. Steels above 2.14 wt% carbon would meet a eutectic at 1147 °C and 4.30 wt% carbon; those are the **cast irons**, which melt at lower temperatures and pour well.

The block applies the lever rule just below 727 °C across the steel range, both to microconstituents (proeutectoid phase and pearlite) and to phases (total ferrite and cementite).

```python run
# The steel corner of the iron-carbon (Fe-Fe3C) diagram, just below the eutectoid at 727 C.
# Compositions in wt% carbon (assessments differ slightly: the eutectoid is quoted as 0.76-0.77).
C_ferrite, C_eutectoid, C_cementite = 0.022, 0.76, 6.70

def lever(c, left, right):
    """Fraction of the right-hand phase for overall composition c on a tie line left-right."""
    return (c - left) / (right - left)

print(" wt% C   proeutectoid       pearlite   total ferrite   total cementite")
for c in (0.05, 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.76, 1.00, 1.40):
    if c <= C_eutectoid:            # hypoeutectoid: ferrite forms first, between A3 and 727 C
        pearlite = lever(c, C_ferrite, C_eutectoid)
        first = f"ferrite {1 - pearlite:5.3f}"
    else:                           # hypereutectoid: cementite forms first, on the grain boundaries
        pearlite = 1 - lever(c, C_eutectoid, C_cementite)
        first = f"cementite {1 - pearlite:3.3f}"
    cementite = lever(c, C_ferrite, C_cementite)
    print(f"{c:6.2f}   {first:>16}   {pearlite:8.3f}   {1 - cementite:13.3f}   {cementite:15.3f}")

inside = lever(C_eutectoid, C_ferrite, C_cementite)
print(f"\npearlite itself is {100 * inside:.1f} % cementite and {100 * (1 - inside):.1f} % ferrite by mass")

# The eutectoid composition in atom per cent, for comparison with cementite, Fe3C (25 at% C).
M_C, M_Fe = 12.011, 55.845
for name, w in (("eutectoid", C_eutectoid), ("cementite", C_cementite)):
    nC, nFe = w / M_C, (100 - w) / M_Fe
    print(f"{name:9}: {w:.2f} wt% C = {100 * nC / (nC + nFe):.1f} at% C")
```

A 0.40 wt% carbon steel, typical of shafts and bolts, is about half proeutectoid ferrite and half pearlite (0.488 and 0.512 by mass), and only 5.7 % of it is cementite. Pearlite itself is 11.1 % cementite, so its lamellae of ferrite are about eight times thicker than those of cementite. As carbon rises the fraction of hard cementite rises in proportion, which is why strength and hardness increase and ductility falls; past the eutectoid, the proeutectoid cementite forms a brittle network along the old austenite grain boundaries, which is why plain high-carbon steels need careful heat treatment. The eutectoid composition is only 3.4 atom per cent carbon: a little carbon goes a long way.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Strictly, this is not an equilibrium diagram. Cementite is **metastable**: the truly stable form of carbon in iron is graphite, and the stable iron–graphite diagram has slightly different lines. In steels, graphite forms too slowly to matter, so the Fe–Fe₃C diagram is the useful one. In cast irons, silicon and slow cooling let graphite form, giving grey cast iron with graphite flakes, which is why foundries need both diagrams. Alloying elements in real steels also shift every line on the diagram.

</Narrator>

<Section id="free-energy" kind="computation" title="Free-energy curves behind the diagrams">

Where do the lines come from? At each temperature, every possible phase has a molar Gibbs energy that depends on composition, $G(x)$. Lumenary Chemistry's [entropy and free energy](/en/chemistry/entropy-and-free-energy/) explains why equilibrium is where $G$ is lowest, and its [chemical equilibrium](/en/chemistry/chemical-equilibrium/) applies the idea to reacting mixtures. For a mixture, though, "lowest" has a twist: an alloy need not be one phase. It can split into two phases of different compositions, and its Gibbs energy is then the lever-rule average of theirs, which lies on the straight line joining the two points on the $G(x)$ curves.

<Theorem name="The common-tangent construction">

At fixed temperature and pressure, two phases with compositions $x_\alpha$ and $x_\beta$ are in equilibrium when one straight line is tangent to the curve $G_\alpha(x)$ at $x_\alpha$ and to $G_\beta(x)$ at $x_\beta$. For any overall composition between them, the two-phase mixture has a lower Gibbs energy than any single phase. The phase boundaries of the diagram at that temperature are the tangent points.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For a two-phase mixture of overall composition $x_0$, $G_\text{mix} = f_\alpha G_\alpha(x_\alpha) + f_\beta G_\beta(x_\beta)$ with the lever-rule fractions, which is the value at $x_0$ of the straight line through $(x_\alpha, G_\alpha)$ and $(x_\beta, G_\beta)$. Minimising over the choice of $x_\alpha$ and $x_\beta$ means lowering that chord as far as possible; it reaches its lowest position when it touches both curves without crossing either, that is, when it is tangent to both. The tangent's intercepts at $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ are the chemical potentials of the two components, so the condition is the same as equal chemical potentials of each component in both phases.

</Proof>

The simplest model that produces a two-phase region is the **regular solution**. Mixing atoms A and B adds an ideal entropy of mixing, which always favours mixing, and an enthalpy of mixing $\Omega x(1-x)$, where the interaction parameter $\Omega$ is positive when A–B bonds are weaker than the average of A–A and B–B bonds:

$$G_\text{mix}(x) = \Omega\,x(1-x) + RT\,\big[x\ln x + (1-x)\ln(1-x)\big].$$

At high temperature the entropy term wins and $G_\text{mix}$ is a single bowl: one solution at every composition. Below the **critical temperature** $T_\text{c} = \Omega/2R$ the curve develops two minima with a hump between them, and compositions under the hump split into two phases: a **miscibility gap**. Inside the gap there is a smaller region where $\partial^2 G/\partial x^2 \lt 0$, bounded by the **spinodal**, where the solution is unstable even to tiny fluctuations and separates without needing to nucleate, which is spinodal decomposition (see <LessonLink slug="phase-transformations">phase transformations</LessonLink>). The block computes the gap for $\Omega = 20$ kJ mol⁻¹.

```python run
import numpy as np

R = 8.314             # J/(mol K)
W = 20e3              # interaction parameter Omega, J/mol (positive: A-B bonds are unfavourable)

def G_mix(x, T):
    """Gibbs energy of mixing of a regular solution, J/mol."""
    return W * x * (1 - x) + R * T * (x * np.log(x) + (1 - x) * np.log(1 - x))

def binodal(T):
    """Left edge of the miscibility gap: for this symmetric model the common tangent is
    horizontal, so solve dG/dx = W(1 - 2x) + RT ln(x/(1-x)) = 0 for 0 < x < 1/2 by bisection."""
    lo, hi = 1e-12, 0.5 - 1e-9
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        slope = W * (1 - 2 * mid) + R * T * np.log(mid / (1 - mid))
        lo, hi = (lo, mid) if slope > 0 else (mid, hi)
    return mid

def spinodal(T):
    """Where d2G/dx2 = RT/(x(1-x)) - 2W = 0, i.e. x(1-x) = RT/(2W)."""
    return 0.5 - np.sqrt(0.25 - R * T / (2 * W))

Tc = W / (2 * R)
print(f"critical temperature Tc = W/2R = {Tc:.1f} K\n")
print(" T (K)   gap from x =   to x =   spinodal from   to")
for T in (600, 800, 1000, 1100, 1150, 1200):
    b, s = binodal(T), spinodal(T)
    print(f"{T:5d}   {b:12.4f}   {1 - b:6.4f}   {s:13.4f}   {1 - s:6.4f}")

# Below Tc a solution inside the gap lowers its Gibbs energy by splitting into two phases.
# The common tangent touches the curve at the two binodal compositions; the alloy's
# Gibbs energy as a two-phase mixture lies on that line.
T = 900
b = binodal(T)
tangent = G_mix(b, T)                       # horizontal here, by symmetry
print(f"\nat {T} K the common tangent touches G_mix at x = {b:.3f} and {1 - b:.3f}, at {tangent:.0f} J/mol")
print("    x    one solution (J/mol)   two phases (J/mol)   gain from splitting")
for x in (0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50):
    g = G_mix(x, T)
    print(f"{x:5.2f}   {g:20.0f}   {tangent:18.0f}   {g - tangent:19.0f}")
```

The critical temperature is 1202.8 K. At 600 K the gap spans almost the whole range, from $x = 0.021$ to $0.979$; it narrows as the temperature rises and closes at $T_\text{c}$, giving the dome in the figure. At 900 K a 50:50 solution would have $G_\text{mix} = -187$ J mol⁻¹, but by splitting into two phases with $x = 0.111$ and $0.889$ it reaches $-635$ J mol⁻¹, a gain of 448 J mol⁻¹. Real systems with gaps of this kind include gold–nickel and the aluminium–zinc alloys, and the same physics, with $\Omega$ written in terms of polymer–polymer contacts, explains why most pairs of polymers do not mix.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here is the idea to take away: a phase diagram is a picture of the **lower convex envelope** of all the free-energy curves, read off one temperature at a time. Wherever a single curve is the envelope, that phase alone is stable; wherever the envelope is a straight common tangent, two phases share the alloy. Databases such as the Materials Project build the same envelope, which they call the convex hull, from energies computed by <LessonLink slug="density-functional-theory">density functional theory</LessonLink> at absolute zero.

</Narrator>

<Section id="real-diagrams" kind="human" title="Reading real diagrams">

Real phase diagrams are measured and then **assessed**: specialists collect every experiment on a system, from thermal analysis, X-ray diffraction of quenched samples and microscopy, and fit a consistent thermodynamic description. The standard collections are ASM International's Alloy Phase Diagram Database and its handbooks for metals, and the NIST/ACerS Phase Equilibria Diagrams (NIST Standard Reference Database 31) for ceramics; both are in the references. A few habits make reading them safer:

- **Check the units and the axis.** Diagrams are drawn in weight per cent or atom per cent, often with the other scale along the top. The difference is large for elements of very different mass: 0.76 wt% carbon in iron is 3.4 at%.
- **Check whether lines are measured or estimated.** Assessments draw uncertain boundaries dashed. Low-temperature solvus lines are often uncertain because equilibrium there takes years to reach.
- **Check stable or metastable.** The iron–cementite diagram is metastable; many diagrams in handbooks show both.
- **For three or more components, use sections.** Real alloys have many elements. Ternary diagrams are shown as triangular **isothermal sections** at one temperature, or as **isopleths** (vertical sections at one fixed ratio), which do not obey the lever rule because tie lines leave the section.

Since the 1970s, following Larry Kaufman's work, diagrams have increasingly been **calculated** by the **CALPHAD** method. Each phase gets a model of $G(T, x)$ with parameters fitted to experiments and to first-principles calculations, stored in databases, and the equilibrium is found by minimising the total $G$, which is the common-tangent construction done numerically in many dimensions. Commercial packages such as Thermo-Calc (Sweden), FactSage (Canada and Germany) and Pandat (United States), and open-source codes such as OpenCalphad and pycalphad, do this for alloys of a dozen elements. CALPHAD made it practical to explore **high-entropy alloys**, introduced in 2004 by groups in the United Kingdom and Taiwan: alloys of five or more elements in near-equal amounts, which live in the middle of multicomponent diagrams that nobody had mapped. As of 2026, first-principles and machine-learned calculations are feeding more and more of the parameters in these databases, especially for systems where experiments are scarce (see <LessonLink slug="ai-materials-discovery">AI-assisted materials discovery</LessonLink>).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The lever rule gives the compositions of the phases."** The ends of the tie line give the compositions; the lever rule gives the amounts. And the amount of a phase is the length of the *opposite* arm divided by the whole tie line.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A phase diagram shows the microstructure you will get."** It shows equilibrium. A steel quenched from austenite forms martensite, which is not on the diagram at all, and a casting cooled in minutes is cored. The diagram says where the system is heading; kinetics, the subject of the next lesson, says how far it gets.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A eutectic is a compound with its own formula."** A eutectic is a fine mixture of two phases that forms at one composition and temperature. Its composition is set by the shapes of the free-energy curves, not by a ratio of whole numbers, and it changes, slightly, with pressure.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A phase means solid, liquid or gas."** Those are states of matter. A solid can contain several phases, such as ferrite and cementite in pearlite, and some systems have two liquid phases, like oil and water.

</Misconception>
