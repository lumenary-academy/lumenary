<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What is it made of?">

Look around the room you are in. A metal spoon, a glass window, a plastic bottle, a wooden chair, a cotton T-shirt, a ceramic mug, a brick wall. Every one of them was made from a **material** that somebody chose, and usually for good reasons. A spoon made of glass would snap in the washing-up. A window made of steel would keep the rain out but also the light. A bottle made of concrete would be far too heavy to carry.

**Materials science** asks two questions about every material: *why does it behave the way it does?* and *how can we make it better?* The answers have given us everything from stronger, lighter aircraft to smaller phones and safer bicycle helmets. They begin with sorting materials into families and learning the words for what they are like.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Hello, I'm Chloe! My favourite question about any material is: what is happening *inside* it, down at the level of its atoms? A spoon bends and a mug shatters because of how their atoms are joined. Keep that question in your pocket: in this lesson we describe materials from the outside, and in the next one we look inside.

</Narrator>

<Section id="families" kind="definitions" title="The five families of materials">

There are many thousands of different materials, but almost all of them belong to one of five big families. Materials in the same family tend to behave in similar ways, so knowing the family already tells you a lot.

<Definition term="the five families of materials">

1. **Metals**, such as iron, aluminium, copper and gold, and **alloys**, which are metals mixed with small amounts of other elements. Steel is iron with a little carbon; brass is copper with zinc.
2. **Ceramics and glasses**, made from clay, sand and rock: compounds of elements such as silicon, aluminium and calcium with oxygen. Pottery, bricks, tiles, porcelain, cement and window glass belong here.
3. **Polymers**, made of very long molecules, like chains thousands of atoms long. Most plastics and rubbers are polymers.
4. **Composites**, which combine two or more materials so that each makes up for the other's weaknesses: carbon fibres glued in plastic, glass fibres in a boat hull, steel bars cast inside concrete.
5. **Natural materials**, which grow: wood, bamboo, cotton, wool, silk, leather, cork, bone and shell.

</Definition>

| Family | What it is usually like | At home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metals | shiny, heavy, carries heat and electricity well, bends before it breaks | cutlery, saucepans, keys, wires, drinks cans |
| Ceramics and glasses | hard, stiff, copes with heat, never rusts, but **brittle**: it cracks instead of bending | mugs, plates, tiles, sinks, windows, the walls of the house |
| Polymers | light, easy to shape, does not carry electricity, softens when hot | bottles, toothbrushes, phone cases, rubber bands, polyester clothes |
| Composites | very strong or stiff for their weight, often expensive | carbon-fibre bicycles and tennis rackets, fibreglass boats, reinforced-concrete floors |
| Natural materials | light, often strong along the grain or fibre, renewable | wooden furniture, cotton and wool clothes, cork boards, leather shoes |

The families overlap at the edges. Stone is a natural material and also a ceramic. Wood is a natural composite: long fibres of cellulose (a polymer) glued together by another polymer, lignin. Concrete is a ceramic made by gluing sand and stones together with cement paste, which makes it a kind of composite too. Families are a tool for thinking, not a set of locked boxes.

</Section>

<Section id="properties" kind="definitions" title="Properties: what a material is like">

To choose a material you need words, and numbers, for what it is like. These are its **properties**. Six of the most important are below.

<Definition term="density">

**Density** is how much mass is packed into each bit of space: mass divided by volume. It is measured in grams per cubic centimetre (g/cm³). Water's density is 1 g/cm³: one cubic centimetre, a little cube 1 cm on each side, has a mass of 1 gram. Anything denser than water sinks in it, and anything less dense floats.

</Definition>

<Definition term="stiffness, strength, hardness and toughness">

- **Stiffness** is how much a material resists being bent or stretched while it is still springy, so that it goes back to its shape when you let go. A steel ruler is stiff; a rubber band is not.
- **Strength** is how big a load a material can carry before it gives way, either by bending out of shape for good or by breaking. A steel wire is strong; a cotton thread of the same thickness is not.
- **Hardness** is how well a surface resists scratches and dents. Glass is hard; candle wax is soft.
- **Toughness** is how well a material resists cracking. A tough material bends or dents and soaks up a blow; a **brittle** material cracks and shatters. Copper is tough; glass is brittle.

</Definition>

<Definition term="conductivity">

**Thermal conductivity** is how well a material carries heat, and **electrical conductivity** how well it carries an electric current. Metals are good conductors of both. Most ceramics, glasses, polymers and natural materials are poor conductors, called **insulators**.

</Definition>

These words mean different things, and a material can score well on one and badly on another. Glass is **stiff** and **hard**, but not **tough**: a glass that slips from your hand onto a tiled floor shatters. A rubber band is not stiff at all, but it is quite **tough**: you can stretch it, twist it and stand on it. A steel spring is stiff, strong and tough at once, which is why steel is so useful.

<Narrator who="margaret">

I'm Margaret, and I like to ask: what does the test actually measure? To find a material's strength, a laboratory clamps a sample, often shaped like a dog bone, in a machine that pulls it slowly apart while recording the force and how much the sample stretches. Strength is the pull it survived divided by the area of its cross-section, so a thick bar and a thin wire of the same steel get the same strength. That is why strengths are given in **megapascals** (MPa): one megapascal is a pull of one newton on every square millimetre.

</Narrator>

<Section id="materials-hunt" kind="examples" title="A materials hunt at home">

You can test materials with things already in your kitchen. Do these with an adult, use only everyday objects, and leave out anything sharp, hot, breakable or valuable.

<Example title="Sort the kitchen into families">

Collect ten small objects: a metal spoon, a wooden spoon, a ceramic mug, a plastic bottle top, a cork, a rubber band, a candle, a cotton tea towel, a drinks can and a pebble from the garden. Sort them into the five families. Which were easy? Which made you argue? (A candle is a puzzle: paraffin wax is made of chain molecules like a polymer's, only much shorter, so most people put it with the polymers. A pebble is a natural ceramic; a drinks can is a metal with a thin coat of plastic on the inside.)

</Example>

<Example title="Float or sink: a density test">

Fill a large bowl with water and lower each object in gently. The cork, the wooden spoon, the candle and the bottle top float: they are less dense than water. The metal spoon and the pebble sink: they are denser. Now try an apple, and a lump of plasticine, first as a ball and then shaped into a little open boat.

</Example>

<Example title="Which feels coldest? A conductivity test">

Leave a metal spoon, a wooden spoon and a plastic spoon on the table for an hour, so that they are all at room temperature. Then touch each with the back of your hand. The metal one feels coldest, although a thermometer would show all three at the same temperature. Your skin is warmer than the room, and metal carries heat away from your skin much faster than wood or plastic does. What you feel is not the temperature of the spoon but how fast it takes heat from you.

</Example>

<Example title="Scratch and magnet tests">

**Hardness.** Try to scratch each object gently with your fingernail. It marks candle wax easily and dents soft wood, but cannot scratch the mug's glaze or the steel spoon. In 1812 the German geologist Friedrich Mohs made a scale like this for minerals: each one scratches those below it. Talc is 1, quartz 7 and diamond 10, the hardest. Your fingernail comes in at about 2½ and window glass at about 5½.

**Magnetism.** Hold a fridge magnet to each metal object. A steel food can is pulled hard; an aluminium drinks can is not attracted at all. Recycling plants use exactly this difference, with giant magnets, to separate steel from aluminium, as you will see in <LessonLink slug="choosing-and-reusing-materials">Choosing and reusing materials</LessonLink>.

</Example>

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon, and I look for the ways a test can fool us. The plasticine boat floats even though plasticine is denser than water, because a boat's shape traps air, so the float test only measures density when the object is solid all through. And try the magnet on your cutlery: much stainless-steel cutlery is hardly magnetic at all, because of how its atoms are arranged, so "not magnetic" does not prove "not steel".

</Narrator>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Densities and strengths in Python">

Here are nine materials, at least one from every family, with their densities and strengths. The program does two things. First it works out how heavy one litre of each would be (a cube 10 cm on each side, the same volume as a one-litre carton of juice), and whether it would float in water. Then it asks a stranger question: if you hung a long rod of each material from the top of a very tall tower, how long could the rod be before it snapped under its own weight? That length depends only on strength divided by density, so it is a fair way to compare **strength for weight**. Press **Run**:

```python run
# Nine materials from the five families, with typical values (rounded).
# density: grams per cubic centimetre (g/cm3)
# strength: megapascals (MPa), the pull on each square millimetre, in newtons,
#           that the material can take before it gives way
materials = [
    # name                          family        density  strength
    ("steel (grade S355)",          "metal",       7.85,    355),
    ("aluminium alloy (6061-T6)",   "metal",       2.70,    276),
    ("titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4V)",  "metal",       4.43,    880),
    ("copper (soft, annealed)",     "metal",       8.96,     69),
    ("window glass",                "glass",       2.50,     45),
    ("concrete (grade C30/37)",     "ceramic",     2.40,    2.9),
    ("polyethylene (HDPE)",         "polymer",     0.95,     25),
    ("carbon-fibre composite",      "composite",   1.55,    550),
    ("softwood timber (C24)",       "natural",     0.42,   14.5),
]

g = 9.81  # the pull of gravity, newtons per kilogram

print("1. How heavy is one litre (a 10 cm cube)? Will it float in water?")
for name, family, density, strength in sorted(materials, key=lambda m: m[2]):
    floats = "floats" if density < 1.0 else "sinks"
    print(f"   {name:28} {density:5.2f} kg   {floats}")

print()
print("2. Hang a long rod of each material from the top. How long could it be")
print("   before it snaps under its own weight?")
results = []
for name, family, density, strength in materials:
    # strength (newtons per square metre) / weight of a 1 m column (newtons per square metre)
    length_m = (strength * 1e6) / (density * 1000 * g)
    results.append((length_m / 1000, name))
for km, name in sorted(results, reverse=True):
    print(f"   {name:28} {km:6.2f} km  " + "#" * round(km))
```

The first list shows that a litre of copper weighs 8.96 kg, more than twenty times as much as a litre of softwood (0.42 kg), and that only the wood and the polyethylene would float. The second list holds the surprise. Steel is one of the strongest materials in the table, yet a steel rod could hang only about **4.6 km** before snapping, because steel is also heavy. The aluminium alloy is a little weaker than this steel but only about a third as dense, so it manages **10.4 km**; the titanium alloy reaches about **20 km**, and the carbon-fibre composite **36 km**. Concrete, pulled rather than squashed, manages only 0.12 km, about **120 m**. That is why aircraft are built from aluminium, titanium and carbon fibre, and why concrete is always used where it will be squashed, or with steel bars inside it to take the pulling.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Try changing the program: add a row for your own material, or change the steel's strength to 1000 MPa, which some heat-treated steels reach. Notice that the rod's thickness never appears in the calculation: a thicker rod is stronger, but heavier by exactly the same factor, so it can hang no further.

</Narrator>

### Where the numbers come from

Numbers about materials should always come with a source, because every grade of a material is a little different.

- **Densities.** The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) publishes [measured densities of every element](https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/tab1.html) and of [many common materials](https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/XrayMassCoef/tab2.html): iron 7.874, aluminium 2.699, titanium 4.540 and copper 8.960 g/cm³, polyethylene 0.93 and ordinary concrete 2.30. Alloys and grades differ slightly. Steel is usually taken as 7.85, the value in the European design standard for steel structures, and the titanium alloy is lighter than pure titanium because it is mixed with aluminium, a lighter metal.
- **Strengths.** Several grade names are their strengths. **S355** steel is guaranteed to carry at least 355 MPa before it starts to bend for good, and the **30** in concrete C30/37 is its guaranteed strength in megapascals when *squashed*; the European design standard for concrete gives its strength when pulled as 2.9 MPa, ten times less. Softwood timber (pine or spruce) of class **C24** has a mean density of 420 kg/m³ (0.42 g/cm³) and is expected to take about 14.5 MPa when pulled along the grain, and window glass about 45 MPa when bent; both values come from European product standards. The values for the aluminium alloy, titanium alloy, copper and polyethylene are typical figures from published data sheets ([MatWeb](https://www.matweb.com/) collects many), and the carbon-fibre value is near the low end of the range given in Michael Ashby's book (see the references).

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice that "strength" means different things in that list. For the metals and the plastic it is the pull at which they start to bend out of shape for good; for glass, concrete and wood it is the load at which they crack. Some are values nearly every piece is guaranteed to beat (the steel, the wood and the glass); the others are typical values. Glass's real strength depends mostly on the tiny scratches on its surface, which is why two panes that look the same can break at very different loads.

</Narrator>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="How materials scientists measure and look things up">

In a real testing laboratory, materials are measured by methods written down in **standards**, so that a test done in São Paulo, Pune or Stuttgart gives the same answer. Standards organisations such as [ISO](https://www.iso.org/) and [ASTM International](https://www.astm.org/) publish them. The pulling test Margaret described is called a **tensile test**. **Hardness** is measured by pressing a tiny diamond point into the surface with a known force and measuring the size of the dent. **Toughness** can be measured by swinging a heavy pendulum into a notched bar and seeing how much energy it takes to break it: a brittle bar snaps cleanly, while a tough one bends and tears.

Nobody measures everything from scratch. Engineers look properties up in handbooks, in manufacturers' data sheets and in databases. Some databases hold measurements, like the NIST tables above. Others hold **calculations**: the [Materials Project](https://materialsproject.org/), run from the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory in the United States, lists properties *computed* from the laws of physics for well over a hundred thousand crystals, many of which have never been made. Later in Lumenary Materials you will learn to do such calculations yourself, in <LessonLink slug="atomistic-simulation">Atomistic simulation</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="density-functional-theory">Density functional theory</LessonLink>.

Materials scientists work wherever things are made: in steelworks and chip factories, in hospitals designing hip joints from titanium and ceramics, in battery laboratories, in companies making clothes, paints and packaging, and in museums working out how ancient objects were made. Lumenary ME's [structures](/en/me/structures/) shows how engineers put strong and stiff materials to work, and Lumenary Chemistry's [what is matter?](/en/chemistry/what-is-matter/) starts with the particles everything is made of.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Strong, stiff, hard and tough all mean the same thing."** They are four different properties. Glass is stiff and hard but brittle, so not tough. A rubber band is tough but not stiff. A good engineer always asks *which* kind of "strong" a job needs.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Heavy materials are stronger."** Lead is very dense but soft and weak, while carbon-fibre composites are light and very strong. Density and strength are separate properties, and the most useful materials for anything that moves are often the ones that are strong *for their weight*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Glass is weak."** Glass is very stiff and very strong when squashed, and thin, freshly made glass fibres are stronger than most steels, which is why they are used to reinforce boats and wind-turbine blades. Glass is *brittle*: a scratch or a chip lets a crack start and run straight through. Toughened glass, used in car side windows and oven doors, is treated so that its surface is permanently squeezed, which makes it much harder for a crack to open.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"All metals stick to magnets."** Only a few do strongly: iron, most steels, nickel and cobalt. Aluminium, copper, gold, silver and much stainless steel do not.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Plastic is one material."** There are dozens of common plastics, each a different polymer with different properties. A soft carrier bag, a clear fizzy-drinks bottle and a hard toy brick are made of three different ones, which matters a great deal when they are recycled.

</Misconception>
