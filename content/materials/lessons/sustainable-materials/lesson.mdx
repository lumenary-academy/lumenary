<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A quarter of the problem is made of stuff">

Most discussions of climate change are about energy: power stations, cars, heating. But about a quarter of the world's greenhouse-gas emissions come from *making materials* — Edgar Hertwich's input–output analysis put materials production at 23 per cent of global emissions in 2015, up from 15 per cent in 1995, with steel, cement, plastics and aluminium leading. Some of those emissions are fuel and electricity and will fall as energy decarbonises. Some are chemistry: carbon is how iron is pulled from its ore, and carbon dioxide is what limestone releases when it becomes cement. Those need new materials science.

Lumenary Chemistry's [sustainable chemistry](/en/chemistry/sustainable-chemistry/) treats carbon capture, green hydrogen, chemical recycling and life-cycle assessment; Lumenary ME's [sustainable engineering](/en/me/sustainable-engineering/) treats the product view, design for disassembly and the process energy of steel and cement plants. This lesson asks the materials scientist's questions: what in a material's structure and processing sets its footprint, what limits the alternatives, and how a designer should choose.

</Section>

<Section id="embodied" kind="computation" title="Embodied carbon, for a small product">

<Definition term="Embodied carbon">

The **embodied carbon** of a material is the greenhouse-gas emission, in kilograms of CO₂-equivalent, attributable to producing one kilogram of it, usually counted **cradle to gate**: from extraction to the factory gate, excluding use and end of life. Values come from life-cycle inventories, industry associations and product-specific **environmental product declarations** (EPDs) verified to standards such as ISO 14025 and EN 15804.

</Definition>

The block computes the cradle-to-gate embodied carbon of a bicycle's materials from its bill of materials, with five possible frames. The carbon intensities are rounded, representative values within published ranges, not the figures for any producer; the [ICE database](https://circularecology.com/embodied-carbon-footprint-database.html), the [worldsteel](https://worldsteel.org/) and [International Aluminium Institute](https://international-aluminium.org/) statistics and manufacturers' EPDs are where real numbers come from. Each frame is charged for the material bought, including offcuts and machining scrap.

```python run
# Cradle-to-gate embodied carbon of a bicycle from its bill of materials.
# Carbon intensities (kg CO2e per kg of material) are rounded, representative values within published
# ranges; real values depend on the producer, the electricity used and the recycled content.
CI = {
    "steel, blast furnace":     2.2,
    "steel, scrap + EAF":       0.7,
    "aluminium, primary":      16.0,
    "aluminium, recycled":      0.8,
    "titanium":                35.0,
    "carbon-fibre composite":  25.0,
    "rubber":                   3.0,
    "nylon and other plastics": 6.0,
}
# Parts common to every version: (material, kg in the finished bicycle)
common = [
    ("wheels: rims", "aluminium, primary", 1.0),
    ("wheels: spokes and hubs", "steel, blast furnace", 0.8),
    ("tyres and tubes", "rubber", 1.0),
    ("chain, cassette, chainrings", "steel, blast furnace", 1.6),
    ("cranks, brakes, derailleurs", "aluminium, primary", 0.9),
    ("bar, stem, seatpost", "aluminium, primary", 0.9),
    ("saddle, cables, grips", "nylon and other plastics", 0.6),
]
# Frame and fork options: (material, finished mass in kg, kg of material bought per kg finished)
frames = {
    "steel (CrMo tubes)":      ("steel, blast furnace", 2.6, 1.3),
    "aluminium (primary)":     ("aluminium, primary", 1.8, 1.3),
    "aluminium (recycled)":    ("aluminium, recycled", 1.8, 1.3),
    "titanium":                ("titanium", 1.9, 1.5),
    "carbon-fibre composite":  ("carbon-fibre composite", 1.3, 1.5),
}

base = sum(CI[m] * kg for _, m, kg in common)
print(f"parts common to all versions: {sum(kg for *_, kg in common):.1f} kg, {base:.0f} kg CO2e")
for part, m, kg in sorted(common, key=lambda p: -CI[p[1]] * p[2]):
    print(f"   {part:30s} {CI[m] * kg:5.1f} kg CO2e")

print(f"\n{'frame and fork':24s} {'mass':>6s} {'frame CO2e':>11s} {'bike CO2e':>10s} {'bike mass':>10s}")
for name, (m, kg, buy) in frames.items():
    frame = CI[m] * kg * buy                 # offcuts and machining scrap are made too
    print(f"{name:24s} {kg:4.1f} kg {frame:8.0f} kg {base + frame:8.0f} kg {kg + sum(k for *_, k in common):7.1f} kg")

# Perspective: car trips replaced. A mid-sized petrol car emits about 0.17 kg CO2e per km in use.
total = base + CI["aluminium, primary"] * 1.8 * 1.3
print(f"\nthe primary-aluminium bicycle is repaid after replacing about {total / 0.17:,.0f} km of car driving")
```

Three things stand out. The aluminium components (rims, cranks, bars), not the steel ones, dominate the common parts: 2.8 kg of primary aluminium carries 44.8 kg of the 57 kg CO₂e, while 2.4 kg of steel carries 5.3 kg. For the frame, a steel one at 7 kg CO₂e and a recycled-aluminium one at 2 kg are cheapest in carbon; primary aluminium (37 kg), carbon-fibre composite (49 kg) and titanium (100 kg) are lighter by a few hundred grams to a kilogram and cost five to fifty times as much carbon. And the whole bicycle, at 59 to 156 kg CO₂e for its materials, is repaid after a few hundred to a thousand kilometres of car journeys replaced: the materials matter most when products are made in enormous numbers or replaced often.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Every number in that table carries a range wider than the differences it shows. Primary aluminium runs from about 4 kg CO₂e/kg when smelted with hydropower to over 20 on a coal-heavy grid; "recycled" may mean post-consumer scrap or factory offcuts that were never waste; and cradle-to-gate leaves out welding, painting, transport, use and end of life. Compare products with EPDs for the actual supply chain, and report the range, not only the ranking.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="indices" kind="computation" title="Carbon per function: material indices">

Carbon per kilogram is the wrong figure of merit, because different materials need different masses to do the same job. Ashby's method from materials selection fixes that: for a component of given length and required performance, the mass needed scales as $\rho/\sigma$ for a strut or tie of given strength, $\rho/E^{1/2}$ for a beam of given bending stiffness whose section may grow, and $\rho/E^{1/3}$ for a panel of given stiffness. Multiplying by the carbon intensity $c$ gives the carbon needed for the job. The block ranks seven structural options relative to blast-furnace steel, with rounded, typical properties.

```python run
# Embodied carbon per unit of function, Ashby-style. For a component of fixed length and required
# performance, the mass needed scales as rho/sigma (a tie of given strength), rho/E^(1/2) (a beam of given
# bending stiffness, section size free) or rho/E^(1/3) (a panel of given stiffness). Multiply by the
# carbon intensity c (kg CO2e/kg) to rank materials by the CO2 needed for the job.
# Properties are rounded, typical values; c as in the previous block (fossil carbon only for timber).
materials = {  # E (GPa), strength (MPa), density (kg/m^3), c (kg CO2e/kg)
    "structural steel, BF-BOF":    (210, 355, 7850, 2.2),
    "structural steel, scrap EAF": (210, 355, 7850, 0.7),
    "aluminium alloy, primary":    (70, 250, 2700, 16.0),
    "aluminium alloy, recycled":   (70, 250, 2700, 0.8),
    "CFRP, quasi-isotropic":       (60, 600, 1550, 25.0),
    "glulam timber (softwood)":    (12, 25, 480, 0.4),
    "concrete C30 (compression)":  (33, 30, 2400, 0.13),
}
rows = []
for name, (E, s, rho, c) in materials.items():
    tie = c * rho / s                 # CO2 for a tie of given strength (strength-limited)
    beam = c * rho / E ** 0.5         # CO2 for a beam of given stiffness
    panel = c * rho / E ** (1 / 3)    # CO2 for a panel of given stiffness
    rows.append((name, tie, beam, panel))
ref = rows[0]
print(f"{'CO2 for the same job, relative to BF-BOF steel':44s} {'strong':>6s} {'beam':>6s} {'panel':>6s}")
for name, tie, beam, panel in rows:
    print(f"{name:44s} {tie / ref[1]:6.2f} {beam / ref[2]:6.2f} {panel / ref[3]:6.2f}")
print("\nstrong: a strut or tie of given strength; beam, panel: given bending stiffness")
print("(concrete's strength column is for compression; in tension it needs reinforcing steel)")
```

Primary aluminium needs 3.6 to 4.3 times the carbon of blast-furnace steel for the same job, and recycled aluminium only 0.18 to 0.22 times; scrap-based steel is a third of blast-furnace steel throughout. Carbon-fibre composite is competitive only where strength is what is needed (1.33), not stiffness (4.20). Timber and concrete win by a factor of twenty or more for stiff beams and panels in this crude index, which is why mass timber and better concrete design dominate discussions of low-carbon buildings. The index ignores joints, fire, durability, end of life and the carbon stored in timber (which is excluded here); it is a first screen, exactly as in <LessonLink slug="composites">Composites</LessonLink> and the selection charts of <LessonLink slug="metals-and-alloys">Metals and alloys</LessonLink>.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The deepest lever is not on the chart at all: use less. Studies of real buildings have found steel beams loaded to well under half their capacity, because standard sections and generous rounding are cheaper in design hours than material. A materials scientist can make steel with less carbon; a designer can often simply need less steel.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="steel-cement" kind="examples" title="Low-carbon steel and cement: the materials science">

Lumenary ME's [sustainable engineering](/en/me/sustainable-engineering/) computes the process emissions of blast furnaces, electric arc furnaces and cement kilns. Here are the materials questions underneath.

**Why hydrogen reduction is different.** A blast furnace reduces iron oxide with carbon monoxide made from coke; hydrogen direct reduction (H₂-DRI) uses hydrogen in a shaft furnace at about 900 °C, producing solid "sponge iron" that is then melted. The block compares the two overall reactions with standard thermodynamic data.

```python run
# Reducing hematite with carbon monoxide (blast furnace) or hydrogen (direct reduction), from standard
# enthalpies of formation (kJ/mol) and entropies (J/mol K) at 298 K, as tabulated by NIST and in the
# NBS tables. Treating dH and dS as constant with temperature is a rough but instructive approximation.
data = {            # dHf (kJ/mol), S (J/mol K)
    "Fe2O3": (-824.2, 87.4), "Fe": (0.0, 27.3),
    "CO": (-110.5, 197.7), "CO2": (-393.5, 213.8),
    "H2": (0.0, 130.7), "H2O(g)": (-241.8, 188.8),
}

def reaction(products, reactants):
    dH = sum(n * data[s][0] for s, n in products.items()) - sum(n * data[s][0] for s, n in reactants.items())
    dS = sum(n * data[s][1] for s, n in products.items()) - sum(n * data[s][1] for s, n in reactants.items())
    return dH, dS / 1000                      # kJ/mol and kJ/(mol K)

routes = {
    "Fe2O3 + 3 CO -> 2 Fe + 3 CO2": reaction({"Fe": 2, "CO2": 3}, {"Fe2O3": 1, "CO": 3}),
    "Fe2O3 + 3 H2 -> 2 Fe + 3 H2O": reaction({"Fe": 2, "H2O(g)": 3}, {"Fe2O3": 1, "H2": 3}),
}
mol_fe2o3_per_t_fe = 1e6 / 55.845 / 2
print(f"{'reaction (per mol Fe2O3)':30s} {'dH kJ':>7s} {'dG 25 C':>8s} {'dG 900 C':>9s}   heat per t Fe")
for name, (dH, dS) in routes.items():
    g25, g900 = dH - 298.15 * dS, dH - 1173.15 * dS
    q = dH * mol_fe2o3_per_t_fe / 3.6e6       # MWh per tonne of iron (+ means heat must be supplied)
    print(f"{name:30s} {dH:7.1f} {g25:8.1f} {g900:9.1f}   {q:+.3f} MWh")

h2_kg = mol_fe2o3_per_t_fe * 3 * 2.016 / 1000
co2_kg = mol_fe2o3_per_t_fe * 3 * 44.01 / 1000
print(f"\nper tonne of iron: {h2_kg:.0f} kg of H2 in the reaction (shaft furnaces circulate several times more),")
print(f"or, by the CO route, {co2_kg:,.0f} kg of CO2 from the reduction alone")
```

Reduction by CO is mildly exothermic ($\Delta H = -24.8$ kJ per mole of Fe₂O₃); reduction by hydrogen is endothermic ($+98.8$ kJ) and unfavourable at room temperature ($\Delta G = +56.6$ kJ), but its large entropy gain (water vapour is released) makes it favourable at 900 °C ($-67.2$ kJ). So a hydrogen shaft furnace must be fed hot gas carrying about 0.25 MWh of heat per tonne of iron for the reaction itself, on top of the 54 kg of hydrogen consumed, while the CO route releases 1.18 t of CO₂ per tonne of iron from the reduction alone. Above about 800 °C hydrogen is in fact the stronger reductant, and it diffuses faster through the pellet's pores, so the kinetics are good.

The materials constraints are elsewhere. A shaft furnace does not melt anything, so the ore's **gangue** (silica, alumina) stays in the sponge iron and must be removed as slag in the electric furnace; classic direct reduction needs high-grade "DR-grade" pellets with about 67 per cent iron or more, a small fraction of the world's iron ore. Routes being piloted in Australia, Europe and elsewhere add an electric smelting furnace after the shaft to handle ordinary blast-furnace-grade ore. Sponge iron made with hydrogen contains almost no carbon, unlike blast-furnace iron, which changes how the steel is refined and alloyed. And **scrap**, the lowest-carbon iron source of all, carries "tramp" elements: copper from wiring and motors cannot be removed by oxidation in steelmaking, because it is more noble than iron, and above about 0.1–0.2 per cent it concentrates at the surface under the oxide scale during hot rolling and penetrates grain boundaries as a liquid, cracking the steel (**hot shortness**). Copper can only be diluted, which is why scrap sorting and design for disassembly are metallurgical issues. As of late 2026, Sweden's HYBRIT project has made fossil-free sponge iron at pilot scale since 2021, Stegra's large plant at Boden was refinanced in 2026 after construction delays, with its start-up timetable under review, Chinese producers operate hydrogen-rich shaft furnaces, and several European projects have been postponed on cost.

**Cement chemistry.** Portland cement clinker is mostly **alite** (Ca₃SiO₅) and **belite** (Ca₂SiO₄), made by heating limestone and clay to about 1450 °C. Every CaO came from CaCO₃ and released a CO₂, so the less lime a binder contains, the less process CO₂ it carries: belite has 2 CaO per SiO₂ instead of alite's 3 (an exercise computes the difference), but it hardens slowly. The established lever is to replace clinker with **supplementary cementitious materials** that react with the calcium hydroxide that cement hydration releases, forming more of the calcium silicate hydrate binder: fly ash from coal power and blast-furnace slag from iron-making — both shrinking in supply as coal power closes and steel moves to electric furnaces — and **calcined clay**, which is abundant almost everywhere. Limestone calcined clay cement ([LC3](https://lc3.ch/)), developed by EPFL with partners in India, Cuba and elsewhere, replaces about half the clinker with calcined clay and ground limestone, which react together to form carboaluminate phases. Alkali-activated binders ("geopolymers") avoid clinker altogether but need consistent precursors and new standards. Concrete also slowly reabsorbs some CO₂ by carbonation over its life, a real but partial and slow offset, and carbonation is the same reaction that lets moisture corrode reinforcing steel.

The materials science of recyclability starts with the chain. **Mechanical recycling** — sorting, washing, melting and re-extruding — breaks some chains each time through heat, shear and hydrolysis, so the molar mass falls and, because melt viscosity scales roughly as $M_w^{3.4}$ above the entanglement mass (as in <LessonLink slug="polymers">Polymers</LessonLink>), processability and toughness fall faster still. PET can be "solid-state polycondensed" back to bottle grade, which is why PET bottles are the best-recycled plastic; mixed polyolefins and multilayer films are not, because immiscible polymers form weak, phase-separated blends. Design responses include **mono-material** packaging, compatibilisers, and polymers built to come apart: **vitrimers** (2011), cross-linked networks whose bonds exchange when heated so the material can be reshaped and repaired; and polymers with dynamic covalent bonds such as the poly(diketoenamine)s (2019), which depolymerise in acid back to monomers that can be separated from additives and repolymerised. Thermoset composites are the hardest case — wind-turbine blades are made of glass fibre in epoxy — and manufacturers began offering recyclable-resin blades in the early 2020s. Chemical recycling routes are covered in [sustainable chemistry](/en/chemistry/sustainable-chemistry/).

<Section id="critical" kind="human" title="Critical raw materials and substitution">

A raw material is **critical** when it is economically important and its supply is at risk: concentrated in a few countries, hard to substitute, little recycled. The EU's [Critical Raw Materials Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1252/oj) of 2024 lists critical and "strategic" materials and sets benchmarks for 2030: at least 10 per cent of the EU's annual consumption of strategic raw materials extracted in the EU, 40 per cent processed and 25 per cent recycled there, and no more than 65 per cent of any one strategic material, at any stage of processing, from a single third country. The United States, Japan, Korea, India, Australia and others keep their own lists. Export controls became a policy instrument: China placed licensing on gallium and germanium (2023), graphite (2023), antimony (2024) and several medium and heavy rare earths and magnets (April 2025), and announced broader rules in October 2025, parts of which were later suspended. These measures change quickly; check current sources.

Materials scientists respond in three ways. **Thrifting** uses less of the critical element for the same function: grain-boundary diffusion puts dysprosium only at the grain boundaries of neodymium–iron–boron magnets, where it resists demagnetisation, cutting its use severalfold, as <LessonLink slug="magnetic-materials">Magnetic materials</LessonLink> explains. **Substitution at the material level** replaces it: cobalt-free LFP and manganese-rich battery cathodes (<LessonLink slug="battery-materials">Battery materials</LessonLink>), cerium partly replacing neodymium, doped zinc oxide or tin oxide for indium tin oxide in some transparent electrodes. **Substitution at the system level** changes the design: electric motors with wound rotors or induction machines need no permanent magnets at all, at some cost in efficiency or size. Each substitution trades one property for another, and the computational screening of <LessonLink slug="ai-materials-discovery">AI-assisted materials discovery</LessonLink> is routinely constrained to avoid critical elements.

</Section>

<Section id="circular" kind="computation" title="Circular design, and the limit growth sets">

A circular economy keeps materials in use at their highest value for as long as possible: maintain, repair and reuse first, then remanufacture, and recycle only what is left, with as little leaving the loop as possible. The inner loops preserve the work done in making the product; recycling preserves only the material, and often degrades it.

Even perfect recycling faces arithmetic. Material that returns as scrap today went into products years ago, when demand was smaller. If a product lasts $L$ years and demand grows at a rate $g$ per year, the scrap returning is only $(1+g)^{-L}$ of today's demand, before any losses in collection and processing. The block computes the resulting ceiling on recycled content with rounded, illustrative parameters.

```python run
# How much of today's demand can end-of-life scrap supply? Material put into use L years ago returns now;
# if demand grows at a rate g per year, today's demand is (1 + g)^L times larger than what is returning.
# The best possible recycled share is collection x process yield x (1 + g)^-L.
# All parameters are rounded and illustrative, chosen to show the effect, not to forecast.
cases = {  # lifetime L (years), demand growth g, collection rate, process yield
    "steel in buildings":         (50, 0.015, 0.85, 0.90),
    "steel in cars":              (15, 0.015, 0.90, 0.90),
    "aluminium, average product": (20, 0.030, 0.70, 0.90),
    "copper in wiring":           (30, 0.025, 0.60, 0.95),
    "lithium in EV batteries":    (12, 0.150, 0.60, 0.85),
    "PET drinks bottles":         (0.5, 0.030, 0.50, 0.75),
}
print(f"{'material and use':28s} {'L':>4s} {'growth':>7s} {'returned/demand':>16s} {'max recycled share':>19s}")
for name, (L, g, coll, yld) in cases.items():
    ratio = (1 + g) ** -L
    print(f"{name:28s} {L:4g} {100 * g:6.1f}% {ratio:13.2f}    {100 * coll * yld * ratio:13.0f} %")

print("\nlithium: maximum recycled share if growth slows (L = 12 years, 60 % collected, 85 % yield)")
for g in (0.30, 0.20, 0.10, 0.05, 0.02, 0.0):
    print(f"  growth {100 * g:4.0f} % a year -> {100 * 0.60 * 0.85 * (1 + g) ** -12:5.1f} %")
```

Steel in long-lived buildings can supply at most 36 per cent of today's demand from end-of-life scrap in this model, even with 85 per cent collection — close to the share of scrap in world steelmaking today — while steel from short-lived cars could supply 65 per cent. Lithium in electric-vehicle batteries, with demand growing at 15 per cent a year, could supply only 10 per cent; at 30 per cent growth just 2.2 per cent, rising to 51 per cent only when growth stops. PET bottles return within months, so their ceiling (37 per cent) is set almost entirely by collection and yield. **Recycling cannot supply a growing stock**; for decades, growing economies and new technologies need primary material, and the case for recycling is about the long run, about energy (recycled aluminium needs about a twentieth of the energy of primary), and about keeping quality high enough that the loop is worth closing.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Quality is the second limit. In the aluminium industry, cast alloys can absorb mixed scrap but wrought alloys for sheet and extrusions need tightly controlled compositions, so mixed scrap tends to be "downcycled" into castings; as cars use fewer cast engine blocks, that outlet shrinks. Sensor-based sorting, alloy-specific collection and alloys designed to tolerate more impurities are active research areas for exactly this reason.

</Narrator>

<Section id="policy" kind="human" title="Policy, as of 2026">

Policy now shapes materials choices as much as price does. What follows was in force or agreed by late 2026; much of it is being phased in, and details change.

- **Carbon pricing at the border.** The EU's [Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism_en) entered its definitive period on 1 January 2026, covering imports of iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity: importers above a 50-tonne annual threshold must declare embedded emissions and, with certificate sales from February 2027, pay for them at the EU carbon price, as free allocation to EU producers under the Emissions Trading System is phased out to 2034.
- **Emissions trading beyond Europe.** China's national carbon market was extended in 2025 to steel, cement and aluminium smelting, with intensity-based allocation; India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme notified emission-intensity targets for aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali and pulp and paper in October 2025, with more sectors added in 2026; Japan's GX-ETS became mandatory for large emitters from April 2026.
- **Product rules.** The EU's [Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1781/oj) (2024) lets the Commission set durability, recycled-content and carbon requirements and digital product passports; its first working plan (2025) put steel and aluminium among the first product groups, alongside textiles, furniture, tyres and mattresses. The 2023 Battery Regulation sets recycled-content and recovery targets, and the 2025 Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation sets minimum recycled content for plastic packaging from 2030.
- **Procurement.** Public buyers are using embodied-carbon limits: "Buy Clean" rules in some US states, such as California, cap the global-warming potential of structural steel and other products in state projects, and several European countries require whole-life carbon assessments for buildings. United States federal support for low-carbon industry changed course in 2025, with several programmes cut or cancelled.
- **Plastics.** Negotiations on a global plastics treaty under UNEP failed to agree a text at Busan (2024) and Geneva (August 2025); a new chair was elected in February 2026, and as of mid-2026 talks remained deadlocked over whether to limit production. Check [UNEP's page](https://www.unep.org/inc-plastic-pollution) for the current state.

<Misconception>

"Policy will make low-carbon materials cheap." Most of these instruments make high-carbon materials more expensive, or require disclosure; they do not by themselves make hydrogen, clean electricity or new cements cheap, and they create real risks of shifting production elsewhere, which border adjustments try to address. Their effect on materials will depend on prices and enforcement over the next decade.

</Misconception>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Lighter is greener."** Only if the energy saved in use outweighs the extra carbon of making the lighter material, which depends on how much the product moves and on the material's route: in the bicycle, a titanium frame saved 0.7 kg and cost about 92 kg CO₂e more than steel. For vehicles, see the break-even calculation in Lumenary ME's [sustainable engineering](/en/me/sustainable-engineering/).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Bio-based plastics are biodegradable, and biodegradable plastics solve litter."** Bio-based PE is chemically identical to fossil PE. Many "compostable" plastics such as PLA break down only at the high temperatures of industrial composting, not in soil or the sea, and they contaminate conventional recycling streams.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Everything should be made from recycled material."** For growing demand the scrap simply is not there (the recycling ceiling), and demanding recycled content for one product can just divert scrap from another without reducing total emissions. Recycling's benefit is real when it increases the total amount of material recovered and keeps its quality.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Hydrogen steel is carbon-free."** Its emissions depend on how the hydrogen and the electricity for the furnace are made; on a fossil-heavy grid, electrolytic-hydrogen steel can emit nearly as much as a blast furnace. The materials route is necessary but not sufficient.

</Misconception>
