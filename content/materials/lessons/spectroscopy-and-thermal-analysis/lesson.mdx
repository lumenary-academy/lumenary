<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What is it made of, and what does it do when heated?">

A supplier changes nothing, it says, yet a new batch of polymer clips cracks during assembly while the old batch did not. An aluminium panel that bonded well for years suddenly lets its adhesive peel. A nickel alloy forging fails an inspection for a small, hard particle sitting at the origin of a crack. In each case microscopy, from <LessonLink slug="microscopy">the last lesson</LessonLink>, shows *where* the problem is. The next questions are chemical and thermal: which elements are in that particle, what is the chemical state of the aluminium's surface, how crystalline is that polymer, what happens to it on heating?

This lesson covers the instruments that answer them. Three spectroscopies tell what a solid is made of: **energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy** (EDS) gives elements in a micrometre-sized spot under the electron microscope; **X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy** (XPS) gives elements *and their chemical state* in the top few nanometres; **Raman spectroscopy** gives the vibrations of bonds, which fingerprint phases and stresses. Three thermal methods tell how it behaves: **differential scanning calorimetry** (DSC) measures heat absorbed or released, **thermogravimetric analysis** (TGA) measures mass lost or gained, and **dilatometry** measures length. Lumenary Chemistry's [spectroscopy](/en/chemistry/spectroscopy/) lesson covers the molecular methods (UV–visible, infrared, NMR, mass spectrometry) and [analytical chemistry](/en/chemistry/analytical-chemistry/) covers calibration, uncertainty and trace analysis; here the subject is solids.

</Section>

<Section id="eds" kind="computation" title="EDS: elements under the electron beam">

When a beam electron knocks an inner-shell electron out of an atom, an electron from a higher shell drops into the hole and the energy difference leaves as an X-ray photon. Because inner-shell energies depend almost entirely on the nuclear charge, each element emits X-rays at its own **characteristic energies**: the K$\alpha$ line (L shell to K shell), K$\beta$, L$\alpha$ and so on. In 1913 Henry Moseley, at Manchester, measured them for a series of elements and found that the square root of the frequency rises in a straight line with atomic number, which settled the order of the periodic table and showed where elements were missing. A simple model, a K-shell hole screened by the one remaining K electron, gives **Moseley's law** for the K$\alpha$ energy, $E \approx \tfrac34 \times 13.6\ \text{eV} \times (Z-1)^2$.

An **energy-dispersive** detector, today a silicon drift detector on the side of an SEM or TEM, converts each arriving photon into a pulse of charge proportional to its energy and sorts the pulses into a spectrum. It records all elements from boron or carbon upwards at once, but its energy resolution is limited, about 125 to 130 eV at 5.9 keV. A **wavelength-dispersive** spectrometer (WDS) instead diffracts the X-rays from a crystal by Bragg's law and counts one wavelength at a time: slower, but with a resolution of a few eV. The block checks Moseley's law against tabulated energies and shows what resolution means for a famous overlap.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

# 1. Moseley's law: K-alpha photon energy ~ (3/4) x 13.606 eV x (Z - 1)^2,
#    compared with tabulated K-alpha1 energies (X-ray Data Booklet), in keV.
tabulated = {"Al": (13, 1.487), "Si": (14, 1.740), "S": (16, 2.308), "Ti": (22, 4.511),
             "Fe": (26, 6.404), "Ni": (28, 7.478), "Cu": (29, 8.048), "Mo": (42, 17.479)}
print("element   Z   Moseley (keV)   tabulated (keV)   difference")
for el, (Z, E) in tabulated.items():
    E_m = 0.75 * 13.606 * (Z - 1) ** 2 / 1000
    print(f"{el:>7} {Z:3d}   {E_m:13.3f}   {E:15.3f}   {100 * (E_m - E) / E:+8.1f} %")

# 2. Sulfur K-alpha (2308 eV) next to molybdenum L-alpha (2293 eV), seen by an EDS detector
#    and by a wavelength-dispersive (WDS) spectrometer.  The EDS peak width grows with energy:
#    FWHM^2 = FWHM(Mn K-alpha)^2 + 2.5 (E - 5895 eV), all in eV, for a detector rated at 130 eV.
def gaussian_lines(E, fwhm, centres=(2293.0, 2308.0)):
    s = fwhm / 2.3548
    return sum(np.exp(-0.5 * ((E - c) / s) ** 2) for c in centres)

w_eds = math.sqrt(130.0**2 + 2.5 * (2300 - 5895))
w_wds = 8.0
fine = np.arange(2200.0, 2400.0, 0.25)
for name, w in [("EDS", w_eds), ("WDS", w_wds)]:
    y = gaussian_lines(fine, w)
    maxima = int(np.sum((y[1:-1] > y[:-2]) & (y[1:-1] > y[2:])))
    print(f"\n{name}: peak width {w:.0f} eV; maxima in the summed spectrum: {maxima}")

print("\nenergy (eV)   EDS                    WDS")
for e in np.arange(2272.0, 2330.0, 4.0):
    a = gaussian_lines(e, w_eds) / gaussian_lines(fine, w_eds).max()
    b = gaussian_lines(e, w_wds) / gaussian_lines(fine, w_wds).max()
    print(f"{e:11.0f}   {'#' * round(20 * a):20s}   {'#' * round(20 * b)}")
```

Moseley's crude model is within 2 per cent for every element in the table, and within half a per cent from sulfur to nickel, good enough to identify a peak on sight. The second part is the problem every microanalyst meets. Sulfur's K$\alpha$ and molybdenum's L$\alpha$ lines are only 15 eV apart, while the EDS peak at that energy is 89 eV wide; the two merge into a single peak, and a sulfide inclusion in a molybdenum-bearing steel can be misread. Lead's M$\alpha$ line, at 2.35 keV, joins the same crowd. With 8 eV peaks, WDS shows two clean maxima. Software deconvolves overlaps by fitting reference peak shapes, but only if the analyst has thought to include the right elements.

Turning peak heights into concentrations needs **ZAF** corrections for how the matrix affects the X-rays: atomic number (how far electrons penetrate and how many backscatter), absorption on the way out, and fluorescence excited by other X-rays. With standards and a flat polished specimen, an electron probe microanalyser with WDS reaches about 1 per cent relative accuracy for major elements; "standardless" EDS on a rough surface is often off by 10 per cent relative or more.

<Narrator who="gideon">

EDS cannot see hydrogen or helium at all, and sees lithium only with special detectors, so it is blind to hydrogen in steel and nearly blind to lithium in a battery cathode. And remember the interaction volume from the microscopy lesson: at 20 kV the X-rays from a 1 µm inclusion in aluminium come partly from the aluminium around and beneath it, so its "composition" is a mixture. Lower the voltage, or thin the specimen for the TEM, before trusting the analysis of anything small.

</Narrator>

<Section id="xps" kind="computation" title="XPS: the top few nanometres, and chemical states">

In **X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy**, monochromatic X-rays, usually aluminium K$\alpha$ at 1486.6 eV, eject electrons from the atoms of the sample, and an analyser measures their kinetic energy. Energy conservation gives the **binding energy** of the level each came from:

$$E_\text{B} = h\nu - E_\text{K} - \phi,$$

where $\phi$ is the work function of the spectrometer. Core-level binding energies identify the element, as X-ray energies do in EDS. The decisive difference is that they also shift by a few electronvolts with the atom's **chemical state**. Kai Siegbahn's group in Uppsala developed the method in the 1950s and 1960s, called it ESCA (electron spectroscopy for chemical analysis), and Siegbahn shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why should a core electron, deep inside the atom, care about chemical bonding? Because the valence electrons form a shell of charge around it. When silicon gives up electron density to oxygen, that shell thins, the nucleus is less screened, and every core level is held about 1 eV more tightly per oxygen neighbour. Silicon in SiO₂, with four oxygen neighbours, sits about 4 eV above elemental silicon.

</Narrator>

Electrons travelling through a solid lose energy in collisions; only those that escape *without* any loss contribute to the sharp peak. The chance of escaping from depth $z$ falls as $e^{-z/\lambda}$, where the inelastic mean free path $\lambda$ is a few nanometres for electrons of about 1 keV. That is what makes XPS a surface technique, and it also lets you measure the thickness of very thin films. The block works through three calculations for a silicon wafer with an oxide on it.

```python run
import math

# 1. XPS: binding energy from the measured kinetic energy (Al K-alpha source).
h_nu, phi = 1486.6, 4.5                       # photon energy and spectrometer work function, eV
for label, KE in [("peak A", 1382.7), ("peak B", 1378.6)]:
    print(f"{label}: kinetic energy {KE} eV -> binding energy {h_nu - KE - phi:.1f} eV")

# 2. Where does the signal come from?  Electrons from depth z escape without losing energy
#    with probability exp(-z / (lambda cos theta)), theta measured from the surface normal.
lam = 3.0                                     # nm, a typical inelastic mean free path here
print("\nfraction of the signal from the top ...    1 nm    3 nm    6 nm    9 nm")
for theta in (0, 60, 75):
    L = lam * math.cos(math.radians(theta))
    fr = [1 - math.exp(-t / L) for t in (1, 3, 6, 9)]
    print(f"take-off {theta:2d} deg from the normal:      " + "  ".join(f"{f:6.0%}" for f in fr))

# 3. Oxide thickness from how much it dims the silicon signal underneath.
ratio = 0.38                                  # Si(0) 2p intensity: oxidised / freshly cleaned wafer
print(f"\nsubstrate signal at {ratio:.0%} of the clean value -> oxide {-lam * math.log(ratio):.2f} nm thick")
```

The two Si 2p peaks have binding energies of 99.4 and 103.5 eV, matching reference values for elemental silicon and silicon dioxide in the NIST database: the wafer and its oxide, told apart in one spectrum. Looking straight down, 63 per cent of the signal comes from the top 3 nm and 95 per cent from the top 9 nm, three mean free paths. Tilting the sample so the analyser looks at 75° from the normal squeezes 72 per cent of the signal into the top nanometre: **angle-resolved XPS** uses this to separate a surface layer from what lies under it. If the substrate signal falls to 38 per cent of a clean wafer's, the oxide is 2.9 nm thick.

In practice XPS needs ultra-high vacuum, detects every element except hydrogen and helium down to about 0.1 to 1 atomic per cent, and analyses a spot typically tens to hundreds of micrometres across. Depth profiles are made by sputtering with argon ions, or with clusters of thousands of argon atoms for polymers; insulators charge up and are corrected with a low-energy electron flood and a reference peak. The peeling adhesive in the motivation is a classic XPS problem: a few nanometres of silicone release agent are invisible to EDS and obvious to XPS.

</Section>

<Section id="raman" kind="examples" title="Raman spectroscopy: listening to the lattice">

Shine a laser on a solid and almost all the scattered light keeps its colour. A tiny fraction, around one scattered photon in a million or fewer, comes out with a little less energy, having left it behind as a vibration of the lattice or a molecule (**Stokes** scattering), or with a little more, having picked up a vibration's energy (**anti-Stokes**). The shift, quoted in wavenumbers, cm⁻¹, is the vibration's frequency and does not depend on the laser. C. V. Raman and K. S. Krishnan reported the effect from Calcutta in 1928, and Grigory Landsberg and Leonid Mandelstam found it in quartz in Moscow the same year; Raman received the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physics.

A vibration appears in the Raman spectrum if it changes the material's polarisability, a different rule from infrared absorption, which needs a changing dipole (see Lumenary Chemistry's [spectroscopy](/en/chemistry/spectroscopy/) lesson), so the two are complementary. For solids, Raman spectra are fingerprints:

- **Carbon.** Diamond has a single sharp line at 1332 cm⁻¹. Graphite has its G band near 1580 cm⁻¹; disorder and crystallite edges activate a D band near 1350 cm⁻¹, and the D/G ratio measures how disordered a carbon is. The shape of the 2D band near 2700 cm⁻¹ counts the layers in graphene.
- **Polymorphs.** Anatase and rutile, two forms of TiO₂ with the same composition, give entirely different spectra (anatase's strongest line is near 144 cm⁻¹), so Raman distinguishes them where EDS cannot.
- **Stress.** Compressing a crystal stiffens its bonds and raises the frequency of its lines. Silicon's line near 520 cm⁻¹ shifts by a few cm⁻¹ per gigapascal, which is how the strain built into transistor channels is mapped.

Metals have no useful Raman spectrum, because their free electrons screen the light, but their oxides, corrosion products and coatings do.

<Example title="Where the lines are, and the laser as a heater">

With a 532 nm green laser, whose wavenumber is $10^7/532 = 18\,797$ cm⁻¹, silicon's 520 cm⁻¹ Stokes line appears at $10^7/(18\,797 - 520) = 547.1$ nm and the anti-Stokes line at $10^7/(18\,797 + 520) = 517.7$ nm. Anti-Stokes scattering needs a vibration that is already excited, so its strength relative to the Stokes line follows the Boltzmann factor $e^{-hc\tilde\nu/k_\text{B}T}$, multiplied by a factor for the different frequencies of the two lines. At 295 K the ratio works out at 0.099; at 400 K, 0.192; at 600 K, 0.359. A measured ratio of 0.120 under a tightly focused laser therefore means the spot is at about 320 K: the laser is heating the sample by 25 K. That matters, because heat shifts Raman lines too and can transform delicate phases under the beam.

</Example>

Raman microscopes focus the laser to a spot about a micrometre across and work in air, through windows and in liquids, ideal for watching corrosion or battery electrodes in operation. The lasers can harm eyes, so instruments are enclosed and interlocked.

</Section>

<Section id="dsc" kind="computation" title="Differential scanning calorimetry">

A **differential scanning calorimeter** heats a few milligrams of sample in a small pan, alongside an empty reference pan, at a steady rate, typically 10 K/min. A *heat-flux* DSC measures the small temperature difference between the two pans and converts it into a heat flow; a *power-compensated* DSC heats the two pans with separate heaters and measures the difference in power needed to keep them at the same temperature. Either way the output is the extra **heat flow** into the sample, in milliwatts, against temperature. Away from any transition it is the sample's heat capacity times the heating rate. Transitions show as features:

- a **glass transition** as a step, because the heat capacity rises when an amorphous polymer or glass becomes able to rearrange (<LessonLink slug="polymers">polymers</LessonLink>, <LessonLink slug="glasses-and-nanomaterials">glasses and nanomaterials</LessonLink>);
- **crystallisation**, a release of heat, as an exothermic peak;
- **melting**, which absorbs heat, as an endothermic peak whose area is the enthalpy of fusion.

The block simulates the curve of a PET-like polyester that was cooled quickly from the melt, so that it is mostly amorphous, then analyses it as a thermal analyst would: the midpoint of the glass-transition step, and the peak areas above straight baselines. The **crystallinity** is the enthalpy of melting, less any that came from crystals formed during the scan itself, divided by the enthalpy of fusion of a fully crystalline polymer; about 140 J/g is the value commonly used for PET.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# A simulated DSC run on 8.0 mg of a PET-like polymer that was quenched from the melt,
# heated at 10 K/min.  Heat flow in mW, endothermic up.
mass_g, beta = 8.0e-3, 10 / 60                     # g, K/s
T = np.arange(30.0, 290.01, 0.1)                   # deg C

def gauss(T, centre, width):                       # area 1 when integrated over T
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((T - centre) / width) ** 2) / (width * math.sqrt(2 * math.pi))

cp = 1.15 + 0.0015 * (T - 30) + 0.30 / (1 + np.exp(-(T - 78) / 1.8))   # J/(g K): step at Tg
true_cc, true_m = -27.0, 39.0                      # J/g: cold crystallisation (exo), melting (endo)
dH = true_cc * gauss(T, 133, 5.0) + true_m * gauss(T, 251, 4.5)       # J/(g K)
heat_flow = 1e3 * mass_g * beta * (cp + dH) + rng.normal(0, 0.004, T.size)   # mW

# --- Analysis, as a thermal analyst would do it ---
def line_fit(lo, hi):
    sel = (T >= lo) & (T <= hi)
    return np.polyfit(T[sel], heat_flow[sel], 1)

# Glass transition: the midpoint, where the curve is halfway between the baselines before and after.
before, after = line_fit(50, 68), line_fit(90, 110)
window = (T > 68) & (T < 90)
half = (np.polyval(before, T) + np.polyval(after, T)) / 2
Tg = T[window][np.argmin(np.abs(heat_flow[window] - half[window]))]
dcp = (np.polyval(after, Tg) - np.polyval(before, Tg)) / (1e3 * mass_g * beta)
print(f"glass transition (midpoint): {Tg:.1f} C, step in heat capacity {dcp:.2f} J/(g K)")

# Peaks: integrate above a straight baseline joining the curve on either side of the peak.
def peak_enthalpy(lo, hi):
    sel = (T >= lo) & (T <= hi)
    Ts, q = T[sel], heat_flow[sel]
    base = q[0] + (q[-1] - q[0]) * (Ts - Ts[0]) / (Ts[-1] - Ts[0])
    excess = q - base
    area_mJ = np.sum((excess[1:] + excess[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(Ts)) / beta   # mW s = mJ
    peak_T = Ts[np.argmax(np.abs(excess))]
    return area_mJ / (mass_g * 1e3), peak_T                                 # J/g

H_cc, T_cc = peak_enthalpy(112, 155)
H_m, T_m = peak_enthalpy(228, 272)
print(f"cold crystallisation: peak {T_cc:.1f} C, {H_cc:+.1f} J/g (exothermic)")
print(f"melting:              peak {T_m:.1f} C, {H_m:+.1f} J/g (endothermic)")

H100 = 140.0                                       # J/g, a commonly used value for 100 % crystalline PET
print(f"\ncrystallinity as received: ({H_m:.1f} - {abs(H_cc):.1f}) / {H100:.0f} = {(H_m - abs(H_cc)) / H100:.0%}")
print(f"crystallinity just before melting: {H_m / H100:.0%}")
print(f"(the simulation was built with {(true_m + true_cc) / H100:.0%} and {true_m / H100:.0%})")
```

The analysis recovers what was put in: a glass transition at 78.0 °C with a heat-capacity step of 0.30 J/(g K), a cold-crystallisation peak at 133 °C releasing 26.9 J/g, and melting at 251 °C absorbing 39.1 J/g. The trap is in the crystallinity. The melting peak alone suggests 28 per cent, but most of those crystals *grew during the scan*, in the cold-crystallisation peak, when the warmed glass gained enough mobility to order. The material as received was only 9 per cent crystalline. That is the kind of difference that separates a clip that flexes from one that cracks.

<Narrator who="margaret">

A DSC's temperature and heat-flow scales are calibrated, not assumed: the standard check is a few milligrams of pure indium, which must melt at 156.6 °C and absorb 28.6 J/g, run at the same heating rate and in the same kind of pan as the samples. Laboratories repeat it regularly and also run zinc or tin to check other temperatures, because thermocouples drift and sensors age.

</Narrator>

DSC is just as useful for metals and composites: it finds an alloy's solidus and liquidus, the precipitation reactions of an age-hardening aluminium alloy (<LessonLink slug="phase-transformations">phase transformations</LessonLink>) and the cure of an epoxy matrix. Chip-based fast calorimeters heat and cool at thousands of kelvin per second, fast enough to mimic injection moulding or additive manufacturing.

<Section id="tga" kind="computation" title="Thermogravimetric analysis">

A **thermogravimetric analyser** hangs or rests a sample on a microbalance, resolving fractions of a microgram, inside a furnace, and records its mass while heating it in a chosen gas. Losses mean something left: moisture, solvent, a decomposing polymer, carbon dioxide from a carbonate. Gains mean something arrived, usually oxygen. The derivative of the mass curve, the **DTG** curve, turns each step into a peak at the temperature where the loss is fastest. A gas analyser (a mass spectrometer or an infrared spectrometer) on the exhaust identifies what left.

Calcium oxalate monohydrate is the classic test material because it decomposes in three clean steps: it loses its water, then carbon monoxide, then carbon dioxide, leaving calcium oxide. The block computes the step sizes from stoichiometry, simulates a TGA curve with illustrative first-order kinetics that place the steps near where they are usually observed at 10 K/min, and then demonstrates **Kissinger's method**: the DTG peak moves to higher temperature at faster heating rates, and plotting $\ln(\beta/T_\text{p}^2)$ against $1/T_\text{p}$ gives a straight line of slope $-E/R$, from which the activation energy $E$ follows.

```python run
import math
import numpy as np

R = 8.314                                         # J/(mol K)

# 1. Stoichiometry: calcium oxalate monohydrate loses H2O, then CO, then CO2, leaving CaO.
M = {"Ca": 40.078, "C": 12.011, "O": 15.999, "H": 1.008}
M_start = M["Ca"] + 2 * M["C"] + 5 * M["O"] + 2 * M["H"]          # CaC2O4.H2O
gases = [("H2O", 2 * M["H"] + M["O"]), ("CO", M["C"] + M["O"]), ("CO2", M["C"] + 2 * M["O"])]
left = 100.0
print(f"CaC2O4.H2O, M = {M_start:.2f} g/mol")
for gas, m in gases:
    left -= 100 * m / M_start
    print(f"  loses {gas:4s} = {100 * m / M_start:5.2f} % of the starting mass -> {left:6.2f} % left")

# 2. A simulated TGA run.  Each step is first order with an Arrhenius rate constant
#    k = A exp(-E/RT); at a constant heating rate beta its conversion is
#    alpha(T) = 1 - exp(-(A / beta) * integral of exp(-E/RT) dT).
#    Illustrative activation energies; each A is chosen so that at 10 K/min the step is
#    fastest at the temperature given (the peak condition E beta / (R Tp^2) = A exp(-E/(R Tp))).
plan = [(90e3, 180.0), (180e3, 495.0), (200e3, 745.0)]          # (E in J/mol, peak in deg C)
steps = []
for (gas, m), (E, Tp_C) in zip(gases, plan):
    Tp = Tp_C + 273.15
    steps.append((m / M_start, E, E * (10 / 60) / (R * Tp**2) * math.exp(E / (R * Tp))))

T = np.arange(25.0, 950.0, 0.05) + 273.15         # K

def tga(beta):
    """Mass (%) and DTG (-d mass / dT, % per K) at heating rate beta in K/s."""
    mass = np.full_like(T, 100.0)
    for fraction, E, A in steps:
        k = np.exp(-E / (R * T))
        integral = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum((k[1:] + k[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(T))))
        mass -= 100 * fraction * (1 - np.exp(-A / beta * integral))
    return mass, -np.gradient(mass, T)

mass, dtg = tga(10 / 60)
print("\nat 10 K/min:  T (C)   mass (%)")
for t in range(100, 901, 100):
    print(f"             {t:5d}   {mass[np.argmin(np.abs(T - 273.15 - t))]:7.2f}")

# 3. Kissinger: the DTG peak of the CO2 step shifts with heating rate, and
#    ln(beta / Tp^2) against 1/Tp is a straight line of slope -E/R.
print("\nbeta (K/min)   CO2 step fastest at (C)")
x, y = [], []
for b in (2, 5, 10, 20):
    m, d = tga(b / 60)
    sel = T > 873.15                               # look only at the third step
    Tp = T[sel][np.argmax(d[sel])]
    x.append(1 / Tp)
    y.append(math.log((b / 60) / Tp**2))
    print(f"{b:12d}   {Tp - 273.15:22.1f}")
slope = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)[0]
print(f"Kissinger activation energy: {-slope * R / 1e3:.1f} kJ/mol (the simulation used 200.0)")
```

The steps land where stoichiometry says they must: 12.33, 19.17 and 30.12 per cent of the starting mass, leaving 38.38 per cent as calcium oxide. That is the point of a test material, and the same reasoning reads real samples. A glass-fibre-reinforced nylon heated in air burns off its polymer and leaves the glass: a residue of 30 per cent means 30 per cent glass by mass. A carbonate that loses 44 per cent of its mass was pure calcium carbonate. A metal that *gains* mass in air is oxidising, and the rate of gain at a fixed temperature measures its oxidation resistance. In the kinetics part, the CO₂ step's fastest point moves from 685 °C at 2 K/min to 773 °C at 20 K/min, and Kissinger's plot recovers exactly the 200 kJ/mol the simulation was built with.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Kissinger's method recovered 200.0 kJ/mol because the simulation obeyed its assumptions exactly: one step, first order, a single activation energy. Real decompositions often run several overlapping reactions whose effective activation energy changes as they proceed, and fitting one number then hides the change. The ICTAC kinetics recommendations in the references ask for runs at several heating rates analysed by isoconversional methods, which estimate $E$ separately at each degree of conversion, before any single value is believed.

</Narrator>

<Section id="dilatometry" kind="examples" title="Dilatometry: measuring length">

A **dilatometer** holds a specimen, typically a rod or cylinder 10 to 25 mm long, in a furnace and measures its length with a push rod and a displacement sensor, resolving a few nanometres. It measures the **coefficient of thermal expansion**, $\alpha = (1/L_0)\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}T$, which matters wherever materials are joined: a chip on a circuit board, a ceramic coating on a turbine blade, a glass sealed to a metal. Typical values near room temperature span two orders of magnitude, from about 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for fused silica and about 1 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for the iron–nickel alloy Invar to about 12 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for steel and 23 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for aluminium.

Phase transformations show up as changes in slope or sudden steps, because different crystal structures pack atoms at different densities.

<Example title="Seeing austenite form in a steel">

Near 912 °C, X-ray diffraction gives lattice parameters of about 0.2904 nm for body-centred cubic iron (2 atoms per cell) and 0.3647 nm for face-centred cubic iron (4 atoms per cell). The volume per atom is $0.2904^3/2 = 0.012245$ nm³ in the BCC form and $0.3647^3/4 = 0.012127$ nm³ in the FCC form: the close-packed structure is 0.97 per cent denser, so each unit length shrinks by about 0.32 per cent when the iron transforms.

A 10.000 mm steel specimen heated from 20 to 700 °C, with a mean expansion coefficient of 14 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, grows by $10.000 \times 14 \times 10^{-6} \times 680 = 0.095$ mm. When it then transforms to austenite it *shrinks* by about 0.032 mm while still being heated, a feature impossible to miss on an instrument that resolves nanometres. On cooling, the reverse transformations expand the specimen, and the temperatures at which the expansion starts and finishes mark them.

</Example>

**Quenching dilatometers** heat small specimens by induction and cool them with gas jets at controlled rates; a series of such runs, with micrographs and hardness tests, is how the continuous-cooling-transformation diagrams of <LessonLink slug="phase-transformations">phase transformations</LessonLink> are measured, with the martensite start $M_\text{s}$ showing as a sharp expansion. Dilatometers also follow the shrinkage of sintering ceramics.

</Section>

<Section id="choosing" kind="human" title="Choosing a technique for a question">

The skill that matters most is matching the instrument to the question. Start from what you need to know, not from the instrument you have.

| Question | First choice | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which elements are in a 5 µm inclusion? | SEM with EDS | elements at a micrometre scale, quickly |
| Is there a trace of sulfur next to molybdenum, or boron in a steel? | WDS in an electron probe | resolves overlaps; better detection limits and light elements |
| What is the oxidation state of chromium in a 3 nm passive film? | XPS, with depth profiling or angle resolution | chemical state in the top nanometres |
| Which phases are present, and how much of each? | X-ray diffraction | crystal structures, quantitative by Rietveld refinement |
| Is this carbon coating diamond-like or graphitic? Is this TiO₂ anatase or rutile? | Raman | bonding and polymorphs, at micrometre scale |
| How crystalline is this polymer? What is its glass transition? | DSC | heat of fusion and heat-capacity step |
| How much filler, moisture or residual solvent is in it? | TGA, ideally with evolved-gas analysis | mass lost at each temperature |
| At what temperature does this steel transform at 5 K/s? | quenching dilatometry | length changes at controlled rates |
| How much hydrogen is in this weld? | thermal desorption or hot extraction | EDS and XPS cannot see hydrogen |
| Parts per million of lead in a solder? | ICP-MS on a dissolved sample | trace analysis, as in Lumenary Chemistry's [analytical chemistry](/en/chemistry/analytical-chemistry/) |

The cases in the motivation resolve this way. The cracking clips: DSC shows the new batch was moulded into a colder tool and is less crystalline, and TGA shows the filler content is unchanged. The peeling adhesive: XPS finds a few nanometres of silicone on the aluminium, transferred from a new release spray, which EDS could never have seen. The hard particle in the nickel forging: SEM backscattered imaging finds it, EDS shows titanium and nitrogen, and WDS confirms the nitrogen, which EDS measures poorly: a titanium nitride inclusion from the melt.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="Practice and standards">

Results from these methods are only comparable when they are done the same way, so most have international standards: the ISO 11357 series for DSC of plastics and ISO 11358 for their thermogravimetry, for example, set specimen sizes, heating rates and how to construct baselines. Accredited laboratories calibrate against certified reference materials and take part in interlaboratory comparisons, as Lumenary Chemistry's [analytical chemistry](/en/chemistry/analytical-chemistry/) describes.

The research frontier pushes three ways. **Operando** measurements follow materials while they work: Raman and X-ray spectroscopy of battery electrodes during charging, near-ambient-pressure XPS of catalysts in gas, calorimetry of cells as they age. **Synchrotrons** add X-ray absorption spectroscopy, which gives oxidation states and local structure for any element in any phase, crystalline or not, and hard-X-ray photoemission, which sees tens of nanometres deep. And **atom probe tomography** field-evaporates a needle-shaped specimen atom by atom and reconstructs the positions and identities of tens of millions of atoms in three dimensions, the most direct view yet of clusters and segregation in alloys. Reference data are shared openly: the NIST XPS database, the X-ray Data Booklet and the RRUFF Raman database are in the references.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"EDS gives the surface composition."** EDS in the SEM samples a volume about a micrometre deep, thousands of atomic layers. For the surface, the top few nanometres, use XPS.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"No peak means the element is absent."** It means the element is below the detection limit, or is hydrogen or helium (invisible to both EDS and XPS), or has its peak hidden under another element's. Raman is silent for vibrations that do not change the polarisability and for metals.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A DSC peak temperature is a property of the material."** Peak and onset temperatures move with heating rate, sample mass and thermal history; Kissinger's method relies on exactly that. Report the heating rate with every temperature, and remember that a polymer's crystallinity depends on how it was made.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Mass lost in a TGA means the material decomposed."** It may be moisture or solvent leaving at low temperature, and in air a metal or carbon-containing material can gain mass by oxidation. Identify the gas before naming the reaction.

</Misconception>

</Section>
