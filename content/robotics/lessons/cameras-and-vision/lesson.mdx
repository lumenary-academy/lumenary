<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="The cheapest, richest and most ambiguous sensor">

A tea-picking robot in Assam must find the two leaves and a bud at the tip of each shoot. A warehouse robot in Rotterdam reads the labels on totes. A hospital robot in São Paulo finds its charging dock by a printed square on the wall. All three use cameras, because a camera costing a few dollars delivers millions of measurements thirty or more times a second: colour, texture, text, and the shapes of things. No other sensor comes close in information per unit of cost.

The catch is that a camera throws away one dimension. Every point along a ray from the lens lands on the same pixel, so a photograph cannot tell a small box nearby from a large box far away. Robot vision is largely the art of getting the third dimension back: from known sizes, from markers, from two views, from motion, from learned priors. This lesson builds the geometry, using the frames and transforms of <LessonLink slug="frames-and-transforms">Frames and rigid transforms</LessonLink>, and then surveys how features, markers and neural networks turn pixels into the measurements that the filters of this level consume.

</Section>

<Section id="pinhole" kind="intuition" title="The pinhole camera">

Make a tiny hole in one wall of a dark box, and an upside-down image of the scene appears on the opposite wall: every ray of light passes through the one point of the hole. This **camera obscura** was described by the scholar Ibn al-Haytham in Cairo around 1020, and a modern camera with a lens is, to a very good approximation, the same thing. The hole is the **camera centre**; the lens only gathers more light.

Put the camera centre at the origin, with the optical axis $z$ pointing out of the lens. A point at height $Y$ and depth $Z$ appears on an image plane at distance $f$ (the focal length) at height $y = fY/Z$, by similar triangles. Nearer things look bigger in proportion to $1/Z$: that is perspective.

Projection is the map $(X, Y, Z) \mapsto (X/Z, Y/Z)$, and dividing by $Z$ is exactly what homogeneous coordinates were invented for: the camera is a linear map in projective space, followed by "forget the overall scale". This is why the mathematics of cameras is projective geometry, and why lines stay lines (until the lens bends them) while lengths and angles do not survive.

<Section id="model" kind="definitions" title="Intrinsics, extrinsics and distortion">

Cameras follow the image convention from <LessonLink slug="frames-and-transforms">Frames and rigid transforms</LessonLink>: the **optical frame** $C$ has $z$ out of the lens, $x$ to the right of the image and $y$ down. Pixel coordinates $(u, v)$ start at the top-left corner, $u$ to the right and $v$ down.

<Definition term="Camera intrinsics">

The **intrinsic matrix**

$$
K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & s & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

holds the focal lengths in pixels ($f_x = f/\text{pixel width}$, $f_y$ likewise), the **principal point** $(c_x, c_y)$ where the optical axis meets the image, and a skew $s$ that is zero for every modern sensor. A point ${}^{C}\mathbf{p} = (X, Y, Z)$ projects to $\lambda\,(u, v, 1)^{\mathsf T} = K\,{}^{C}\mathbf{p}$, with $\lambda = Z$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Camera extrinsics and the projection equation">

The **extrinsics** are the camera's pose, for instance ${}^{W}T_{C}$ in the world or ${}^{B}T_{C}$ on the robot body. A world point projects as

$$
\lambda \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = K\,\big[\,I \;\; \mathbf{0}\,\big]\;{}^{C}T_{W} \begin{pmatrix} {}^{W}\mathbf{p} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad {}^{C}T_{W} = \big({}^{W}T_{C}\big)^{-1}.
$$

The $3\times4$ matrix $K\,[I\;\mathbf{0}]\,{}^{C}T_{W}$ is the **camera matrix**.

</Definition>

Real lenses bend straight lines, most strongly near the edges of the image. The common **Brown–Conrady** model distorts the normalised coordinates $x = X/Z$, $y = Y/Z$ before $K$ is applied: with $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, the radial part is $(x, y) \mapsto (1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6)\,(x, y)$, plus small tangential terms $p_1, p_2$ for a lens not quite parallel to the sensor. Negative $k_1$ gives **barrel** distortion (wide-angle lenses), positive gives **pincushion**. Fisheye lenses, with fields of view of 180° or more, need different models altogether, such as the equidistant or Kannala–Brandt models.

The program projects the corners of a box on the floor through a camera mounted on a robot, exactly as in the frames lesson ($20$ cm ahead of the body origin and $45$ cm up, looking forward), then distorts and undistorts a few pixels.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Intrinsics of a 640 x 480 camera: focal lengths and principal point, in pixels.
fx = fy = 600.0
cx, cy = 320.0, 240.0
K = np.array([[fx, 0, cx], [0, fy, cy], [0, 0, 1.0]])

# Extrinsics: the camera's pose on the robot body, B_T_C (optical frame: z forward, x right, y down).
B_T_C = np.eye(4)
B_T_C[:3, :3] = [[0, 0, 1], [-1, 0, 0], [0, -1, 0]]
B_T_C[:3, 3] = [0.20, 0.0, 0.45]                   # 20 cm ahead of the body origin, 45 cm up
C_T_B = np.linalg.inv(B_T_C)

def project(p_B):
    """Body-frame points (n, 3) -> pixels (n, 2) and depths, for an ideal pinhole camera."""
    p_C = (C_T_B @ np.c_[p_B, np.ones(len(p_B))].T)[:3]
    uvw = K @ p_C
    return (uvw[:2] / uvw[2]).T, p_C[2]

# A 30 cm box on the floor, 2 m ahead of the robot and 0.4 m to its left.
box = np.array([[x, y, z] for x in (2.0, 2.3) for y in (0.25, 0.55) for z in (0.0, 0.3)])
uv, depth = project(box)
print("corner (body frame, m)      pixel (u, v)      depth (m)")
for p, q, d in zip(box, uv, depth):
    print(f"({p[0]:.2f}, {p[1]:.2f}, {p[2]:.2f})   ->   ({q[0]:7.1f}, {q[1]:6.1f})     {d:.2f}")

# Depth is lost: every point on the same ray lands on the same pixel.
lens = B_T_C[:3, 3]                                 # the camera centre, in the body frame
ray = lens + np.outer([1.0, 2.0, 4.0], [1.0, 0.3, -0.1])  # 1, 2 and 4 steps along one direction
print("three points on one ray ->", np.round(project(ray)[0], 2).tolist(), " depths", project(ray)[1].round(2).tolist())
print(f"horizontal field of view: {np.degrees(2 * np.arctan(cx / fx)):.1f} deg")

# Lens distortion (Brown-Conrady radial terms) on normalised coordinates x = X/Z, y = Y/Z.
k1, k2 = -0.25, 0.08                                # a typical wide-ish lens: barrel distortion
def distort(xn):
    r2 = np.sum(xn**2, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return xn * (1 + k1 * r2 + k2 * r2**2)

def undistort(xd, iters=10):                        # no closed form: fixed-point iteration
    xn = xd.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        r2 = np.sum(xn**2, axis=1, keepdims=True)
        xn = xd / (1 + k1 * r2 + k2 * r2**2)
    return xn

pixels = np.array([[320.0, 240.0], [480.0, 240.0], [640.0, 240.0], [640.0, 480.0]])
xn = (pixels - [cx, cy]) / [fx, fy]
moved = distort(xn) * [fx, fy] + [cx, cy]
back = undistort(distort(xn)) * [fx, fy] + [cx, cy]
print("\nideal pixel      distorted pixel     shift (px)   recovered by undistortion")
for a, b, c in zip(pixels, moved, back):
    print(f"({a[0]:5.0f}, {a[1]:4.0f})   ({b[0]:6.1f}, {b[1]:6.1f})   {np.hypot(*(b - a)):8.1f}       ({c[0]:.2f}, {c[1]:.2f})")
```

The box's near face, 1.8 m from the lens, spans 100 pixels across; the far face, 2.1 m away, spans 86. Three points at depths 1, 2 and 4 m along one ray all land on pixel $(140, 300)$. With $f_x = 600$ px across a 640-pixel image, the horizontal field of view is $2\arctan(320/600) = 56.1°$. The distortion barely moves a pixel near the centre (2.8 px halfway to the edge) but moves the image corner by 38 px. Undistortion has no closed form; the fixed-point iteration recovers the ideal pixels to within a hundredth of a pixel.

<Section id="calibration" kind="human" title="Calibration: measuring K and the distortion">

Intrinsics are measured, not looked up: the nominal focal length on a datasheet is good to a few per cent, and the principal point is rarely exactly at the image centre. **Zhang's method** (2000), used by OpenCV, MATLAB and most other tools, needs only a flat pattern of known geometry, typically a printed checkerboard or a ChArUco board (a checkerboard with markers, so partial views still work):

1. Photograph the board in 10 to 30 different orientations, filling different parts of the image, especially the corners where distortion is strongest.
2. In each image, detect the board's corners with sub-pixel accuracy. Because the board is flat, each view is related to the board by a $3\times3$ **homography** $H = K\,[\mathbf{r}_1\;\mathbf{r}_2\;\mathbf{t}]$, estimated from the corner correspondences.
3. The columns $\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2$ of a rotation are orthonormal, which gives two linear equations per view in the entries of $K^{-\mathsf T}K^{-1}$. With three or more views, solve for $K$ in closed form; then recover each view's pose.
4. Refine everything (intrinsics, distortion and every pose) together by nonlinear least squares, minimising the **reprojection error**: the distance in pixels between each detected corner and where the model projects it.

A good calibration has an RMS reprojection error of a fraction of a pixel; one well above a pixel usually means a blurred image, a board that was not flat, or too few views near the edges. In OpenCV the whole procedure is a few calls (shown for reading, not run here; it needs OpenCV and real images):

```python
# OpenCV calibration sketch: not run here (needs OpenCV and a folder of board images).
import glob
import cv2
import numpy as np

cols, rows, square = 9, 6, 0.025                       # inner corners, and square size in metres
board = np.zeros((cols * rows, 3), np.float32)
board[:, :2] = np.mgrid[0:cols, 0:rows].T.reshape(-1, 2) * square

object_points, image_points = [], []
for name in sorted(glob.glob("calib/*.png")):
    grey = cv2.imread(name, cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
    found, corners = cv2.findChessboardCorners(grey, (cols, rows))
    if found:
        corners = cv2.cornerSubPix(grey, corners, (11, 11), (-1, -1),
                                   (cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_EPS + cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_MAX_ITER, 30, 1e-3))
        object_points.append(board)
        image_points.append(corners)

rms, K, dist, rvecs, tvecs = cv2.calibrateCamera(object_points, image_points, grey.shape[::-1], None, None)
print("RMS reprojection error (px):", rms)
print("K =\n", K, "\ndistortion (k1, k2, p1, p2, k3) =", dist.ravel())
```

Robots need more than intrinsics. The **extrinsic** calibration ${}^{B}T_{C}$ between a camera and the robot body, or between a camera and a lidar, is estimated from shared views of targets; a camera on an arm's wrist needs **hand–eye calibration**; and a camera fused with an IMU needs their relative pose *and* the time offset between their clocks, which open tools such as ETH Zurich's Kalibr estimate together. As <LessonLink slug="frames-and-transforms">the frames lesson</LessonLink> showed, a 1° error in a mounting rotation misplaces a point 10 m away by 17 cm, so this work is never wasted.

</Section>

<Section id="features" kind="human" title="Features and matching">

To recognise a place, follow motion or build a map, a robot must find the *same physical point* in different images. The classical answer is **local features**.

- A **detector** finds points that can be located precisely: corners (Harris, 1988; FAST, 2006) or blob centres at a range of scales (SIFT, 1999 and 2004). A flat wall or a uniform sky has none; a bookshelf has thousands.
- A **descriptor** summarises the patch around each point as a vector, designed to change little with viewpoint, lighting and rotation. SIFT's is 128 numbers built from gradient directions. ORB (2011), designed for speed and free of patents, uses 256 bits, each the result of comparing two pixel intensities, so two descriptors are compared by counting differing bits (the Hamming distance), which processors do in one instruction.
- **Matching** pairs each descriptor with its nearest neighbour in the other image. Many nearest neighbours are wrong, especially on repetitive textures such as tiles, windows and shelving. David Lowe's **ratio test** keeps a match only if the best candidate is clearly better than the second best (say, distance less than 0.8 times the second distance); an ambiguous match is worse than none.
- **Geometric verification** fits a model (a homography, an essential matrix, a pose) to the surviving matches with **RANSAC** (Fischler and Bolles, 1981): repeatedly fit the model to a random minimal sample, count how many matches agree, keep the best. Outliers that survived the ratio test are rejected because they disagree with the geometry.

Since about 2018, learned features have joined the classical ones. SuperPoint (2018) learns a detector and descriptor together; SuperGlue (2020) and its faster successor LightGlue (2023) match features with attention, the mechanism of Lumenary CS's [attention and transformers](/en/cs/attention-and-transformers/) lesson, reasoning about all the points at once; LoFTR (2021) matches densely without a detector. They are markedly more robust to large changes of viewpoint, season and lighting, and are standard in visual localisation research; ORB remains common on small robots because it is cheap and predictable.

</Section>

<Section id="markers" kind="computation" title="Fiducial markers and pose from known points">

When a robot needs a pose it can trust (a dock, a pallet, a calibration target, a test rig), it is easiest to put a known pattern in the world. **Fiducial markers** such as AprilTag (Edwin Olson, University of Michigan, 2011; later versions 2016 and 2019) and ArUco (University of Córdoba) are black-and-white squares whose interior encodes an identity with error-correcting codes. A detector finds quadrilaterals, reads the code and reports the identity and the four corner pixels to sub-pixel accuracy.

Four corners of a square of known size are enough to recover the marker's full pose ${}^{C}T_{M}$: this is the **Perspective-n-Point** (PnP) problem. Closed-form solvers exist (P3P and its relatives, and IPPE for planar targets), but the most instructive method is the one every solver finishes with: **least squares on the reprojection error**. Start from a rough guess; repeatedly linearise the projection of the four corners around the current pose (a $8\times6$ Jacobian: 8 pixel coordinates, 6 pose parameters), solve for the small motion that best reduces the error, and apply it. This is Gauss–Newton, the same method as numerical <LessonLink slug="inverse-kinematics">inverse kinematics</LessonLink>, with the pose update applied as a small rotation vector through Rodrigues' formula, as in <LessonLink slug="rotations-and-quaternions">Rotations and quaternions</LessonLink>.

```python run
import numpy as np

K = np.array([[600.0, 0, 320], [0, 600.0, 240], [0, 0, 1]])
s = 0.16 / 2                                           # a 16 cm square marker; corners in its own frame M
corners_M = np.array([[-s, s, 0], [s, s, 0], [s, -s, 0], [-s, -s, 0]])

def rodrigues(w):
    """Rotation matrix exp([w]x) for a rotation vector w (axis times angle)."""
    th = np.linalg.norm(w)
    if th < 1e-12:
        return np.eye(3)
    k = w / th
    Kx = np.array([[0, -k[2], k[1]], [k[2], 0, -k[0]], [-k[1], k[0], 0]])
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(th) * Kx + (1 - np.cos(th)) * Kx @ Kx

def project(R, t, P):                                   # pose C_T_M = (R, t): marker in the camera frame
    pc = P @ R.T + t
    uv = pc @ K.T
    return uv[:, :2] / uv[:, 2:]

def solve_pnp(uv, R, t, iters=10, verbose=False):
    """Gauss-Newton on the reprojection error, updating the pose by small rigid motions."""
    for it in range(iters):
        r = (uv - project(R, t, corners_M)).ravel()      # 8 residuals, in pixels
        if verbose:
            print(f"  iteration {it}: RMS reprojection error {np.sqrt(np.mean(r**2)):9.3f} px")
        J = np.zeros((8, 6))
        for j in range(6):                               # numerical Jacobian, one column per pose parameter
            d = np.zeros(6); d[j] = 1e-6
            Rj, tj = rodrigues(d[:3]) @ R, rodrigues(d[:3]) @ t + d[3:]
            J[:, j] = (project(Rj, tj, corners_M) - project(R, t, corners_M)).ravel() / 1e-6
        delta = np.linalg.lstsq(J, r, rcond=None)[0]     # solve J delta = r in the least-squares sense
        dR = rodrigues(delta[:3])
        R, t = dR @ R, dR @ t + delta[3:]                # apply the small motion on the left
    return R, t

# The true pose: the marker 1.5 m away, turned 30 deg about the vertical and tilted back 20 deg.
R_true = rodrigues(np.radians([0, 30, 0])) @ rodrigues(np.radians([-20, 0, 0]))
t_true = np.array([0.10, -0.05, 1.5])
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
uv = project(R_true, t_true, corners_M) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, (4, 2))     # detected corners, 0.5 px noise
print("solving from a guess of 'facing the camera, 1 m away':")
R, t = solve_pnp(uv, np.eye(3), np.array([0, 0, 1.0]), iters=7, verbose=True)
ang = np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R.T @ R_true) - 1) / 2, -1, 1)))
print(f"position error {1000 * np.linalg.norm(t - t_true):.1f} mm, orientation error {ang:.2f} deg")

# How does the answer degrade with distance? 200 noisy detections at each range.
print("\ndistance   sd of estimated distance   RMS orientation error")
for dist in (0.5, 1.5, 3.0, 6.0):
    tt = np.array([0.0, 0.0, dist])
    zs, angs = [], []
    for _ in range(200):
        uv = project(R_true, tt, corners_M) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, (4, 2))
        R, t = solve_pnp(uv, R_true, tt)
        zs.append(t[2])
        angs.append(np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R.T @ R_true) - 1) / 2, -1, 1))))
    print(f"  {dist:3.1f} m          {1000 * np.std(zs):7.1f} mm              {np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(angs))):5.2f} deg")
```

From a poor first guess (facing the camera at 1 m, when the marker is at 1.5 m and turned 30°), the error falls from 37 px to 0.22 px in four iterations and then stops improving: 0.22 px is the level of the pixel noise, so the remaining error is the measurement's, not the solver's. The pose is within 6 mm and 0.34°.

The second table is the important one for system design. With the same 0.5-pixel noise, the standard deviation of the estimated distance grows from 1.3 mm at 0.5 m to 205 mm at 6 m, roughly with the *square* of the distance, and the orientation error grows in proportion to the distance. At 6 m this 16 cm marker spans only 16 pixels, and the four corners barely constrain its tilt.

<Narrator who="gideon">

A small or distant planar marker has a second pose that fits almost as well: the square tilted the "other way" about an axis in its plane projects to nearly the same four corners. Good marker libraries compute both candidates and report the ambiguity; a robot that docks on a marker should check that the pose is stable over several frames and, better, use a larger marker or several markers in a known layout.

</Narrator>

<Narrator who="margaret">

A useful rule of thumb from the projection equation: a marker of side $s$ at distance $Z$ spans about $f s / Z$ pixels. With $f = 600$ px, a 16 cm marker covers 64 px at 1.5 m and 16 px at 6 m. If you need a pose to 1 cm at 6 m with this camera, the arithmetic says you need a bigger marker, a longer focal length, or a different sensor.

</Narrator>

<Section id="learning" kind="human" title="Deep learning for detection and segmentation">

Markers and features find *points*. Robots also need to know *what* things are: a person, a pallet, a ripe tomato, a doorway, the free floor. Since around 2012 this has been the domain of deep neural networks, trained on large labelled datasets; Lumenary CS's [neural networks](/en/cs/neural-networks/), [deep architectures](/en/cs/deep-architectures/) and [attention and transformers](/en/cs/attention-and-transformers/) lessons explain how they work. As of 2026, the main tools a robotics engineer reaches for are:

- **Object detection**: a box and a class label for each object. Convolutional detectors such as the YOLO family (from 2015, developed since by several groups) run at video rate on small embedded GPUs; transformer detectors (DETR, 2020, and its successors) remove hand-tuned post-processing.
- **Segmentation**: a class or instance label for every pixel (Mask R-CNN, 2017), which a robot needs to grasp an object or to know which pixels are drivable floor. Meta's Segment Anything (2023) and SAM 2 (2024) segment almost any object from a click or a box prompt, without training on that object.
- **Open-vocabulary detection**: models trained on images paired with text, such as OWL-ViT and Grounding DINO, find objects described in words ("the blue mug next to the kettle") rather than from a fixed list of classes. They connect robot perception to the language interfaces of the <LessonLink slug="vision-language-action-models">vision-language-action models</LessonLink> of the Frontier level.
- **General-purpose visual features** learned without labels, such as DINOv2 (2023), are used as a starting point for many downstream tasks, from place recognition to grasp prediction.
- **Monocular depth**: networks that estimate depth from a single image, covered in <LessonLink slug="depth-and-point-clouds">Depth sensing and point clouds</LessonLink>.

Engineering with these models is different from engineering with geometry. Their outputs carry no calibrated uncertainty unless you add it; they fail on images unlike their training data (night, rain, a new warehouse's lighting, an unusual object), and they can fail confidently. Robots therefore combine them with geometry: a detector proposes "pallet here", depth or a model fit gives its pose, and a tracker (a <LessonLink slug="kalman-filters">Kalman filter</LessonLink> per object) smooths it over time and rejects one-frame mistakes. Latency matters too: a network taking 100 ms on an embedded computer means the robot is reacting to where things were.

</Section>

<Section id="vo" kind="computation" title="Two views: the first step of visual odometry">

**Visual odometry** (VO) estimates a camera's motion from the images it takes: match features between consecutive frames, find the relative pose that explains the matches, and chain the relative poses together. For a calibrated camera, matches between two views obey the **epipolar constraint**

$$
\mathbf{x}_2^{\mathsf T}\,E\,\mathbf{x}_1 = 0, \qquad E = [\mathbf{t}]_\times R,
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2$ are a point's normalised coordinates $K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ in the two images, $(R, \mathbf{t})$ is the motion, and $[\mathbf{t}]_\times$ is the cross-product matrix. $E$ is the **essential matrix**. Each match gives one equation that is *linear* in the nine entries of $E$, so eight matches determine it (the eight-point algorithm; Nistér's five-point algorithm, used inside RANSAC in practice, needs only five). $E$ factors into four possible motions, and the right one is the one that puts the points in front of both cameras.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
K = np.array([[600.0, 0, 320], [0, 600.0, 240], [0, 0, 1]])

def rot_y(a):
    c, s = np.cos(a), np.sin(a)
    return np.array([[c, 0, s], [0, 1, 0], [-s, 0, c]])

def skew(v):
    return np.array([[0, -v[2], v[1]], [v[2], 0, -v[0]], [-v[1], v[0], 0]])

# 3-D points in front of camera 1, and camera 2's motion: 0.5 m to the right and 5 deg turn.
P1 = np.column_stack([rng.uniform(-2, 2, 40), rng.uniform(-1, 1, 40), rng.uniform(4, 10, 40)])
R, t = rot_y(np.radians(5)), np.array([-0.5, 0.0, 0.05])     # x2 = R x1 + t (camera 2 coordinates)

def pixels(P):
    uv = P @ K.T
    return uv[:, :2] / uv[:, 2:]

uv1, uv2 = pixels(P1), pixels(P1 @ R.T + t)
uv1n, uv2n = uv1 + rng.normal(0, 0.3, uv1.shape), uv2 + rng.normal(0, 0.3, uv2.shape)

# Scale ambiguity: double the scene AND the motion, and the images do not change at all.
change = max(np.abs(pixels(2 * P1) - uv1).max(), np.abs(pixels(2 * P1 @ R.T + 2 * t) - uv2).max())
print(f"largest pixel change when the scene and the motion are both doubled: {change:.6f} px")

# Eight-point algorithm on normalised coordinates: each match gives one linear equation x2^T E x1 = 0.
x1 = np.c_[uv1n, np.ones(40)] @ np.linalg.inv(K).T
x2 = np.c_[uv2n, np.ones(40)] @ np.linalg.inv(K).T
A = np.einsum("ni,nj->nij", x2, x1).reshape(40, 9)
E = np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1].reshape(3, 3)              # the null vector of A
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(E)
E = U @ np.diag([1, 1, 0]) @ Vt                        # an essential matrix has singular values (s, s, 0)

# Four (R, t) pairs fit E; keep the one that puts the points in front of both cameras.
W = np.array([[0, -1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]])
best = None
for Rc in (U @ W @ Vt, U @ W.T @ Vt):
    Rc = Rc * np.sign(np.linalg.det(Rc))
    for tc in (U[:, 2], -U[:, 2]):
        # triangulate each point by least squares: depth1 * R x1 + t = depth2 * x2
        good = 0
        for a, b in zip(x1, x2):
            M = np.column_stack([Rc @ a, -b])
            d1, d2 = np.linalg.lstsq(M, -tc, rcond=None)[0]
            good += (d1 > 0) and (d2 > 0)
        if best is None or good > best[0]:
            best = (good, Rc, tc)
good, R_est, t_est = best
rot_err = np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R_est.T @ R) - 1) / 2, -1, 1)))
dir_err = np.degrees(np.arccos(t_est @ t / np.linalg.norm(t)))
print(f"points in front of both cameras: {good} of 40")
print(f"rotation error {rot_err:.3f} deg; translation direction error {dir_err:.2f} deg")
print(f"estimated translation {np.round(t_est, 3)} has length {np.linalg.norm(t_est):.1f}: the true length,"
      f" {np.linalg.norm(t):.3f} m, cannot be recovered from the images")
print("epipolar residuals |x2^T E x1| (median):", f"{np.median(np.abs(np.einsum('ni,ij,nj->n', x2, E, x1))):.1e}")
```

Doubling the scene and the camera's motion together changes no pixel at all, so no algorithm working on these images alone can measure the length of $\mathbf{t}$. The eight-point algorithm recovers the rotation to 0.3° and the *direction* of travel to 1.5° from 40 noisy matches, and returns a translation of length 1 by convention. This **scale ambiguity** is fundamental to a single camera: a monocular VO system knows the shape of its path but not its size, and the unknown scale slowly drifts as errors accumulate. It is resolved by a second camera with a known baseline (stereo), by an IMU, whose accelerometer measures metres per second squared, by wheel odometry, or by objects of known size. Visual-inertial odometry, a camera fused with an IMU, is what phones, headsets and most drones use; the filters and optimisers that do it appear in <LessonLink slug="kalman-filters">Kalman filters</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="slam">SLAM</LessonLink>.

A practical VO front end, sketched with OpenCV (for reading, not run here):

```python
# Monocular VO step with OpenCV: not run here (needs OpenCV and two images).
import cv2
import numpy as np

orb = cv2.ORB_create(nfeatures=2000)
kp1, d1 = orb.detectAndCompute(img1, None)
kp2, d2 = orb.detectAndCompute(img2, None)
pairs = cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_HAMMING).knnMatch(d1, d2, k=2)
good = [m for m, n in pairs if m.distance < 0.8 * n.distance]           # Lowe's ratio test
p1 = np.float32([kp1[m.queryIdx].pt for m in good])
p2 = np.float32([kp2[m.trainIdx].pt for m in good])
E, inliers = cv2.findEssentialMat(p1, p2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)
n, R, t, mask = cv2.recoverPose(E, p1, p2, K, mask=inliers)              # t has unit length
```

<Section id="practice" kind="human" title="In practice">

Several details separate a camera that works on the bench from one that works on a robot.

- **Shutter.** Most inexpensive sensors have a **rolling shutter**: rows are exposed one after another over several milliseconds, so a fast-turning robot sees vertical lines leaning. **Global-shutter** sensors expose all pixels at once and are preferred for VO and for anything fast.
- **Exposure and motion blur.** In dim light the exposure lengthens, and a moving robot smears features; auto-exposure that changes between frames also confuses matching. Robots often fix exposure limits and add lighting.
- **Time.** A camera image must be stamped with when it was *exposed*, not when it arrived, and synchronised with the IMU and odometry; a 20 ms offset at 1 m/s is a 2 cm error before any vision is done.
- **Software.** In ROS 2, a camera driver publishes `sensor_msgs/Image` with a `CameraInfo` message holding $K$ and the distortion, and the `image_pipeline` packages rectify images; OpenCV provides most classical algorithms, and marker detection is available in both OpenCV and the AprilTag library.
- **Safety and privacy.** Cameras on robots in homes, hospitals and streets record people. Good practice processes images on the robot, stores as little as possible, and follows local data-protection law.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The focal length is 4 mm, so $f_x = 4$."** $K$ uses focal lengths *in pixels*: the focal length divided by the pixel pitch. A 4 mm lens on a sensor with 3 µm pixels has $f_x \approx 1333$ px. Calibration measures this number directly.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The image $y$ axis points up."** In image and optical-frame conventions it points down, and $v$ grows downwards from the top-left corner. Mixing this up flips poses upside down, and it is one of the most common bugs when connecting vision code to robot frames.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A single camera can measure distance if the software is clever enough."** Not from geometry alone: the program showed that scaling the world and the motion together leaves every pixel unchanged. Monocular depth networks estimate distance from learned *priors* (how big cars and doors usually are), which works often and fails without warning on unusual scenes.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Distortion only matters for fisheye lenses."** Even ordinary lenses move pixels near the image corners by tens of pixels, as the first program showed. Ignoring distortion bends straight walls in a map and biases every pose estimated from points near the edges.

</Misconception>
