<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Two questions customers now ask">

Two questions come up in cloud contracts in 2026 that rarely came up in 2016. The first is **what does this cost the planet?** Data centres used about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, around 1.5% of the world's total, and the International Energy Agency's base case has that roughly doubling by 2030, with AI the largest driver ([IEA, *Energy and AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai)). Regulators now ask for numbers: larger data centres in the EU must report their energy and water use ([Delegated Regulation 2024/1364](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_del/2024/1364/oj)), and many companies must report the emissions of what they buy, including cloud services.

The second is **can my provider see my data?** Encryption at rest and in transit, from <LessonLink slug="cloud-security-and-identity">security and identity</LessonLink>, protects data on disks and wires, but a program must decrypt data to compute on it, and while it does, the host operating system, the hypervisor and anyone who controls them could in principle read it. For a hospital in Seoul, a bank in Johannesburg or a government in Paris weighing the sovereignty questions of the <LessonLink slug="edge-multicloud-and-sovereignty">previous lesson</LessonLink>, that gap matters.

This lesson answers both with numbers and mechanisms: energy, carbon and water, computed; carbon-aware scheduling, simulated; trusted execution and attestation, built in miniature; the new isolation runtimes, compared; and a short, dated look at where the cloud is going. How the electricity reaches the building is Lumenary EE's subject, in [powering data centres](/en/ee/powering-data-centres/) and [renewables and storage](/en/ee/renewables-and-storage/).

</Section>

<Section id="energy" kind="definitions" title="Energy, carbon and water">

Most of the electricity a data centre draws goes into the IT equipment — servers, storage, network. The rest goes into cooling, power conversion and distribution losses, lighting and offices.

<Definition term="Power usage effectiveness (PUE)">

The ratio of a facility's total energy to the energy delivered to its IT equipment, measured over a year:

$$
\text{PUE} = \frac{\text{facility energy}}{\text{IT energy}} \ge 1 .
$$

A PUE of 1.5 means half a kilowatt-hour of overhead for every kilowatt-hour of computing. Introduced by The Green Grid and standardised as ISO/IEC 30134-2, it has companion metrics: **WUE**, litres of water per kWh of IT energy, and **CUE**, kilograms of CO₂ per kWh of IT energy.

</Definition>

Uptime Institute's 2025 survey put the industry's average PUE at 1.54, almost unchanged since 2018 ([Uptime Institute](https://intelligence.uptimeinstitute.com/resource/uptime-institute-global-data-center-survey-2025)); large providers report fleet averages of roughly 1.1 to 1.2 for their own newest sites. PUE is a measure of the building, not of the computing: a data centre full of idle servers can have an excellent PUE.

<Definition term="Carbon intensity">

The mass of greenhouse gases, in CO₂-equivalent, emitted per kilowatt-hour of electricity: g CO₂e/kWh. The **average** intensity of a grid is total emissions divided by total generation; the **marginal** intensity is the emissions of the generators that respond when demand changes by a little. Both vary by place and by hour.

</Definition>

Grid intensities differ by more than a factor of ten. Grids built largely on hydro, nuclear or geothermal power — Norway, France, Kenya, Québec — run at tens of grams per kWh in typical hours; coal-heavy grids such as those of India, South Africa or Poland at several hundred, around 700 or more at their most coal-dependent; most others lie between. Open data from [Ember](https://ember-energy.org/) and [Electricity Maps](https://www.electricitymaps.com/) give the current figures, and since the best region for carbon is often not the best for latency or law, this becomes a real design input.

The block computes the footprint of one month-long training run — 1,024 GPUs — in four kinds of site, and the energy per token of serving, using example figures.

```python run
# Energy, carbon and water of one month-long training run, in different data centres.
# All figures are illustrative examples; real ones come from metering.
gpus = 1024
kw_per_gpu = 1.2            # average draw per GPU including its share of the server (CPUs, memory, fans, NICs)
hours = 30 * 24
it_mwh = gpus * kw_per_gpu * hours / 1000

sites = [   # (description, PUE, on-site water use effectiveness in litres per kWh of IT energy, grid gCO2e/kWh)
    ("modern site, low-carbon grid",        1.10, 0.2,  50),
    ("modern site, mixed grid",             1.10, 0.2, 400),
    ("evaporative cooling, mixed grid",     1.20, 1.8, 400),
    ("older site, coal-heavy grid",         1.60, 0.5, 700),
]

print(f"IT energy: {it_mwh:,.0f} MWh  ({gpus} GPUs x {kw_per_gpu} kW x {hours} h)\n")
print(f"{'site':34s} {'PUE':>4s} {'facility MWh':>12s} {'overhead MWh':>12s} {'t CO2e':>7s} {'water m3':>9s}")
for name, pue, wue, grid in sites:
    facility = it_mwh * pue                       # PUE = facility energy / IT energy
    tonnes = facility * 1000 * grid / 1e6         # kWh x g/kWh -> tonnes
    water = it_mwh * 1000 * wue / 1000            # WUE is per kWh of IT energy; litres -> cubic metres
    print(f"{name:34s} {pue:4.2f} {facility:12,.0f} {facility - it_mwh:12,.0f} {tonnes:7,.0f} {water:9,.0f}")

# The same arithmetic for serving: an 8-GPU server making 6,000 output tokens per second (example).
server_kw = 8 * kw_per_gpu * 1.10                 # with PUE 1.10
wh_per_1000_tokens = server_kw * 1000 / 6000 * 1000 / 3600
print(f"\nserving: {wh_per_1000_tokens:.2f} Wh per 1,000 output tokens at full load; "
      f"{wh_per_1000_tokens * 400 / 1000:.2f} g CO2e on a 400 g/kWh grid")
```

The run uses 885 MWh of IT energy, about what 250 households using 3,500 kWh a year each would use in a year. The building's PUE moves the total by up to 45%, but the grid matters far more: 49 tonnes of CO₂e in a modern site on a low-carbon grid against 991 in an older site on a coal-heavy one, a factor of twenty, of which the grid alone accounts for fourteen. Water tells a third story: evaporative cooling lowers PUE by spending water, nine times as much here as the modern sites. Serving costs about half a watt-hour per thousand tokens at full load in this example — the same order of magnitude as Google's published figure of 0.24 Wh for its median Gemini Apps text prompt, which counted idle capacity and overheads but not training ([Google, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15734)) — and much more when servers sit half idle.

<Narrator who="margaret">

Before trusting anyone's footprint figure, mine included, check its boundary. Does it count idle machines held in reserve, the cooling and power overhead, the network, the user's device, the training run amortised over its queries, the manufacture of the chips? Published estimates for "one AI query" have differed by more than ten times largely because they drew these boundaries differently.

</Narrator>

### Accounting, and its arguments

Companies report electricity emissions under the [GHG Protocol](https://ghgprotocol.org/scope-2-guidance) in two ways. **Location-based** accounting multiplies consumption by the average intensity of the local grid. **Market-based** accounting subtracts clean energy the company has bought — renewable certificates, or long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) — so a company can report near-zero emissions while drawing power from a fossil-heavy grid at night, provided it bought enough solar energy over the year. That gap led some providers to adopt the stricter goal of **hourly matching** (often called 24/7 carbon-free energy), in which clean supply must match consumption hour by hour and place by place, and the GHG Protocol has been revising its Scope 2 guidance with hourly matching among the proposals. Beyond electricity lies **embodied carbon** — making the chips, servers and buildings — which can dominate on a clean grid and grows with every hardware refresh.

### Water

Data centres consume water directly, by evaporation in cooling towers and adiabatic coolers, and indirectly, through the power plants that supply them. Evaporative cooling typically consumes one to two litres per kWh of IT energy in warm climates; air cooling with dry coolers uses almost none on site but more electricity; closed-loop liquid cooling, now standard for racks drawing over 100 kW, moves heat efficiently but must still reject it somewhere. Distinguish **withdrawal** (water taken) from **consumption** (water not returned), and note *where*: a litre evaporated in a water-stressed region matters more than one in a wet one. Estimates of AI's water footprint ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271)) are contested in their assumptions but have made the question visible, and some operators now publish per-site water data.

<Section id="carbon-aware" kind="computation" title="Carbon-aware scheduling">

Not all work is urgent. Training runs, nightly batch jobs, video transcoding, model evaluation and backups usually have deadlines measured in hours, not milliseconds. **Carbon-aware scheduling** moves such work to the hours (**time shifting**) or the regions (**location shifting**) with cleaner electricity. Google has described doing this across its fleet, using day-ahead forecasts of carbon intensity to set hour-by-hour limits on flexible compute in each cluster for the next day ([Radovanović et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11750)); open tools such as the Green Software Foundation's [Carbon Aware SDK](https://github.com/Green-Software-Foundation/carbon-aware-sdk) expose intensity forecasts to any scheduler.

The simulation runs 40 flexible jobs over two days in a sunny region whose grid is cleanest at midday and a smaller hydro-and-wind region that is cleaner but has room for only two jobs at a time. Each job has a deadline; jobs are placed in order of deadline, and capacity is respected.

```python run
import numpy as np

# Two days of hourly grid carbon intensity (g CO2e/kWh) in two regions, synthetic but shaped like real grids:
# "south" has much solar power (cleanest at midday, dirtiest at night); "north" is hydro and wind (low, flatter).
hours = np.arange(48)
solar = np.clip(np.sin((hours % 24 - 6) / 12 * np.pi), 0, None)          # 0 at night, 1 at noon
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
INTENSITY = {
    "south": 520 - 330 * solar + rng.normal(0, 15, 48),
    "north": 160 + 40 * np.sin(hours / 9) + rng.normal(0, 10, 48),
}
CAPACITY = {"south": 6, "north": 2}      # jobs each region can run at once; the north region is small

# 40 flexible batch jobs: (submitted at hour, hours to run, power in kW, hours allowed from submission to finish)
jobs = [(int(rng.integers(0, 24)), int(rng.integers(1, 6)), float(rng.choice([20, 40, 80])), int(rng.integers(8, 24)))
        for _ in range(40)]

def kg_co2e(region, start, dur, kw):
    return kw * INTENSITY[region][start:start + dur].sum() / 1000

def schedule(policy):
    load = {r: np.zeros(48, dtype=int) for r in INTENSITY}
    total, late = 0.0, 0
    for sub, dur, kw, allowed in sorted(jobs, key=lambda j: j[0] + j[3]):   # earliest deadline first
        regions = ["south", "north"] if policy == "shift in time and place" else ["south"]
        free = [(s, r) for r in regions for s in range(sub, 48 - dur + 1) if (load[r][s:s + dur] < CAPACITY[r]).all()]
        on_time = [(s, r) for s, r in free if s + dur <= sub + allowed]
        if policy == "run as soon as possible" or not on_time:
            s, r = min(free)                                   # earliest start with room
        else:
            s, r = min(on_time, key=lambda o: kg_co2e(o[1], o[0], dur, kw))
        late += s + dur > sub + allowed
        load[r][s:s + dur] += 1
        total += kg_co2e(r, s, dur, kw)
    return total, late

base, _ = schedule("run as soon as possible")
for policy in ["run as soon as possible", "shift in time", "shift in time and place"]:
    kg, late = schedule(policy)
    print(f"{policy:24s} {kg:6,.0f} kg CO2e  ({1 - kg / base:5.1%} less)   deadlines missed: {late}")
```

Shifting in time alone saves 9.2%: the deadlines are too short for many jobs to wait for noon, and the sunny region has room for only six jobs at once when they do. Allowing jobs to move to the cleaner region as well saves 43.6%, with no deadline missed. In practice location shifting is limited by everything the previous lessons taught: where the data is and what moving it costs, latency, residency rules, and whether the cleaner region has spare capacity — which, for the newest accelerators, it often does not.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The simulation scores each job by the grid's *average* intensity, which is what most dashboards show. But the emissions a decision actually changes are *marginal*: if moving a job to noon is absorbed by solar power that would otherwise have been curtailed, the saving is large; if it is met by a gas plant ramping up, it may be small. Marginal and average signals can disagree even about which hour is cleaner, and at large scale shifting load changes the grid it responds to. Use marginal data where you can, and say which you used.

</Narrator>

<Section id="confidential" kind="definitions" title="Confidential computing">

All the isolation in <LessonLink slug="virtualisation-and-containers">virtualisation and containers</LessonLink> protects tenants from each other and the provider from tenants. Confidential computing turns the arrow round: it protects a tenant's code and data **from the provider** — from the host operating system, the hypervisor, the cloud's operators, and anyone who has compromised them.

<Definition term="Trusted execution environment (TEE)">

A hardware-enforced region of execution whose memory is encrypted and integrity-protected by the processor, with keys the processor generates and never reveals, so that software outside it — including the most privileged software on the machine — cannot read or tamper with what is inside. A TEE can produce **attestation evidence**: a report, signed by a key rooted in the hardware, of what software was loaded into it.

</Definition>

TEEs come in two shapes. **Process enclaves**, such as Intel SGX, protect part of an application, which must be split into trusted and untrusted halves; the trusted computing base is small, but porting software is hard. **Confidential virtual machines** protect a whole VM, so unmodified operating systems and applications run inside: [AMD SEV-SNP](https://www.amd.com/en/developer/sev.html), [Intel TDX](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/tools/trust-domain-extensions/overview.html) and the realms of the [Arm Confidential Compute Architecture](https://www.arm.com/architecture/security-features/arm-confidential-compute-architecture), announced in 2021, whose hardware support is arriving later than the other two's. Since 2023 GPUs have joined: [NVIDIA's](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/solutions/confidential-computing/) Hopper and Blackwell GPUs can run in a confidential mode, encrypting traffic between the confidential VM and the GPU, which matters for AI models and prompts. The [Confidential Containers](https://confidentialcontainers.org/) project brings the same protection to Kubernetes pods.

Providers offer these side by side, each documented on its own pages: confidential VMs on [Azure](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/confidential-computing/), [Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/confidential-computing/docs/confidential-computing-overview), [AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/sev-snp.html) and [Alibaba Cloud](https://www.alibabacloud.com/help/en/ecs/user-guide/confidential-computing), among others, mostly at a small premium. AWS also offers [Nitro Enclaves](https://aws.amazon.com/ec2/nitro/nitro-enclaves/), a different design: isolated environments carved out of an instance by AWS's own Nitro hypervisor, with attestation by the Nitro system — so the root of trust is the provider's hardware and firmware rather than the CPU vendor's.

### Attestation: trusting a workload you cannot see

Memory encryption alone is not enough. How does a data owner know that the VM asking for a decryption key is really running inside a TEE, on genuine hardware, with the software they approved — and not a copy the operator modified to leak data? **Remote attestation** answers that, in the roles the IETF's RATS architecture names ([RFC 9334](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9334)):

1. While the TEE boots, the hardware **measures** each component — firmware, kernel, application — by hashing it into a register.
2. A **verifier** sends a fresh random **nonce**. The TEE asks the hardware for a report containing the measurements and the nonce, signed with a key whose certificate chains to the manufacturer.
3. The verifier checks the signature chain, the nonce, security-relevant flags (no debug mode, current firmware) and that the measurements match **reference values**, ideally produced by reproducible builds of software the owner has reviewed.
4. Only then does a **relying party**, often a key-management service, release the secret — typically wrapped to a public key the TEE put in the report, so that only that TEE can unwrap it.

The block builds the protocol in miniature with Python's standard library, and tries five ways of fooling it.

```python run
import hashlib
import hmac

def extend(register, component):
    """Measured boot: fold each component's hash into a running register, as TPMs and TEEs do.
    The result depends on every component and on their order, and cannot be 'un-extended'."""
    return hashlib.sha256(register + hashlib.sha256(component).digest()).digest()

def measure(components):
    register = bytes(32)
    for c in components:
        register = extend(register, c)
    return register

# Stand-in for the chip's attestation key. Real TEEs sign reports with an asymmetric key whose certificate
# chains to the manufacturer, so anyone can verify them; an HMAC key keeps this sketch to the standard library.
CHIP_KEY = b"fused-into-the-processor-at-manufacture"

def tee_report(components, nonce, debug=False):
    """What the hardware produces when the workload asks for evidence: measurement, the verifier's nonce, a flag."""
    body = measure(components) + nonce + (b"\x01" if debug else b"\x00")
    return body, hmac.new(CHIP_KEY, body, hashlib.sha256).digest()

APPROVED = {measure([b"firmware v7", b"kernel 6.12 hardened", b"model-server 2026.11"])}   # from reproducible builds

def verify_and_release(report, nonce):
    body, tag = report
    if not hmac.compare_digest(tag, hmac.new(CHIP_KEY, body, hashlib.sha256).digest()):
        return "refused: not signed by genuine hardware"
    measurement, got_nonce, debug = body[:32], body[32:-1], body[-1]
    if got_nonce != nonce:
        return "refused: stale or replayed evidence"
    if debug:
        return "refused: debug mode lets the host read guest memory"
    if measurement not in APPROVED:
        return "refused: software is not on the approved list"
    return "released: data key sent to this workload"

good = [b"firmware v7", b"kernel 6.12 hardened", b"model-server 2026.11"]
n1, n2 = b"nonce-1f4a", b"nonce-93c0"
old_report = tee_report(good, n1)
forged_body = measure(good) + n2 + b"\x00"
cases = {
    "genuine workload, fresh nonce":         tee_report(good, n2),
    "host swapped in a logging kernel":      tee_report([b"firmware v7", b"kernel 6.12 + debug log", b"model-server 2026.11"], n2),
    "same components, different order":      tee_report([b"kernel 6.12 hardened", b"firmware v7", b"model-server 2026.11"], n2),
    "old genuine report replayed":           old_report,
    "report forged without the chip's key":  (forged_body, hashlib.sha256(forged_body).digest()),
    "genuine software, debug mode on":       tee_report(good, n2, debug=True),
}
for name, report in cases.items():
    print(f"{name:38s} -> {verify_and_release(report, n2)}")
```

Only the genuine workload, with a fresh nonce and debug mode off, gets the key. A kernel modified by the operator produces a different measurement; so does the right software loaded in the wrong order; an old report fails the nonce check; a report not signed by the chip fails the signature; and debug mode, which would let the host read the VM's memory, is refused by policy even though the software is right.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The idea is to move trust rather than remove it. Without attestation you trust everyone who can touch the host; with it you trust the chip's manufacturer, a few kilobytes of measured firmware, and the software whose hash you approved. Notice that the last of these is *your* responsibility: attestation proves exactly which software is running, not that the software is any good.

</Narrator>

### The trust model, precisely

A confidential VM is designed to protect the confidentiality and integrity of its memory against the host software and the provider's operators. It does **not** protect:

- **Availability.** The host can always stop, pause or starve the VM.
- **Input and output.** Disk and network traffic leave the TEE; the guest must encrypt them itself, and verify what it reads.
- **Against bugs in the guest.** A vulnerable application inside a TEE is still vulnerable; attestation will faithfully report that it is running.
- **Against all side channels.** Since 2018, researchers have repeatedly shown how timing, caches, power and speculative execution can leak secrets from SGX and SEV, and vendors have repeatedly patched them.
- **Against physical attacks, in most designs.** In 2025 academic researchers published three attacks in which inexpensive devices placed on a server's memory bus defeat the memory encryption of current TEEs — [WireTap](https://wiretap.fail/) and [Battering RAM](https://batteringram.eu/) on DDR4 systems, [TEE.fail](https://tee.fail/) on DDR5 systems with Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP, including extracting attestation keys. The vendors responded that physical attacks of this kind are outside their stated threat models. The practical lesson is that confidential computing reduces how much you must trust the operator; it does not make physical security of the building irrelevant.

Used with that understanding, confidential computing has real uses: releasing decryption keys only to attested workloads in a chosen jurisdiction (a technical answer to some sovereignty questions); letting several organisations analyse pooled data that none may see in full; protecting model weights and user prompts in AI serving. Some AI services now describe designs in which the software images of their serving nodes are published so that outside researchers can check what attestation will accept ([Apple's Private Cloud Compute](https://security.apple.com/blog/private-cloud-compute/) is one documented example). The [Confidential Computing Consortium](https://confidentialcomputing.io/) of the Linux Foundation hosts much of the open-source work.

<Section id="isolation" kind="human" title="WebAssembly and new isolation runtimes">

The isolation table in <LessonLink slug="virtualisation-and-containers">virtualisation and containers</LessonLink> ran from processes to bare-metal hosts. Two newer kinds of boundary now sit near its top, trading generality for start-up times of microseconds to milliseconds and very high density — exactly what edge platforms and <LessonLink slug="serverless-and-event-driven">serverless</LessonLink> functions need.

**Language-VM isolates.** A JavaScript engine such as V8 can host thousands of separate *isolates* in one process, each with its own heap, started in a few milliseconds. Edge platforms use them to run many customers' functions on every server; the boundary is the engine's correctness, so such platforms add process sandboxes and other defences around it.

**WebAssembly.** [WebAssembly](https://webassembly.org/) (Wasm) is a compact, portable bytecode, standardised by the W3C and first shipped in browsers in 2017, that compilers for C, C++, Rust, Go, Python and many other languages can target. Three properties make it attractive beyond the browser:

- **A sandbox by construction.** A module can touch only its own linear memory, bounds-checked by the runtime, and can call only the functions its host gives it.
- **Capabilities, not ambient authority.** Through [WASI](https://wasi.dev/), the system interface, a module gets no files, sockets or clock unless the host grants them, directory by directory. A container, by contrast, sees whatever its kernel exposes unless something takes it away.
- **Components.** The component model describes typed interfaces between modules written in different languages. WASI 0.2 (2024) was the first stable release built on it, and [WASI 0.3](https://bytecodealliance.org/articles/WASI-0.3), ratified in 2026, adds native asynchronous I/O.

Runtimes include [Wasmtime](https://wasmtime.dev/) (Bytecode Alliance), [WasmEdge](https://wasmedge.org/) (a CNCF project) and [Wasmer](https://wasmer.io/); edge platforms run Wasm at scale, and the [runwasi](https://github.com/containerd/runwasi) shims let Kubernetes schedule Wasm workloads beside containers. The limits are real too: code generally runs somewhat slower than native, support for threads, GPUs and some system interfaces is still maturing, and the runtime's compiler is itself attack surface, so platforms running hostile code layer Wasm inside a process sandbox or microVM rather than relying on it alone.

Other new runtimes chase the same goal from the virtualisation side: [Hyperlight](https://github.com/hyperlight-dev/hyperlight) runs a single function in a tiny VM with no guest operating system, started in a millisecond or two; unikernels such as [Unikraft](https://unikraft.org/) compile an application and only the operating-system parts it needs into one small VM image. A pressing new customer for all of them is AI agents, which write and run code that nobody has reviewed and so need cheap, strong, short-lived sandboxes by the million.

| Boundary | What separates tenants | Start-up, order of magnitude | Fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Container | the shared host kernel | hundreds of ms | one organisation's services |
| MicroVM | a minimal hypervisor | about 100 ms | multi-tenant functions and containers |
| Function VM without a guest OS | a hypervisor, no guest kernel | about 1 ms | very short functions |
| JavaScript isolate | the language engine, plus process sandboxing | a few ms | edge functions |
| WebAssembly instance | bounds-checked memory and granted capabilities | microseconds to ms | edge, plugins, untrusted extensions |
| Confidential VM | hardware memory encryption, against the host too | seconds | data the operator must not see |

</Section>

<Section id="future" kind="human" title="Where the cloud is going">

Predictions about computing age quickly, so here are forces visible in late 2026, with what is uncertain about each.

- **Power is the constraint.** In several regions the wait for a grid connection, not chips or money, sets how fast data centres are built. Providers have signed agreements to buy power from existing and restarted nuclear plants and from small modular reactors not yet built, alongside wind, solar and storage. The uncertainty is large: the IEA's own scenarios for 2035 differ widely, efficiency keeps improving, and greater efficiency has so far tended to raise demand rather than lower it.
- **Heat becomes a resource.** Liquid cooling of dense AI racks produces warmer water that is easier to reuse, and some data centres, especially in the Nordic countries, already feed district-heating networks.
- **Confidential by default.** Confidential VMs cost little more than ordinary ones, and confidential GPUs make protected AI serving practical; the open questions are side channels, physical attacks and whether attestation can be made understandable to the people relying on it.
- **Regional clouds.** Sovereignty rules and capacity scarcity are pushing toward more regions, more local operators and more partner-run clouds, at some cost in scale and features.
- **Finer isolation.** WebAssembly components, function VMs and sandboxes for agents are making units of computation smaller and shorter-lived, extending the line from servers to VMs to containers to functions.
- **Accountability.** Reporting rules for energy and water, emissions disclosure, transparency logs for attested software, and published post-incident reviews all push the cloud toward being measurable from outside.

<Reflection title="Stewardship">

The cloud makes computing feel weightless, yet every query is drawn from rivers, mines and grids that other people depend on. An old idea of stewardship — that what we are given to use is held in trust, for neighbours and for those who come after — fits the cloud well: measure honestly, waste little, and do not hide the costs that others bear.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A low PUE means a green data centre."** PUE measures only the building's overhead. A site with PUE 1.1 on a coal-heavy grid emits far more than one with PUE 1.4 on a hydro grid, and PUE says nothing about water, embodied carbon or idle servers.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Our provider buys 100% renewable energy, so our cloud use has zero emissions."** Annual market-based matching can coexist with fossil power at night and in winter. Hourly, location-based figures tell you what the grid actually supplied.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Confidential computing means the provider can't affect my workload."** It protects memory confidentiality and integrity. The provider can still stop the VM, observe its I/O patterns and timing, and — according to the 2025 research — attack some designs physically.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Attestation proves the code is secure."** It proves which code is running. Whether that code deserves trust depends on review, reproducible builds and the security of its supply chain.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"WebAssembly will replace containers."** It suits small, fast-starting, sandboxed units of code, and is spreading at the edge and in plugins; most existing server software, with its threads, system calls and native libraries, runs more naturally in containers and VMs. The two increasingly run side by side.

</Misconception>
