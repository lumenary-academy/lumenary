<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A chart is an argument">

A regional health service introduced online booking at six clinics in March and wants to know whether waiting times fell. The analysis is done: means for February and April, intervals for the change, notes on the data. Now it has to reach the board, whose members will look at one chart for perhaps half a minute before moving to the next agenda item. Whatever they take away in those thirty seconds *is* the result of the analysis, whatever the appendix says.

A chart is therefore not decoration on top of an analysis; it is an argument, and it can be a good argument or a misleading one. The same numbers can be drawn so that the main finding jumps out, so that it hides, or so that a small change looks dramatic. This lesson is about the choices that make the difference: which chart for which question, what the human visual system judges well and badly, colour that works for everyone, honest axes and uncertainty, and the words around the chart. It ends with a before-and-after redesign of the clinic chart.

The habits here build on the previous lessons: <LessonLink slug="exploratory-data-analysis">exploratory data analysis</LessonLink> made charts for *you*; this lesson makes them for *other people*. For the statistics behind intervals and comparisons, see [estimation and confidence intervals](/en/statistics/estimation-and-confidence-intervals/) in Lumenary Statistics, and for a younger reader's tour of how charts mislead, [misleading statistics](/en/statistics/misleading-statistics/).

</Section>

<Section id="choosing" kind="intuition" title="Start from the question">

Most bad charts begin with the chart type ("let's do a pie chart") instead of the question. Start by writing down, in one sentence, what the reader should be able to see. Then pick the simplest chart that makes it visible.

| The reader needs to… | Good first choices | Usually avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| compare amounts across categories | sorted bar chart (from zero), dot plot | pie with many slices, 3D bars |
| see change over time | line chart; small multiples for several series | bars for long daily series, dual y-axes |
| see a distribution | histogram, box plot, strip or violin plot | a bar of the mean alone |
| see a relationship | scatter plot, with a trend line if it helps | two line charts side by side |
| see parts of a whole | stacked bar; a pie only for two or three parts | pie with ten slices, donut with a hole of text |
| compare before and after per category | dumbbell (slope) chart | grouped bars with a legend |
| see uncertainty | points with intervals, many-outcome displays | bare point estimates, or error bars nobody explains |
| see where | a map only if location matters; otherwise a sorted chart | choropleths where big empty areas dominate |

Two rules of thumb sit behind the table. **One chart, one message**: if a chart must show three things, make three charts, or small multiples (the same small chart repeated per group, on shared axes). And **sort categories by value** unless they have a natural order (months, age groups, ratings), because a sorted chart answers "which is biggest?" before the reader asks.

<Narrator who="chloe">

What question are we really asking? The board's question is not "what were the waiting times?" but "did waits fall, where, and are we sure?" That sentence already chooses the chart: one row per clinic, before and after side by side, sorted by the change, with a mark of uncertainty. Everything in the redesign at the end of this lesson follows from it.

</Narrator>

<Section id="perception" kind="human" title="Perception: position beats area">

In 1984 William Cleveland and Robert McGill asked people to judge proportions encoded in different ways, and measured their errors. Judgements of **position along a common scale** were the most accurate; then position on separate, non-aligned scales; then **length**; then angle and slope; then **area**; with volume, colour saturation and colour hue the least accurate for reading quantities. Later experiments, including large online replications, have broadly confirmed the ordering. It explains most of the standard advice: bar charts and dot plots use position and length, pies use angle and area, and bubble charts and colour scales use the weakest channels of all.

Area is a particular trap, because there are two ways to scale a circle or icon and only one is honest. If a value doubles and you double the *radius*, the area quadruples, and readers (who respond to area) see a fourfold increase. Edward Tufte's **lie factor** measures distortions like this: the relative change shown in the graphic divided by the relative change in the data. An honest graphic has a lie factor of 1.

```python run
def lie_factor(data_a, data_b, shown_a, shown_b):
    """Tufte's lie factor: the relative change a graphic shows, over the relative change in the data."""
    return ((shown_b - shown_a) / shown_a) / ((data_b - data_a) / data_a)

# Bars for 50 and 55 drawn on an axis that starts at 48 instead of 0.
print("truncated bars:   ", round(lie_factor(50, 55, 50 - 48, 55 - 48), 1))
print("bars from zero:   ", round(lie_factor(50, 55, 50, 55), 1))

# Circles whose radius (not area) is proportional to the value: 10 and 20.
import math
area = lambda r: math.pi * r * r
print("radius-scaled:    ", round(lie_factor(10, 20, area(10), area(20)), 1))
print("area-scaled:      ", round(lie_factor(10, 20, area(math.sqrt(10)), area(math.sqrt(20))), 1))

# A pie and a dot plot of the same four shares: which one lets you rank them?
shares = {"Bus": 21, "Rail": 27, "Bicycle": 24, "Walk": 28}
for name, s in shares.items():
    print(f"{name:8} {s:3d}%  slice {3.6 * s:5.1f} degrees   dot at {'.' * (s - 15)}o")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

A truncated axis is far worse than it looks. Bars for 50 and 55 drawn from a baseline of 48 have lengths 2 and 7, so a 10% difference in the data is shown as a 250% difference on the page: a lie factor of 25. Scaling circles by radius gives a lie factor of 3, and scaling them by area brings it back to exactly 1.

</Narrator>

<Section id="colour" kind="human" title="Colour and accessibility">

Colour does three different jobs, and each needs a different kind of palette.

- **Categorical** (qualitative) palettes distinguish groups that have no order: towns, products, transport modes. Use few colours (five or six at most is a good limit); beyond that, label directly or split into small multiples.
- **Sequential** palettes show ordered quantities from low to high, usually light to dark. Perceptually uniform colour maps such as viridis change in lightness evenly, so equal steps in the data look like equal steps on the page, and they survive printing in greyscale. The old "rainbow" colour map does neither: it creates bright bands that look like boundaries in the data.
- **Diverging** palettes show quantities above and below a meaningful midpoint (zero change, a target, the national average), with two hues meeting at a neutral colour.

About one man in twelve and one woman in two hundred of Northern European ancestry have some form of red–green colour-vision deficiency, with lower rates in many other populations; across a large audience, someone always does. So: never let colour be the only way to tell things apart (add labels, shapes or position); avoid red against green as the main contrast; and check **contrast** against the background. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines define a contrast ratio from 1:1 to 21:1 and ask for at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for graphical elements that carry meaning. All of this can be computed.

```python run
import numpy as np

def to_linear(hex_colour):
    """sRGB hex such as '#1f77b4' to linear-light RGB in [0, 1] (the sRGB transfer function)."""
    c = np.array([int(hex_colour[i:i + 2], 16) for i in (1, 3, 5)]) / 255
    return np.where(c <= 0.04045, c / 12.92, ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) ** 2.4)

def luminance(rgb):
    return float(rgb @ [0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722])

def contrast(a, b):
    """WCAG 2 contrast ratio between two colours, from 1:1 to 21:1."""
    hi, lo = sorted([luminance(to_linear(a)), luminance(to_linear(b))], reverse=True)
    return (hi + 0.05) / (lo + 0.05)

def lab(rgb):
    """Linear RGB to CIELAB (D65 white), to measure how different two colours look."""
    xyz = np.array([[0.4124, 0.3576, 0.1805], [0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722],
                    [0.0193, 0.1192, 0.9505]]) @ rgb / [0.9505, 1.0, 1.089]
    f = np.where(xyz > (6 / 29) ** 3, np.cbrt(xyz), xyz / (3 * (6 / 29) ** 2) + 4 / 29)
    return np.array([116 * f[1] - 16, 500 * (f[0] - f[1]), 200 * (f[1] - f[2])])

# Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009): simulated deuteranopia, in linear RGB.
DEUTAN = np.array([[0.367322, 0.860646, -0.227968],
                   [0.280085, 0.672501, 0.047413],
                   [-0.011820, 0.042940, 0.968881]])

def difference(a, b, deutan=False):
    ra, rb = to_linear(a), to_linear(b)
    if deutan:
        ra, rb = np.clip(DEUTAN @ ra, 0, 1), np.clip(DEUTAN @ rb, 0, 1)
    return float(np.linalg.norm(lab(ra) - lab(rb)))           # CIE76 colour difference

print("contrast with a white background (graphics need at least 3:1):")
for name, c in [("light grey", "#cccccc"), ("yellow", "#f2c500"), ("mid blue", "#1f77b4"),
                ("dark grey", "#595959"), ("orange", "#e66100")]:
    print(f"  {name:10} {c}  {contrast(c, '#ffffff'):4.1f}:1")

print("\ncolour difference (CIE76; under about 10 is hard to tell apart in a small mark):")
for a, b, label in [("#d62728", "#2ca02c", "red vs green"), ("#e66100", "#5d3a9b", "orange vs purple"),
                    ("#1f77b4", "#ff7f0e", "blue vs orange")]:
    print(f"  {label:17} normal vision {difference(a, b):5.1f}   simulated deuteranopia {difference(a, b, True):5.1f}")
```

Light grey and a bright yellow, both popular for "secondary" series, fail the 3:1 rule on white; the mid blue, the dark grey and the orange pass. The colour differences tell the bigger story: red and green that differ by about 120 units for typical vision differ by about 7 under simulated deuteranopia (the most common form of red–green deficiency), so a chart that relies on them becomes a puzzle for millions of readers. Orange against purple, or blue against orange, stay far apart. Tools such as [ColorBrewer](https://colorbrewer2.org/) and the colour-blind simulators built into many design programs make these checks routine.

Accessibility goes beyond colour. Give every chart a text alternative that states its message (as the figures in this lesson do); make text large enough to read on a phone; and put labels next to the data instead of in a legend that the eye must keep returning to.

<Section id="honesty" kind="human" title="Honest axes and uncertainty">

A handful of rules prevent most misleading charts.

- **Bars and areas start at zero.** Their length or area *is* the value, so a truncated axis multiplies differences (the lie factor of 25 above). Dot plots and line charts encode values by position and may zoom in, as long as the axis is clearly labelled.
- **Be wary of dual y-axes.** Two series on two independent scales can be made to cross, diverge or move together at will by choosing the scales. Use two panels, or index both series to 100 at a common starting point.
- **Choose scales for the question.** A log scale shows rates of growth (equal steps for equal percentage changes) and is the right choice for things like epidemic curves or prices over decades; say clearly that it is a log scale. Per-capita rates beat raw counts when populations differ.
- **Show the denominator and the time window.** "Complaints doubled" means little without "from 4 to 8 out of 12,000 orders" and the period chosen; a carefully chosen start date can manufacture almost any trend.
- **Show uncertainty.** A point estimate without an interval invites readers to over-interpret small differences. Show intervals, show the raw data when there are few points, and say in words what the interval means.

Here are the clinic data. The block computes each clinic's mean wait before and after, and a 95% **bootstrap** interval for the change (resampling each month's patients 2,000 times; see [resampling and simulation](/en/statistics/resampling-and-simulation/)), and prints a text version of the chart the board will see.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Synthetic waiting times (minutes) at six clinics, in February (before online booking) and April.
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
clinics = ["Amani", "Bela Vista", "Sakura", "Al-Nur", "Lakeside", "Hill Road"]
before_mean = [38, 45, 52, 41, 35, 48]
after_mean = [27, 33, 50, 30, 31, 34]
rows = []
for c, b, a in zip(clinics, before_mean, after_mean):
    for month, mu in [("Feb", b), ("Apr", a)]:
        n = int(rng.integers(60, 250))
        rows += [(c, month, w) for w in rng.gamma(3.0, mu / 3.0, n).round(0)]
waits = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["clinic", "month", "minutes"])

def boot_ci(x, y, reps=2000):
    """95% bootstrap interval for mean(y) - mean(x): resample each month's patients."""
    diffs = [rng.choice(y, len(y)).mean() - rng.choice(x, len(x)).mean() for _ in range(reps)]
    return np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])

out = []
for c, g in waits.groupby("clinic"):
    feb, apr = g.loc[g.month == "Feb", "minutes"].to_numpy(), g.loc[g.month == "Apr", "minutes"].to_numpy()
    lo, hi = boot_ci(feb, apr)
    out.append((c, len(feb), len(apr), feb.mean(), apr.mean(), apr.mean() - feb.mean(), lo, hi))
res = pd.DataFrame(out, columns=["clinic", "n_feb", "n_apr", "feb", "apr", "change", "lo", "hi"])
res = res.sort_values("change").reset_index(drop=True)
print(res.round(1).to_string(index=False))

# A text dumbbell chart: o = February, * = April, on a scale from 20 to 60 minutes.
print("\n" + " " * 12 + "20        30        40        50        60")
for r in res.itertuples():
    line = [" "] * 41
    a, b = sorted([round(r.feb) - 20, round(r.apr) - 20])
    for i in range(a, b + 1):
        line[i] = "-"
    line[round(r.feb) - 20], line[round(r.apr) - 20] = "o", "*"
    print(f"{r.clinic:>11} " + "".join(line) + f"  {r.change:+.0f} min ({r.lo:+.0f} to {r.hi:+.0f})")
```

Four clinics show falls whose intervals lie wholly below zero, from 8 to 19 minutes. At Lakeside and Sakura the intervals include zero: the data are consistent with no change, so the honest word is *unclear*. Notice also that Lakeside's interval is wide (from −10 to +2 minutes) because only 64 patients were recorded there in February; sample sizes belong somewhere a curious reader can find them.

<Narrator who="gideon">

The chart says waits fell *after* online booking began, not *because* of it. February and April differ in more than booking: the season, staffing, a flu wave. Without clinics that did not get online booking, or a staggered roll-out to compare against, a before-and-after chart cannot separate the effect of booking from everything else that changed, and the headline should not claim it can.

Read it as a board member would, and list what goes wrong.

1. **The axis starts at 25.** Bela Vista's April bar looks about a sixth the height of its February bar, a fall of more than 80%; the real fall is from 47.9 to 28.6 minutes, about 40%.
2. **Alphabetical order** means the eye has to hunt for the biggest change.
3. **Red against green** carries the only distinction between the months, so for readers with red–green deficiency the pairs are hard to tell apart; and the legend sits in a corner, far from the bars.
4. **The title says what the chart is, not what it shows.**
5. **No uncertainty.** Sakura's and Lakeside's small falls look as real as the others.
6. **Grouped bars** make the reader compare bar heights within each pair and then compare those differences across pairs, a two-step comparison of lengths that a dumbbell chart turns into one look along a row.

What changed, and why each change matters:

- **Position on a common scale** replaces bar length, so the axis can show 20 to 60 minutes honestly, and every clinic's before and after sit on one line that the eye follows.
- **Sorting by the change** puts the answer to "where did waits fall most?" at the top.
- **Encoding by shape and lightness as well as hue** (open versus filled circles) works in greyscale and for every kind of colour vision; colour is reserved for one meaning, *clear* versus *unclear*.
- **Direct labels** replace the legend; the **numbers with intervals** sit beside each row for the reader who wants them.
- **The title is the finding**, worded no more strongly than the evidence ("after", not "because of").
- **Everything else is removed**: the gridlines are faint, there is no box, no shading, no decoration.

<Section id="story" kind="human" title="Dashboards, reports and the story">

**Dashboards and reports do different jobs.** A dashboard monitors known questions that recur: today's orders, this week's waiting times, whether a pipeline ran. It should be fast to read, stable from day to day, and honest about freshness ("data as of 06:00"). A report, memo or slide supports a particular decision: it has a question, an answer, the evidence and the caveats, in that order. Many organisations build dashboards when they need a decision, and end up with forty charts nobody reads. Tools for both are plentiful in 2026: open-source dashboard servers such as [Apache Superset](https://superset.apache.org/), [Grafana](https://grafana.com/) and [Metabase](https://www.metabase.com/), commercial ones such as Power BI, Tableau and Looker Studio, notebook-based apps, and charting libraries such as matplotlib, seaborn, [Vega-Altair](https://altair-viz.github.io/), Plotly, ggplot2 in R and Observable Plot and D3 on the web. The principles in this lesson apply to all of them.

**Writing the story with the numbers.** The words around a chart do as much work as the chart. A few habits that experienced analysts share:

- **Lead with the answer.** "Waits fell at four of six clinics, by 8 to 19 minutes; at the other two the change is too small to distinguish from noise." Then the evidence, then the caveats, then what you recommend.
- **Give numbers a scale.** Absolute and relative together ("from 48 to 29 minutes, a 40% fall"), denominators ("64 patients recorded in February"), and comparisons people can feel ("about the length of a short bus ride").
- **Round for the reader.** "19 minutes", not "19.27 minutes". Precision beyond what the uncertainty supports is noise that looks like knowledge.
- **Put uncertainty into words.** "We are fairly confident the fall at Bela Vista was between 12 and 27 minutes" is understood by more people than "95% CI (−26.5, −12.3)"; give both if your audience includes analysts.
- **Separate what the data show from what you recommend.** "Waits fell after online booking began" is a finding; "roll out online booking to all clinics" is a recommendation that also depends on cost, staff and patients without internet access, and it is better framed as "roll out in stages, so we can measure the effect" (see <LessonLink slug="experiments-in-products">experiments in products</LessonLink>).
- **Annotate.** A short note on the chart ("online booking started 3 March") saves a paragraph of text and a misreading.

<Analogy>

A good chart with a good headline works like a well-made road sign: it states one thing, in the place you need it, in a form you can read at speed. A dashboard of forty charts is a wall of signs; drivers ignore it.

</Analogy>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Every axis must start at zero."** Bars and areas must, because their length or area represents the value. Line charts and dot plots encode values by position, and zooming in to show variation is fine as long as the axis is labelled. What is never fine is a bar chart with a truncated axis.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Pie charts are always wrong."** For two or three parts of a whole, where the message is "about a quarter" or "most", a pie is readable. With many slices, or when the reader must compare similar slices (as in this lesson's figure), it fails; a sorted bar chart does better.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"More colours and more charts show more information."** Beyond five or six categories, colours stop being distinguishable, and a page of charts competes for attention instead of answering a question. Show less, label directly, and use small multiples.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The chart speaks for itself."** Readers bring different questions and see different things in the same chart. A title that states the finding, a subtitle that explains the encoding and a sentence of caveat make the chart say what the data support, and no more.

</Misconception>
