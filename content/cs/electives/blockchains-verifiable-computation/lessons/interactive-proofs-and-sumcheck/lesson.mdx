<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Checking without redoing">

A rollup operator claims that it executed ten thousand transactions correctly and that the new state root is a particular 32-byte value. A phone app receives a medical model's diagnosis computed on a cloud server. A university's grading service says it ran a student's code on 400 hidden tests. In each case one party, the **prover**, did a large computation, and another party, the **verifier**, wants to be sure the answer is right *without doing the computation again*. Re-execution is exactly what the verifier cannot afford: the phone is too weak, and on Ethereum every step of re-execution costs gas paid by everyone. Optimistic rollups sidestep the problem with fraud proofs and a challenge period (<LessonLink slug="rollups-and-scaling">Scaling: rollups and data availability</LessonLink>); this level is about the other answer.

That answer is a **proof** that is much faster to check than the computation it vouches for. This lesson builds the first mathematical tools for it. They come from complexity theory of the 1980s and early 1990s, and they share one surprising idea: a verifier who is allowed to **ask random questions** can be convinced of things it could never check deterministically, while being fooled only with a probability so small it can be ignored. By the end you will have run the **sumcheck protocol**, the engine inside many of the fastest proof systems of 2026, and caught a lying prover with it.

</Section>

<Section id="proof-systems" kind="definitions" title="What a proof system is">

Fix a claim, written formally as "$x \in L$" for some language $L$: "this graph has 3 triangles", "this program outputs 42 on that input", "this state root follows from that block". A proof system is a pair of algorithms, a prover $P$ and a verifier $V$, that exchange messages about $x$; at the end $V$ accepts or rejects.

<Definition term="interactive proof">

An **interactive proof** for $L$ is a protocol between a prover $P$ and a probabilistic verifier $V$ that runs in polynomial time in $|x|$, such that:

- **Completeness:** if $x \in L$, the honest prover makes $V$ accept with probability at least $1 - \delta$ (often exactly 1).
- **Soundness:** if $x \notin L$, then for **every** prover strategy $P^*$, however clever and however much computing power it has, $V$ accepts with probability at most $\varepsilon$, the **soundness error**.

The costs that matter are the verifier's time, the prover's time, the number of rounds and the total communication.

</Definition>

Soundness quantifies over all provers, including ones with unlimited computing power. Later in this level we relax that to provers that run in polynomial time and cannot break a cryptographic assumption; such systems are called **arguments**, and the "A" in SNARK stands for one (<LessonLink slug="snarks-and-polynomial-commitments">Arithmetisation and SNARKs</LessonLink>). Everything in this lesson is unconditional: no cryptography at all, only probability and algebra.

**NP certificates versus interactive proofs.** You already know one kind of proof system from [hard problems](/en/cs/hard-problems/): an NP **certificate**. To prove that a graph has a 3-colouring, hand over the colouring; the verifier checks every edge, deterministically, and is never fooled. That works well when a short certificate exists and checking it is cheap. It is useless for claims like "this graph has *exactly* 3 triangles", or "this program ran for a billion steps and printed 42": the natural certificate is the whole computation, and checking it means redoing it.

Interaction alone does not help. If $V$ is deterministic, the prover can predict every question $V$ will ask, so it could have written the whole conversation down in advance and sent it as a single certificate: deterministic interactive proofs prove exactly the NP statements. **Randomness** is what changes the picture. A question the prover cannot predict forces the prover to be ready for *all* questions at once, and that is a far stronger constraint than being ready for one.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice the order of quantifiers in soundness: the verifier's coins are chosen *after* the prover has committed to its strategy. If a prover could see or influence the "random" challenge before answering, every guarantee in this lesson would collapse. That one condition is exactly what breaks in the "weak Fiat–Shamir" bugs of the next lesson.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="freivalds" kind="computation" title="A first random check: Freivalds' algorithm">

Here is the simplest example of the idea, from [randomised algorithms](/en/cs/randomized-algorithms/): a prover claims that $C = AB$ for $n \times n$ matrices. Recomputing $AB$ costs about $n^3$ multiplications with the school method. Rūsiņš Freivalds observed in 1977 that the verifier can instead pick a random vector $r$ and check whether

$$
A(Br) = Cr,
$$

which takes three matrix–vector products, about $3n^2$ multiplications. If $C = AB$ the check always passes. If $C \neq AB$, then $D = AB - C$ has some nonzero row, and the check passes only if that row happens to be orthogonal to $r$.

<Theorem name="Freivalds' error bound">

Work over a field $\mathbb{F}$ and let $S \subseteq \mathbb{F}$ be finite. If $C \neq AB$ and the entries of $r$ are chosen independently and uniformly from $S$, then $\Pr[A(Br) = Cr] \le 1/|S|$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $D = AB - C \neq 0$, and pick an entry $d_{ij} \neq 0$. The check passes only if row $i$ of $Dr$ is zero: $d_{ij} r_j = -\sum_{k \neq j} d_{ik} r_k$. Fix all $r_k$ with $k \neq j$ first; then exactly one value of $r_j$ satisfies this equation, and $r_j$ hits it with probability at most $1/|S|$.

</Proof>

With 0/1 vectors the error is at most $1/2$ per trial; with $r$ drawn from a prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$ it is at most $1/p$. The code below works modulo $p = 101$, plants a single wrong entry among $40\,000$, and measures how often each kind of random vector lets it through.

```python run
import numpy as np

p = 101                                   # a toy prime field F_101: for learning only
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n = 200
A = rng.integers(0, p, (n, n))
B = rng.integers(0, p, (n, n))
C = (A @ B) % p                           # the prover's claimed product (correct)
C_bad = C.copy()
C_bad[17, 42] = (C_bad[17, 42] + 1) % p   # one wrong entry out of 40,000

def freivalds(A, B, C, r):
    """Accept iff A(Br) = Cr (mod p): three matrix-vector products, about 3n^2 multiplications."""
    return np.array_equal((A @ ((B @ r) % p)) % p, (C @ r) % p)

trials = 10000
for name, vectors in [("random 0/1 vector", lambda: rng.integers(0, 2, n)),
                      ("random vector in F_101", lambda: rng.integers(0, p, n))]:
    honest = all(freivalds(A, B, C, vectors()) for _ in range(200))
    fooled = sum(freivalds(A, B, C_bad, vectors()) for _ in range(trials))
    print(f"{name:24} honest C always accepted: {honest}   "
          f"wrong C accepted {fooled}/{trials} = {fooled / trials:.4f}")

print(f"\nmultiplications: recompute AB = {n**3:,}   Freivalds check = {3 * n * n:,}")
```

The 0/1 vectors let the bad product through about half the time, exactly as the bound predicts for a single wrong entry, and vectors over $\mathbb{F}_{101}$ about one time in a hundred. Twenty independent 0/1 trials would push the error below one in a million. Meanwhile the check costs $120\,000$ multiplications instead of $8\,000\,000$, and the gap grows with $n$.

Freivalds' check is not yet a proof *system*: the prover just sends $C$. But it shows the pattern we will reuse. The verifier reduces "these two big objects are equal" to "these two random fingerprints are equal", and algebra guarantees that unequal objects rarely have equal fingerprints.

</Section>

<Section id="schwartz-zippel" kind="definitions" title="Polynomial identity testing and the Schwartz–Zippel lemma">

The workhorse behind every random check in this level is a fact about polynomials. A nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ in one variable has at most $d$ roots, so a random point of a large field is very unlikely to be one of them. The same holds with many variables.

<Theorem name="Schwartz–Zippel lemma">

Let $g$ be a nonzero polynomial in $v$ variables of total degree at most $d$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$, and let $S \subseteq \mathbb{F}$ be finite. If $r_1, \ldots, r_v$ are chosen independently and uniformly from $S$, then

$$
\Pr\big[g(r_1, \ldots, r_v) = 0\big] \le \frac{d}{|S|}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Induction on $v$. For $v = 1$, $g$ has at most $d$ roots. For $v > 1$, write $g$ as a polynomial in $x_1$ whose coefficients are polynomials in the other variables: $g = \sum_{i=0}^{k} x_1^{\,i}\, g_i(x_2, \ldots, x_v)$ with $g_k \neq 0$. Then $g_k$ has total degree at most $d - k$, so by induction $\Pr[g_k(r_2, \ldots, r_v) = 0] \le (d-k)/|S|$. If $g_k(r_2, \ldots, r_v) \neq 0$, then $g(x_1, r_2, \ldots, r_v)$ is a nonzero polynomial of degree $k$ in $x_1$, which vanishes at $r_1$ with probability at most $k/|S|$. Adding the two bad events gives at most $d/|S|$.

</Proof>

The lemma (published independently by Jacob Schwartz, Richard Zippel, and Richard DeMillo with Richard Lipton around 1978–1980) gives **polynomial identity testing**: to decide whether two expressions $f$ and $g$ are the same polynomial, evaluate $f - g$ at a random point. If they differ, a random point exposes the difference with probability at least $1 - d/|S|$. No known *deterministic* polynomial-time algorithm does this for polynomials given as arbitrary arithmetic formulas; finding one is a famous open problem.

Here is an identity that Brahmagupta stated in the 7th century, $(a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) = (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2$, tested at random points, together with a version containing one sign error. Over a small field we can also count exactly how many points fail to expose the typo.

```python run
import random
from itertools import product

# Brahmagupta's identity (7th century): (a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) = (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2.
def lhs(a, b, c, d, p):
    return (a * a + b * b) * (c * c + d * d) % p

def rhs(a, b, c, d, p):
    return ((a * c - b * d) ** 2 + (a * d + b * c) ** 2) % p

def rhs_typo(a, b, c, d, p):                # one sign wrong: + instead of -
    return ((a * c + b * d) ** 2 + (a * d + b * c) ** 2) % p

def identity_test(f, g, p, trials, rng):
    """Schwartz-Zippel: evaluate both sides at random points of F_p^4."""
    for _ in range(trials):
        x = [rng.randrange(p) for _ in range(4)]
        if f(*x, p) != g(*x, p):
            return "different"
    return "probably equal"

rng = random.Random(7)
P = 2**61 - 1                                # a large prime: one trial errs with prob <= 4/P
print("true identity :", identity_test(lhs, rhs, P, 5, rng))
print("typo version  :", identity_test(lhs, rhs_typo, P, 5, rng))

# How often does a single random point fail to expose the typo? Count exactly over a small field.
for p in [13, 31]:
    agree = sum(lhs(*x, p) == rhs_typo(*x, p) for x in product(range(p), repeat=4))
    print(f"F_{p}: the two sides agree at {agree} of {p**4} points = {agree / p**4:.4f}"
          f"   (Schwartz-Zippel bound d/p = 4/{p} = {4 / p:.4f})")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

The typo changes the right-hand side by $(ac + bd)^2 - (ac - bd)^2 = 4abcd$, which vanishes exactly when one of $a, b, c, d$ is zero. Over $\mathbb{F}_{13}$ that happens at $1 - (12/13)^4 \approx 0.274$ of all points, against the lemma's bound of $4/13 \approx 0.308$: the bound is nearly tight for this polynomial. Over $\mathbb{F}_{2^{61}-1}$ the same bound is below $2^{-58}$ per trial.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="multilinear" kind="definitions" title="Multilinear extensions">

Proof systems need to turn data into polynomials, so that Schwartz–Zippel can be applied to the data. A table of $2^v$ values is naturally a function $f : \{0,1\}^v \to \mathbb{F}$, indexed by $v$-bit strings. The most economical polynomial that agrees with it is multilinear.

<Definition term="multilinear extension">

A polynomial is **multilinear** if it has degree at most 1 in each variable. For every $f : \{0,1\}^v \to \mathbb{F}$ there is exactly one multilinear polynomial $\tilde f : \mathbb{F}^v \to \mathbb{F}$ that agrees with $f$ on $\{0,1\}^v$, its **multilinear extension** (MLE):

$$
\tilde f(x) = \sum_{w \in \{0,1\}^v} f(w)\, \chi_w(x), \qquad \chi_w(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{v} \big(x_i w_i + (1 - x_i)(1 - w_i)\big).
$$

</Definition>

Each $\chi_w$ is 1 at $w$ and 0 at every other point of the cube, so the sum agrees with $f$ there. Uniqueness: the difference of two such polynomials is multilinear and vanishes on the whole cube, and a multilinear polynomial that vanishes on $\{0,1\}^v$ is zero (induct on $v$, as in the proof above).

Evaluating $\tilde f$ at a point does not require the $2^v$-term sum to be computed naively. Because $\tilde f$ is linear in $x_1$,

$$
\tilde f(r_1, x_2, \ldots, x_v) = (1 - r_1)\, \tilde f(0, x_2, \ldots, x_v) + r_1\, \tilde f(1, x_2, \ldots, x_v),
$$

so fixing the first variable **folds** a table of $2^v$ values into a table of $2^{v-1}$ values, one multiplication per entry. Folding $v$ times leaves the single value $\tilde f(r)$, for about $2^v$ multiplications in all: linear in the size of the table.

<Section id="sumcheck" kind="human" title="The sumcheck protocol">

Now the main event. Let $g(x_1, \ldots, x_v)$ be a polynomial over a prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$ with degree at most $d$ in each variable. The prover claims

$$
H = \sum_{b_1 \in \{0,1\}} \sum_{b_2 \in \{0,1\}} \cdots \sum_{b_v \in \{0,1\}} g(b_1, \ldots, b_v).
$$

Computing $H$ directly needs $2^v$ evaluations of $g$. The **sumcheck protocol** of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff and Nisan (1990) lets the verifier check the claim with $v$ short messages, $O(vd)$ field operations, and a **single** evaluation of $g$ at a random point. The idea is to peel off one variable per round, each time replacing a claim about a sum with a claim about a smaller sum at a random point.

<Definition term="sumcheck protocol">

Set $c_0 = H$. In round $j = 1, \ldots, v$:

1. The prover sends a univariate polynomial $s_j(X)$ of degree at most $d$ (as its $d + 1$ values at $0, 1, \ldots, d$), claimed to equal
   $$
   g_j(X) = \sum_{b \in \{0,1\}^{v-j}} g(r_1, \ldots, r_{j-1}, X, b).
   $$
2. The verifier checks that $s_j(0) + s_j(1) = c_{j-1}$, rejecting otherwise.
3. The verifier picks $r_j \in \mathbb{F}_p$ uniformly at random, sends it, and sets $c_j = s_j(r_j)$.

Finally the verifier evaluates $g(r_1, \ldots, r_v)$ itself and accepts if and only if it equals $c_v$.

</Definition>

If the prover is honest, every check passes: $g_j(0) + g_j(1)$ is the sum over one more Boolean variable, which is precisely $g_{j-1}(r_{j-1})$. Why can't a liar pass?

<Theorem name="Soundness of sumcheck">

If $H$ is not the true sum, then whatever the prover does, the verifier accepts with probability at most $vd/p$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since the true $g_1$ satisfies $g_1(0) + g_1(1) = $ (the true sum) $\neq H$, a prover that passes the first check must send $s_1 \neq g_1$. In general, call round $j$ *lucky* if $s_j \neq g_j$ but $s_j(r_j) = g_j(r_j)$. Two different polynomials of degree at most $d$ agree on at most $d$ points, and $r_j$ is chosen after $s_j$ is sent, so a round is lucky with probability at most $d/p$. If no round is lucky, the false claim propagates: $s_j \neq g_j$ and $c_j = s_j(r_j) \neq g_j(r_j)$, which forces $s_{j+1} \neq g_{j+1}$ for the next check to pass, and in the last round $c_v \neq g_v(r_v) = g(r_1, \ldots, r_v)$, so the final check fails. By the union bound over $v$ rounds, the probability of acceptance is at most $vd/p$.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

The protocol never asks the prover to justify $H$ directly. It asks for something with *structure* — a low-degree polynomial — and structure is what makes lies contagious: a false polynomial differs from the true one almost everywhere, so a single random spot-check carries the lie forward, round after round, to a place where the verifier can finally look.

</Narrator>

**A worked example: counting triangles.** Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a graph on $n = 2^m$ vertices, viewed as a function $A(x, y)$ of two $m$-bit vertex labels. The number of triangles is one sixth of

$$
\sum_{x, y, z \in \{0,1\}^m} \tilde A(x, y)\, \tilde A(y, z)\, \tilde A(x, z),
$$

a sum of $g(x, y, z) = \tilde A(x, y)\tilde A(y, z)\tilde A(x, z)$ over $v = 3m$ Boolean variables, with degree at most 3 in each. Each factor is multilinear in all $3m$ variables (it simply ignores the ones it does not mention), so the prover can compute every round by folding three tables. At the end the verifier needs $g(r)$, which is three evaluations of $\tilde A$, each costing $O(n^2)$ by folding the adjacency matrix. So the verifier spends $O(n^2)$ time, which is just reading the input, and never counts a triangle; the honest prover spends $O(n^3)$, about what counting triangles naively costs anyway.

The program below does this over $\mathbb{F}_p$ with $p = 2^{31} - 1$ for a graph on 8 vertices, prints the first rounds of an honest run (field elements near $p$ are small negative numbers: $2147483641 \equiv -6$), and then lets a lazy liar claim one triangle too many.

```python run
import random

p = 2**31 - 1        # the Mersenne prime 2^31 - 1: a toy field, far too small for real security

def fold(table, r):
    """Fix the first variable of a multilinear table to r: (1 - r)*T[0, rest] + r*T[1, rest]."""
    h = len(table) // 2
    return [(table[i] + r * (table[h + i] - table[i])) % p for i in range(h)]

def mle(table, point):
    """Evaluate the multilinear extension of a table of 2^v values at a point of F_p^v."""
    for r in point:
        table = fold(table, r)
    return table[0]

def interpolate(evals, r):
    """Value at r of the polynomial of degree < len(evals) with values evals at 0, 1, 2, ..."""
    total = 0
    for i, y in enumerate(evals):
        num = den = 1
        for j in range(len(evals)):
            if j != i:
                num, den = num * (r - j) % p, den * (i - j) % p
        total += y * num * pow(den, -1, p)
    return total % p

# The input: an undirected graph on 8 vertices (3 bits per vertex), as an adjacency matrix.
n, bits = 8, 3
edges = [(0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 2), (1, 3), (2, 3), (3, 4), (4, 5), (5, 6), (4, 6), (6, 7), (2, 5)]
adj = [[0] * n for _ in range(n)]
for a, b in edges:
    adj[a][b] = adj[b][a] = 1
A = [adj[x][y] for x in range(n) for y in range(n)]          # A(x, y) on {0,1}^6

# g(x, y, z) = A(x,y) A(y,z) A(x,z) on {0,1}^9; its sum is 6 times the number of triangles.
cube = [(x, y, z) for x in range(n) for y in range(n) for z in range(n)]
factors = [[adj[x][y] for x, y, z in cube], [adj[y][z] for x, y, z in cube],
           [adj[x][z] for x, y, z in cube]]
k, v = len(factors), 3 * bits                                 # degree <= 3 in each of 9 variables

def run_sumcheck(claim, prover_round, rng, verbose=False):
    tables, rs = [list(f) for f in factors], []
    for j in range(v):
        s = prover_round(tables, claim)                       # s(0), s(1), ..., s(k)
        if (s[0] + s[1]) % p != claim:
            return f"rejected in round {j + 1}: s(0) + s(1) != claim"
        r = rng.randrange(p)                                  # the verifier's fresh random challenge
        claim = interpolate(s, r)
        if verbose and j < 3:
            print(f"  round {j + 1}: s(0..3) = {s}, r = {r}")
        rs.append(r)
        tables = [fold(t, r) for t in tables]
    rx, ry, rz = rs[:bits], rs[bits:2 * bits], rs[2 * bits:]
    final = mle(A, rx + ry) * mle(A, ry + rz) * mle(A, rx + rz) % p   # the verifier reads only A
    return "accepted" if final == claim else "rejected at the final check"

def honest_round(tables, claim):
    h = len(tables[0]) // 2
    s = []
    for t in range(k + 1):
        total = 0
        for i in range(h):
            prod = 1
            for T in tables:
                prod = prod * (T[i] + t * (T[h + i] - T[i])) % p   # the line through T[0,i], T[1,i]
            total += prod
        s.append(total % p)
    return s

def lazy_liar(tables, claim):
    """Compute the true s, then shift s(0) so that s(0) + s(1) matches the (false) claim."""
    s = honest_round(tables, claim)
    s[0] = (claim - s[1]) % p
    return s

H = sum(a * b * c for a, b, c in zip(*factors))
print("true sum H =", H, "-> triangles:", H // 6)
print("honest prover, claim H:")
print("  verdict:", run_sumcheck(H, honest_round, random.Random(1), verbose=True))
print("cheating prover, claim H + 6 (one triangle too many):")
print("  verdict:", run_sumcheck(H + 6, lazy_liar, random.Random(2)))
```

The liar passes all nine consistency checks, because it always adjusts $s_j(0)$ to fit the current claim. That does not help: its polynomials are wrong, so the false claim survives every round and is caught by the one evaluation at the end.

**How lucky can a cheater get?** The best strategy follows the proof: in each round, send a polynomial that differs from the true $g_j$ by a multiple of some fixed $q(X)$ of degree $d$ with $q(0) + q(1) = 1$, so the consistency check passes, and hope that $r_j$ is one of the $d$ roots of $q$. If it is, the new claim is *true*, and the cheater can play honestly from then on. The next program plays this strategy thousands of times over tiny fields.

```python run
import random

def cheating_success(p, v=6, k=3, trials=4000, seed=5):
    """The best cheating strategy against sumcheck for a product of k multilinear tables in v
    variables over F_p: claim a wrong sum, and in each round send s = (true polynomial) + D*q,
    where q has degree k, roots 2, 3, ..., k+1 and q(0) + q(1) = 1. If the verifier's random r
    is a root of q, the claim becomes true again and the cheater plays honestly from then on."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    roots = range(2, k + 2)
    def q_raw(t):
        out = 1
        for m in roots:
            out = out * (t - m) % p
        return out
    c0 = pow((q_raw(0) + q_raw(1)) % p, -1, p)
    q = [c0 * q_raw(t) % p for t in range(k + 1)]             # q(0), ..., q(k)

    def interpolate(evals, r):
        total = 0
        for i, y in enumerate(evals):
            num = den = 1
            for j in range(len(evals)):
                if j != i:
                    num, den = num * (r - j) % p, den * (i - j) % p
            total += y * num * pow(den, -1, p)
        return total % p

    wins = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        tables = [[rng.randrange(p) for _ in range(2**v)] for _ in range(k)]
        true_sum = sum(a * b * c for a, b, c in zip(*tables)) % p
        claim = (true_sum + 1) % p                             # a false claim
        point = []
        for _ in range(v):
            h = len(tables[0]) // 2
            g = []
            for t in range(k + 1):
                total = 0
                for i in range(h):
                    prod = 1
                    for T in tables:
                        prod = prod * (T[i] + t * (T[h + i] - T[i])) % p
                    total += prod
                g.append(total % p)
            D = (claim - g[0] - g[1]) % p                      # zero once the cheater is back on track
            s = [(g[t] + D * q[t]) % p for t in range(k + 1)]  # passes the check s(0) + s(1) = claim
            r = rng.randrange(p)
            claim = interpolate(s, r)
            point.append(r)
            tables = [[(T[i] + r * (T[h + i] - T[i])) % p for i in range(h)] for T in tables]
        final = tables[0][0] * tables[1][0] * tables[2][0] % p  # the verifier's own evaluation
        wins += final == claim
    return wins / trials

v, k = 6, 3
print("  p    cheater wins   exact 1-(1-k/p)^v   bound v*k/p")
for p in [11, 101, 1009]:
    print(f"{p:5} {cheating_success(p, v, k):12.4f} {1 - (1 - k / p) ** v:17.4f} {v * k / p:13.4f}")
```

<Narrator who="margaret">

Over $\mathbb{F}_{11}$ the cheater wins about 85% of the time and the bound $vd/p = 18/11$ says nothing at all; over $\mathbb{F}_{1009}$ it wins under 2% of the time, almost exactly the bound. Real systems want an error near $2^{-100}$ or below, so with $v = 30$ and $d = 3$ they need $p$ around $2^{107}$ at least. A 31-bit field like the one above gives only about $31 - \log_2 90 \approx 24.5$ bits, which is why practical provers that compute over small fields draw their challenges from an extension field (<LessonLink slug="starks-and-folding">STARKs and folding</LessonLink>).

</Narrator>

**Costs.** The prover sends $v(d+1)$ field elements; the verifier does $O(vd)$ field operations plus one evaluation of $g$. For a product of $k$ multilinear tables, the honest prover's work is about $k(k+1) \cdot 2^v$ multiplications in total, because the tables halve every round: **linear in the size of the sum**, with a small constant. That last property is rare and precious. Most proof systems make the prover pay a large multiple of the original computation; sumcheck-based provers pay a small one.

</Section>

<Section id="gkr" kind="human" title="From sums to whole computations: GKR, and IP = PSPACE">

Sumcheck verifies one sum. Real computations are circuits. The **GKR protocol** of Goldwasser, Kalai and Rothblum (2008) verifies a layered arithmetic circuit of depth $D$ and size $S$ by applying sumcheck once per layer, from the output down to the input.

Number the layers from the output (layer 0) to the input (layer $D$), and let $V_i(z)$ be the value of gate $z$ in layer $i$. Each gate is the sum or product of two gates in the layer below, so with $\widetilde{\mathrm{add}}_i(z, a, b)$ and $\widetilde{\mathrm{mult}}_i(z, a, b)$ the multilinear extensions of the circuit's wiring (1 if gate $z$ adds, respectively multiplies, gates $a$ and $b$),

$$
\tilde V_i(z) = \sum_{a, b} \Big( \widetilde{\mathrm{add}}_i(z, a, b)\big(\tilde V_{i+1}(a) + \tilde V_{i+1}(b)\big) + \widetilde{\mathrm{mult}}_i(z, a, b)\, \tilde V_{i+1}(a)\, \tilde V_{i+1}(b) \Big).
$$

A claim about $\tilde V_i$ at one random point is a sum, so sumcheck reduces it to a claim about $\tilde V_{i+1}$ at two random points, which a little extra algebra combines into one. After $D$ rounds of this, the claim is about $\tilde V_D$, the multilinear extension of the *input*, which the verifier evaluates itself in linear time. For circuits with regular wiring, the verifier's cost is about $O(n + D \log S)$ for input size $n$, and later work brought the prover to time linear in $S$. GKR is **doubly efficient**: the honest prover is not much slower than just evaluating the circuit, and the verifier is far faster than evaluating it. The price is that verifier time grows with the depth $D$, so GKR suits wide, shallow computations, and it needs no cryptography at all.

Sumcheck's original purpose was more dramatic. Around the turn of 1990, Lund, Fortnow, Karloff and Nisan used it to give interactive proofs for counting problems (#P), and therefore for every problem in the polynomial hierarchy; within weeks Adi Shamir extended the technique to prove **IP = PSPACE**: every problem solvable in polynomial *space* — including deciding the winner of generalised board games, and evaluating quantified Boolean formulas — has an interactive proof with a polynomial-time verifier. Interaction plus randomness is enormously more powerful than a static certificate, assuming (as everyone believes) that PSPACE is larger than NP. (See <TopicLink id="math.comp.complexity-theory">computational complexity</TopicLink> for the classes.) The provers in those proofs need exponential time; the point of GKR, in the title of its paper, was to bring interactive proofs to "muggles" whose provers run in polynomial time.

</Section>

<Section id="professional" kind="human" title="The professional view: sumcheck in 2026">

For two decades after 1992, sumcheck was taught as theory. Two things changed. First, practical implementations, beginning with the work of Cormode, Mitzenmacher and Thaler around 2012, showed that GKR-style provers could run on real data. Second, sumcheck turned out to combine perfectly with the cryptographic tools of the next lessons:

- **The final evaluation $g(r)$** is the one step the verifier cannot do cheaply when $g$ encodes a big witness that the verifier does not have. Systems replace it with a **polynomial commitment**: the prover commits to the multilinear polynomial in advance and later proves the single evaluation (<LessonLink slug="snarks-and-polynomial-commitments">Arithmetisation and SNARKs</LessonLink>).
- **Interaction** is removed with the **Fiat–Shamir transform**: because sumcheck is *public-coin* (the verifier's messages are just random numbers), the challenges can be computed by hashing the transcript so far, giving a non-interactive proof (<LessonLink slug="zero-knowledge">Zero knowledge</LessonLink>).

Sumcheck-based or "multilinear" systems include [Spartan](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/550) (2019), HyperPlonk (2022), the lookup argument [Lasso](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1216) and the zkVM [Jolt](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1217) built on it (2023), and [Binius](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1784) over binary fields (2023); several production zkVMs and provers adopted multilinear designs in 2024–2025, and verifiable-database systems such as Space and Time's [Proof of SQL](https://github.com/spaceandtimefdn/sxt-proof-of-sql) prove query results with sumcheck. The folding inner loop you ran above — read two halves of a table, combine, write half as much — is memory-bound and embarrassingly parallel, which is why these provers map well onto GPUs. Thaler's [book](https://people.cs.georgetown.edu/jthaler/ProofsArgsAndZK.html) is the standard reference; chapters 3 and 4 treat exactly this lesson's material with full proofs.

<Note>

Everything in this lesson used toy parameters: fields of 31 or 61 bits, small graphs, Python integers. Real implementations use carefully chosen fields and extension fields for the challenges, constant-time arithmetic, and audited code. Treat the programs here as learning tools, never as security software.

</Note>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A probabilistic proof is weaker than a real proof."** A soundness error of $2^{-100}$ is far smaller than the probability that the verifying computer suffers an undetected hardware fault during the check. Errors also shrink exponentially with repetition or with field size. What matters is that the probability is over the *verifier's* coins and holds for every prover, not over some assumed distribution of inputs.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Sumcheck verifies a sum without ever looking at $g$."** The verifier must evaluate $g$ once, at a random point it chooses. For triangle counting that costs $O(n^2)$; for a SNARK, the evaluation is replaced by a commitment opening. Forgetting the final check leaves a protocol that any liar passes, as the lazy liar above would have.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Freivalds' check proves the product correct."** It proves it correct *with high probability*, and the error is one-sided: a correct product always passes, and a wrong one passes with probability at most $1/|S|$ per trial. Repetition with fresh randomness multiplies the error probabilities only if the trials are independent; reusing the same vector does not help.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The bigger the field the slower, so use the smallest field that holds the data."** The data may fit in a small field, but soundness depends on where the *challenges* live: the bound is $vd/|\mathbb{F}|$. Modern systems compute on small fields for speed and draw challenges from extension fields for soundness.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="A short history">

Interactive proofs were defined in 1985, independently, by Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff (whose paper also introduced zero knowledge) and by László Babai (whose public-coin "Arthur–Merlin games" are the model sumcheck lives in). Freivalds' matrix check dates from 1977 and the Schwartz–Zippel lemma from 1978–1980. Sumcheck appeared at the end of 1989 and led within weeks to IP = PSPACE (both published in 1990 and, in journal form, 1992); probabilistically checkable proofs and the PCP theorem followed in 1992. Goldwasser, Kalai and Rothblum introduced the GKR protocol in 2008. Practical implementations came in the 2010s, and sumcheck moved to the centre of practical SNARK design in the 2020s. Goldwasser and Micali received the Turing Award in 2012, in part for this line of work.

</Section>
