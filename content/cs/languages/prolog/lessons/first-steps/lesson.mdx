<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Saying what is true">

In most languages you tell the computer what to do, step by step: set this variable, loop over that list, return this value. Prolog asks you to do something different. You describe a small world by stating what is **true** in it, and then you ask **questions**. The system works out the answers by itself.

Here is the flavour. These two lines state facts:

```prolog
parent(amara, chidi).
parent(chidi, emeka).
```

This line states a rule: someone is a grandparent of a grandchild if they are a parent of some person who is a parent of that grandchild.

```prolog
grandparent(G, C) :- parent(G, P), parent(P, C).
```

Now you can ask "who is Amara a grandparent of?" or "who are Emeka's grandparents?" or "is Chidi a grandparent of anyone?", all with the same rule. Nowhere did you write a loop, a search or a function for each question. One description serves every direction of questioning, and that is the first surprise of logic programming.

Why learn it today? Not because it will replace the languages you use every day; it will not. Learn it because it changes how you think about programs. Prolog teaches you pattern matching in its most general form (unification, which you will also find at the heart of type checkers), search with automatic backtracking, programs as data, and the idea that a specification can itself be runnable. It remains a practical tool for some jobs, such as parsing, rule systems, scheduling, puzzles and configuration problems, and its descendant Datalog quietly powers code-analysis and database tools you may already use. Lesson 8 tours where Prolog and its relatives are used.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="From Marseille, 1972">

Prolog was born from a meeting of two research programmes in the early 1970s.

In Marseille, **Alain Colmerauer** and his group were building computer systems that could converse in French: read sentences, store what they said, and answer questions about them. They needed a way to write grammars and reasoning rules together. In Edinburgh, **Robert Kowalski** was studying automated theorem proving. Colmerauer and Kowalski visited each other and found that a restricted form of logic, **Horn clauses** (statements of the form "this is true if these are all true"), could be read in two ways at once: as logical statements, and as procedures that a computer could run. To prove "grandparent" you call "parent" twice. Kowalski later summed up the idea in the slogan *algorithm = logic + control*.

In **1972**, Colmerauer and **Philippe Roussel** implemented the first Prolog system; the name, suggested by Roussel's wife, abbreviates *PROgrammation en LOGique*. The first applications were exactly the ones the group had in mind: programs that took statements and questions in French and answered them. The idea spread quickly to Edinburgh, where **David H. D. Warren** wrote a Prolog compiler for the DEC-10 computer in the late 1970s that made Prolog fast enough to be taken seriously, and whose syntax, the "Edinburgh" syntax, is the one everybody uses today. In 1983 Warren designed the **Warren Abstract Machine**, the virtual machine on which most Prolog compilers have been modelled since.

The 1980s were Prolog's busiest decade. Japan's **Fifth Generation Computer Systems** project (1982 to 1992) placed logic programming at the centre of a national effort to build intelligent computers, and companies around the world built expert systems in Prolog. The project's grandest goals were not met, and interest in logic programming cooled along with the wider AI boom of that era. But the language matured: it gained an ISO standard in 1995, constraint solving, tabling, modules and large libraries.

**SWI-Prolog**, the system this course uses, was started by **Jan Wielemaker** at the University of Amsterdam in 1987 and is today the most widely used free Prolog. Others include SICStus Prolog (commercial, known for its constraint solvers), GNU Prolog, XSB (a pioneer of tabling), Scryer Prolog and Trealla Prolog (newer systems that follow the ISO standard closely). Programs that stick to the standard core run on all of them; SWI-Prolog adds many libraries of its own.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Facts, rules, queries and terms">

<Definition term="Fact">

A statement that something is true, written as a name applied to arguments and ended with a full stop: `capital(peru, lima).` The name (`capital`) is the **predicate**; the number of arguments is its **arity**. Prolog writes the predicate as `capital/2`.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Rule">

A statement that something is true *if* other things are true: `Head :- Body.` Read `:-` as "if" and the comma in the body as "and". `grandparent(G, C) :- parent(G, P), parent(P, C).` Facts and rules together are called **clauses**; all the clauses for one predicate form its **definition**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Query">

A question, typed at the `?-` prompt of the interactive **toplevel** or written in a program: `?- capital(peru, X).` Prolog answers by finding values for the variables that make the query true, or says `false` if it cannot.

</Definition>

Everything in Prolog, data and programs alike, is a **term**, and there are only a few kinds:

| Kind | Examples | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atom | `lima`, `amara`, `'São Paulo'`, `[]` | Starts with a lower-case letter, or is quoted |
| Number | `42`, `-7`, `3.14` | As in other languages |
| Variable | `X`, `Who`, `City`, `_`, `_Count` | Starts with an upper-case letter or an underscore |
| Compound term | `capital(peru, lima)`, `date(2026, 11, 19)` | A name and arguments in brackets |
| String | `"Hello"` | Double quotes; in SWI-Prolog a string object |

<Note>

Every clause has two readings, and fluent Prolog programmers switch between them constantly. The **declarative** reading says what the clause means: "G is a grandparent of C if G is a parent of P and P is a parent of C." The **procedural** reading says what the machine does: "to prove `grandparent(G, C)`, first prove `parent(G, P)`, then, with the `P` found, prove `parent(P, C)`." The declarative reading tells you whether the program is *correct*; the procedural reading tells you whether it *terminates* and how *fast* it is. Good Prolog code reads well both ways.

</Note>

Two rules of thumb trip up newcomers. **Capitalisation matters**: `Lima` is a variable that can stand for anything, while `lima` is a constant. And a **variable is local to its clause**: the `X` in one rule has nothing to do with the `X` in another.

</Section>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Installing SWI-Prolog and a first program">

### Getting Prolog

- **Windows and macOS:** download the installer from [swi-prolog.org](https://www.swi-prolog.org/download/stable). On macOS, `brew install swi-prolog` also works.
- **Linux:** install your distribution's `swi-prolog` package (for example `sudo apt install swi-prolog`). The SWI-Prolog site links to more current builds for Ubuntu.
- **No install:** [SWISH](https://swish.swi-prolog.org/) runs SWI-Prolog in the browser.

Check the installation with `swipl --version`. This course was tested with **SWI-Prolog 10.1**.

### The toplevel

Prolog is often used interactively. Save these facts and rules as `family.pl`:

```prolog
mother(amara, chidi).
mother(amara, ngozi).
mother(ngozi, ada).
mother(ifeoma, emeka).

father(obi, chidi).
father(obi, ngozi).
father(tunde, ada).
father(chidi, emeka).

parent(P, C) :- mother(P, C).
parent(P, C) :- father(P, C).

grandparent(G, C) :-
    parent(G, P),
    parent(P, C).
```

Start Prolog with the file loaded, `swipl family.pl`, and you get the `?-` prompt. Each query ends with a full stop. When there may be more answers, Prolog shows the first and waits: press `;` (or the space bar) for the next, or Enter to stop. Here is a session (Prolog's answers are as SWI-Prolog 10.1 printed them):

```text
?- grandparent(amara, Who).
Who = emeka ;
Who = ada ;
false.

?- grandparent(Who, ada).
Who = amara ;
Who = obi ;
false.

?- parent(chidi, X).
X = emeka.

?- mother(M, emeka), father(F, emeka).
M = ifeoma,
F = chidi.

?- grandparent(tunde, X).
false.

?- mother(amara, ngozi).
true.
```

Read the answers carefully:

- The same `grandparent` rule answered "whose grandparent is Amara?" and "who are Ada's grandparents?". Which arguments you leave as variables decides which question you are asking.
- The final `false` after the last `;` means "no more answers". It is not an error.
- `parent(chidi, X)` gave one answer and no prompt for more, because Prolog could tell no other clause could match.
- A query with a comma is a conjunction: both parts must be true, with the same variable values.
- `grandparent(tunde, X)` is `false`: Tunde has no grandchildren in this little world. Prolog only knows what you told it. This is called the **closed-world assumption**, and lesson 5 returns to it.
- A query without variables is a yes-or-no question.

To reload a file after editing it, type `make.` at the prompt. To leave, type `halt.` or press Ctrl-D.

### Prolog as a script

Every complete program in this course runs as a script. The first line, `:- initialization(main, main).`, is a **directive**: a goal run while the file loads. It tells SWI-Prolog to call `main` once the whole file has been loaded and then exit: with status 0 if `main` succeeded, 1 if it failed, and 2 if it raised an error that nothing caught. In the last two cases SWI-Prolog prints an `ERROR:` line saying what went wrong. Run it with `swipl main.pl`.

```prolog check
:- initialization(main, main).

main :-
    format("Hello from Prolog!~n").
```

```text
Hello from Prolog!
```

`format/2` prints text. In its format string `~w` writes the next argument and `~n` writes a newline; the arguments go in a list as the second argument.

### Facts, queries and forall

This program holds a few facts about countries and asks questions from `main`:

```prolog check
:- initialization(main, main).

% capital(Country, City): City is the capital of Country.
capital(kenya, nairobi).
capital(peru, lima).
capital(japan, tokyo).
capital(portugal, lisbon).
capital(brazil, brasilia).

% speaks(Country, Language): Language is widely spoken in Country.
speaks(kenya, swahili).
speaks(kenya, english).
speaks(peru, spanish).
speaks(peru, quechua).
speaks(japan, japanese).
speaks(portugal, portuguese).
speaks(brazil, portuguese).

main :-
    capital(peru, City),
    format("The capital of Peru is ~w.~n", [City]),
    forall(speaks(Country, portuguese),
           format("Portuguese is spoken in ~w.~n", [Country])),
    forall(( speaks(C, Language), capital(C, Capital) ),
           format("~w: ~w, capital ~w~n", [C, Language, Capital])).
```

```text
The capital of Peru is lima.
Portuguese is spoken in portugal.
Portuguese is spoken in brazil.
kenya: swahili, capital nairobi
kenya: english, capital nairobi
peru: spanish, capital lima
peru: quechua, capital lima
japan: japanese, capital tokyo
portugal: portuguese, capital lisbon
brazil: portuguese, capital brasilia
```

`%` starts a comment. The comment above each predicate, saying what its arguments mean, is a habit worth adopting from day one: a predicate's meaning is not obvious from its name alone. `main` is a rule whose body is a sequence of goals. `capital(peru, City)` finds the one answer. `forall(Condition, Action)` runs `Action` for **every** way of making `Condition` true; it is how a Prolog script prints all answers. The last `forall` has a conjunction as its condition, in brackets, joining the two tables on the country, much like a join in a database. Answers appear in the order of the facts in the file.

### Rules in a program

```prolog check
:- initialization(main, main).

% mother(Mother, Child) and father(Father, Child)
mother(amara, chidi).
mother(amara, ngozi).
mother(ngozi, ada).
mother(ifeoma, emeka).

father(obi, chidi).
father(obi, ngozi).
father(tunde, ada).
father(chidi, emeka).

% A parent is a mother or a father: two clauses, either can apply.
parent(P, C) :- mother(P, C).
parent(P, C) :- father(P, C).

% G is a grandparent of C if G is a parent of some P who is a parent of C.
grandparent(G, C) :-
    parent(G, P),
    parent(P, C).

main :-
    forall(grandparent(G, C),
           format("~w is a grandparent of ~w~n", [G, C])).
```

```text
amara is a grandparent of emeka
amara is a grandparent of ada
obi is a grandparent of emeka
obi is a grandparent of ada
```

Two clauses for `parent` mean "or": a parent is a mother, or a father. That is the usual way to express alternatives in Prolog. Notice that the mothers and fathers are each kept together in the file. If you interleave the clauses of two predicates, SWI-Prolog warns that "clauses of mother/2 are not together in the source-file", because scattered clauses are usually a typing mistake; the directive `:- discontiguous mother/2.` silences the warning when the scattering is deliberate.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A Prolog variable is a box I can assign to."** A Prolog variable stands for some term that is not yet known. Once it is bound to a value, it keeps that value for the rest of the clause; there is no assignment and no `X = X + 1`. New values come from new variables, as later lessons show.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Prolog reasons like a person."** It follows a fixed, simple strategy: try clauses from top to bottom and goals from left to right, and back up when something fails. Lesson 3 explains it exactly. Knowing the strategy is what lets you write programs that terminate and run fast.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"false means my program is broken."** `false` means "with what I know, this cannot be shown true". It is an ordinary answer. Errors look different: they print `ERROR:` with a message.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Names in capitals are constants, as in C."** In Prolog it is the reverse: anything starting with a capital letter is a variable. Write `peru`, not `Peru`, unless you mean "any value".

</Misconception>
