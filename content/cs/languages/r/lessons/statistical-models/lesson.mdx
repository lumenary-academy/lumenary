<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="From describing data to drawing conclusions">

The previous lesson summarised data: averages, counts, tables. Statistics goes one step further and asks what the data says beyond itself. Orange juice seems to make guinea pigs' teeth grow more than ascorbic acid; is the difference larger than what chance variation among sixty animals would produce? Heavier cars use more fuel; by how much per extra thousand pounds, and how sure can we be? These are questions of **inference**, and R was built to answer them.

R's design makes statistical modelling unusually direct. A **formula** such as `mpg ~ wt + cyl` describes a model in a line, the same formula works with dozens of modelling functions, and every result is an object you can print, summarise, extract parts from and pass to other functions. This lesson covers the core tools that come with every R installation: t-tests, linear regression, analysis of variance and logistic regression, plus simulation as a way of checking what a method does. It explains the statistics briefly as it goes; the aim is to use these tools correctly and read their output honestly, not to replace a statistics course. Lumenary Math's <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistical-inference-intro">introduction to statistical inference</TopicLink> covers the ideas underneath.

</Section>

<Section id="formulas" kind="definitions" title="Model formulas">

<Definition term="Formula">

A **formula** is an R object written with a tilde, `response ~ predictors`, that describes a model without evaluating it. The left side is the variable to explain; the right side lists the variables that explain it, combined with special operators. Modelling functions take a formula and a `data` argument, and look the names up as columns of that data frame.

</Definition>

| Formula | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `y ~ x` | `y` depends on `x` (with an intercept) |
| `y ~ x1 + x2` | two predictors, effects added together |
| `y ~ x * g` | `x`, `g` and their interaction `x:g` (the effect of `x` differs by `g`) |
| `y ~ .` | all other columns of the data frame |
| `y ~ x - 1` | without an intercept |
| `log(y) ~ x + I(x^2)` | transformations; `I()` protects arithmetic that would otherwise mean something else in a formula |
| `y ~ g` with `g` a factor | one effect per level of `g`, compared with a baseline level |

Formulas are also used outside modelling, as you saw with `aggregate(len ~ supp, …)` and as plotting functions use them. They are a compact language for "this, explained by that".

</Section>

<Section id="t-tests" kind="examples" title="Comparing two groups">

A **t-test** asks whether the difference between two means is larger than chance variation would plausibly produce. It reports a **p-value**: if the two groups truly had the same mean, how often would samples like these show a difference at least this large? It also reports a **confidence interval** for the difference, which is usually the more informative number. The `ToothGrowth` data set records the tooth length of 60 guinea pigs given vitamin C either as orange juice (`OJ`) or as ascorbic acid (`VC`), at three doses.

```r check
# ToothGrowth: tooth length in 60 guinea pigs given vitamin C as orange juice (OJ)
# or as ascorbic acid (VC), at three doses.
str(ToothGrowth)
aggregate(len ~ supp, data = ToothGrowth, FUN = mean)

# Is the difference between the two supplements larger than chance would explain?
tt <- t.test(len ~ supp, data = ToothGrowth)
tt

# A test result is a list: take out exactly what you need.
round(tt$p.value, 4)
round(tt$conf.int, 2)

# Paired data: the sleep data set records extra hours of sleep for the same
# 10 people under two drugs, so compare within each person.
drug1 <- sleep$extra[sleep$group == 1]
drug2 <- sleep$extra[sleep$group == 2]
t.test(drug2, drug1, paired = TRUE)$p.value
```

By default R runs **Welch's** t-test, which does not assume that the two groups have equal variances (hence the fractional degrees of freedom, 55.3); pass `var.equal = TRUE` for the classical version. The orange juice group's mean was 3.7 longer, but the 95% confidence interval for the difference runs from slightly below zero (−0.17) to 7.57, and the p-value is 0.06. A careful summary is: "orange juice may produce somewhat longer teeth, but this sample cannot rule out no difference; the plausible range of the effect is from about zero to about 7.6." Notice that the result is a list (of class `htest`), from which `$p.value` and `$conf.int` extract numbers for further use; the confidence interval even carries an attribute recording its level.

The `sleep` data, published by William Sealy Gosset under the pen name "Student" in 1908, measures the same ten people under two drugs, so each person serves as their own control. A **paired** test analyses the ten within-person differences, which removes the large variation between people and makes the comparison much more precise (p ≈ 0.003). Treating paired data as two independent groups is a common and costly mistake.

</Section>

<Section id="lm" kind="computation" title="Linear regression">

A **linear model** describes a response as a straight-line function of predictors plus random error: for one predictor, $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon$. `lm()` estimates the coefficients $\beta_0$ (the intercept) and $\beta_1$ (the slope) by least squares, choosing the line that minimises the sum of squared vertical distances from the points. The `mtcars` data set describes 32 car models from a 1974 US magazine; `mpg` is fuel economy in miles per US gallon, `wt` the weight in thousands of pounds.

```r check
# mtcars: 32 car models from a 1974 US magazine. Does weight predict fuel economy?
# mpg is miles per US gallon; wt is weight in thousands of pounds.
fit <- lm(mpg ~ wt, data = mtcars)
summary(fit)

# The pieces, for use in code.
coef(fit)
round(confint(fit), 2)
round(summary(fit)$r.squared, 3)

# Predictions for two new weights, with 95% prediction intervals.
new_cars <- data.frame(wt = c(2.5, 4.0))
round(predict(fit, newdata = new_cars, interval = "prediction"), 1)
```

How to read the summary, section by section:

- **Residuals**: the spread of the differences between observed and fitted values. Roughly symmetric around zero is what you hope for.
- **Coefficients**: each estimate with its standard error, t statistic and p-value. The slope says that each extra thousand pounds goes with about 5.3 fewer miles per gallon; its 95% confidence interval, from `confint()`, is −6.49 to −4.20. The intercept, 37.3 mpg for a car weighing nothing, is a mathematical anchor for the line, not a meaningful prediction.
- **Residual standard error**: the typical size of a residual, about 3 mpg.
- **R-squared**: the share of the variation in `mpg` that the model accounts for, here 75%.

The last lines show the difference between two kinds of interval. A confidence interval describes uncertainty about the *average* fuel economy of all cars of a given weight. A **prediction interval** describes where a *single new car* is likely to fall, which also includes the car-to-car scatter, so it is much wider: a 2,500-pound car is predicted at 23.9 mpg, but anywhere from 17.6 to 30.3 would be unsurprising.

</Section>

<Section id="factors-anova" kind="examples" title="Factors in models, and comparing models">

Predictors can be categories. When a factor appears in a formula, R creates one coefficient for each level except the first, the **baseline**, and each coefficient is a difference from the baseline. The number of cylinders in `mtcars` is stored as a number, so it must be converted with `factor()`; otherwise R would treat it as a quantity and fit a single slope, as if 6 cylinders were exactly halfway between 4 and 8 in effect.

To ask whether adding predictors improves a model, compare **nested** models (where one contains all the terms of the other) with `anova()`. For comparing the means of several groups, the classical tool is **analysis of variance**, `aov()`, which is a linear model with a factor as its only predictor.

```r check
cars <- mtcars
cars$cyl <- factor(cars$cyl)               # 4, 6 or 8 cylinders: a category, not a number

# Two predictors: weight and number of cylinders.
fit2 <- lm(mpg ~ wt + cyl, data = cars)
round(coef(summary(fit2)), 3)

# Does adding cylinders improve on weight alone? Compare nested models.
fit1 <- lm(mpg ~ wt, data = cars)
anova(fit1, fit2)

# The baseline level is the first level of the factor; relevel() changes it.
cars$cyl <- relevel(cars$cyl, ref = "8")
round(coef(lm(mpg ~ wt + cyl, data = cars)), 3)

# One-way analysis of variance: plant weights under a control and two treatments.
summary(aov(weight ~ group, data = PlantGrowth))
```

With 4 cylinders as the baseline, `cyl6` and `cyl8` say that, *at the same weight*, six- and eight-cylinder cars average 4.3 and 6.1 fewer miles per gallon than four-cylinder cars. Notice that the weight coefficient shrank from −5.3 to −3.2 once cylinders were included: heavier cars tend to have more cylinders, so part of what looked like a weight effect was an engine-size effect. Every coefficient in a multiple regression is "holding the other predictors fixed", and its value depends on which other predictors are in the model. The `anova()` comparison (p ≈ 0.003) says cylinders add real explanatory power beyond weight. Releveling to 8 cylinders changed how the coefficients are expressed, not the model: the fitted values are identical.

The analysis of variance on `PlantGrowth` (dried plant weights under a control and two treatments) finds that the group means differ (p ≈ 0.016). It does not say *which* groups differ; `TukeyHSD()` compares every pair with a correction for making several comparisons.

<Section id="glm" kind="computation" title="Logistic regression and simulation">

When the response is yes or no, a straight line is the wrong model, since it can predict probabilities below 0 or above 1. **Logistic regression** models the log-odds of "yes" as a linear function of the predictors. It is one member of the family of **generalised linear models**, fitted with `glm()` and a `family` argument (`binomial` for yes/no, `poisson` for counts).

**Simulation** is the second idea in this program. Instead of trusting a formula, you can generate data where you *know* the truth and see what a method does with it. `set.seed()` fixes the random number generator so that the result is the same every time the script runs, which is essential for reproducible work.

```r check
# Logistic regression: the probability of a manual gearbox (am = 1) by weight.
logit <- glm(am ~ wt, data = mtcars, family = binomial)
round(coef(summary(logit)), 3)

# Coefficients are on the log-odds scale; exp() gives odds ratios.
round(exp(coef(logit)["wt"]), 4)

# Predicted probabilities for cars of 2,000, 3,000 and 4,000 pounds.
round(predict(logit, newdata = data.frame(wt = c(2, 3, 4)), type = "response"), 3)

# Simulation: if two groups really have the same mean, how often does a t-test
# at the 5% level still report a "significant" difference?
set.seed(2026)
p_values <- replicate(2000, t.test(rnorm(20), rnorm(20))$p.value)
mean(p_values < 0.05)
```

The weight coefficient, −4.02 on the log-odds scale, is hard to read directly. Exponentiated, it is an **odds ratio**: each extra thousand pounds multiplies the odds of a manual gearbox by about 0.018, a very strong effect in this small 1974 sample. `predict(…, type = "response")` gives probabilities: 98% for a light car, about even at 3,000 pounds, 2% for a heavy one.

The simulation drew 2,000 pairs of samples from the *same* normal distribution, so every "significant" result is a false alarm. About 5% of them were (5.25% here), exactly as the 5% significance level promises. That is the meaning of a p-value threshold, and also its danger: run twenty tests on data with no real effects, and you should expect about one "discovery". When an analysis makes many comparisons, adjust for it: `p.adjust(p, method = "holm")` or `method = "BH"` (which controls the false discovery rate) turn a vector of p-values into adjusted ones, and analysis plans written before looking at the data keep the number of comparisons honest.

</Section>

<Section id="honest" kind="misconceptions" title="Reading results honestly">

<Misconception>

"p < 0.05 means the effect is real, and p > 0.05 means there is no effect." A p-value measures how surprising the data would be if there were no effect; it is not the probability that the effect is real, and it says nothing about the effect's size. A huge sample can make a trivial difference "significant", and a small one can miss an important effect, as with the guinea pigs. Report estimates with confidence intervals and judge whether the size matters. The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values makes these points in more detail.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"The model fitted, so its assumptions hold." Linear models assume that the relationship is roughly linear, that errors are independent, and that their spread does not change systematically; tests and intervals rely on these. Always look at the residuals: `plot(fit)` draws four standard diagnostic plots, and curved patterns or a funnel shape tell you to transform a variable or choose a different model.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"A strong coefficient shows that the predictor causes the response." Observational data shows association. Heavier cars use more fuel partly because of their weight and partly because of everything that comes with weight. Causal claims need a randomised experiment, as in ToothGrowth, or careful causal reasoning about what else could explain the pattern.

</Misconception>

Beyond these base functions, R's package ecosystem covers nearly every modelling approach in use. Mixed-effects models for repeated measurements and grouped data are in **lme4** and **nlme** (nlme comes with R); generalised additive models for smooth non-linear effects are in **mgcv** (also included); survival analysis is in **survival** (included); Bayesian models are in **brms** and **rstanarm**, built on the Stan language; and the **broom** package turns model results into tidy data frames for reporting. The CRAN Task Views linked below list the packages for each field.
